# Conversations by class at each key date

Research to ground the proposal in [`docs/design/conversations-by-class.md`](../design/conversations-by-class.md):
who was actually present at each vertical-slice key date, what language each group used, what they
had reason to talk about, and which real period words can be quoted. Written 2026-09-28 from sources
fetched in this session (WebSearch, WebFetch, and full texts downloaded from the Internet Archive,
Welsh Newspapers Online, Heneb, Monastic Wales and the Roman Inscriptions of Britain site).

Labels used throughout: **cross-checked** (two independent sources agree), **single-source**,
**search-summary only** (seen only in a search engine's summary, page not read directly, so treat as a
lead), **inference** (my reasoning from sources, not a claim any source makes), **OCR** (read from an
optical-character-recognition text layer, so a letter or number may be wrong). Cross-references to
other notes use their citation ids (e.g. `medieval:S27`).

## Summary

- **June 1282 is the date that needed the most correction.** Carreg Cennen was **in Welsh hands** from
  26 March 1282, taken by Dafydd ap Gruffudd with local lords including Gruffudd and Llywelyn ap Rhys
  Fychan, "lords of Is Cennen" (Annales Cambriae, [S2]; Morris [S1]). The English army only took the
  ruins around 11–16 June and left **about fifty foot and some workmen** there ([S1], from the pay-roll).
  The army itself was **mostly Welsh levies** from the marcher lordships ([S1], [S9]). The English
  garrison that is actually attested sat at **Dinefwr**, a royal castle since 1277, and after the battle
  it was held by about 35 lances of Somerset and Devon knights under Alan Plukenet ([S1]). No land had
  yet been granted "after the conquest": Giffard's grant of Carreg Cennen is dated 1283 (Wikipedia, Gatehouse; Cadw confirms the grant without a year),
  and Morris has him in full possession of Is Cennen in 1284.
- **The battle date resolves to 16 June in the primary text.** Annales Cambriae says young William de
  Valence was killed "in Estratewy XVI Kalendas Julii", which is 16 June. Morris gives 17 June and cites
  the same annal, apparently by a counting slip ([S1], [S2]). The Annales do not name Llandeilo, and no
  source read here names the Welsh leader.
- **The Welsh Brut edition read here stops at Lady Day (25 March) 1282**, so it has no entry for the
  battle at all ([S3]).
- **Real local grievances of 1282 survive in Latin** in Archbishop Peckham's register ([S4]). They
  complain that English soldiers turned the church of Llangadog into a stable, installed prostitutes and
  struck the chaplain by the altar; that the churches of Llandingad and Llanwrda were burned; that John
  Giffard claimed a Welsh lord's inheritance; and that Welsh lords were denied Welsh law. These are the
  best grounding in the whole project for what people in this valley were angry about in 1282.
- **Talley's "Welsh lay brothers" are not attested.** The first canons came from **St-Jean, Amiens**
  ([S13], [S15]). Gerald of Wales, writing c. 1193–1205, describes Talley's abbot, "canons and
  brothers" (*fratres*) and "servants" being thrown out at night by Whitland's Cistercians ([S14]).
  The *fratres* were probably lay brothers (inference), but nothing says where they came from or what
  they spoke. The canons were later identified with the Welsh ([S13], [S16]).
- **The primary Roman forts report was fetched** ([S19]). Fort 1 is 3.85ha and probably early Flavian
  ("soon after AD 74"). Fort 2 is 1.54ha. The garrison unit is **unknown**, and the site was abandoned by
  about AD 140. The finds include Spanish olive-oil amphorae, Gaulish samian ware, and spelt, barley and
  oats.
- **Attested words usable as quotes** exist for every date except the Iron Age: Roman letters from
  Vindolanda and Tacitus on Britain, both labelled as not local ([S21]–[S23]); Gerald on Talley ([S14]);
  the 1282 grievances ([S4]); Anglo-Norman letters of December 1282 ([S4]); a Rebecca letter of 1842 and
  an 1843 handbill ([S25]); Genesis 24:60 in the Welsh Bible of the 1840s ([S26]); The Welshman's account
  of the railway reaching Llandeilo in 1857 ([S28]); Lingen on the Llandilo workhouse school in 1846
  ([S29]); and a recorded Llansawel speaker born in 1907 ([S33]).
- **Anglo-Norman and Middle English**: both used pronounced final consonants and a sounded final *-e*,
  and Middle English had its long vowels before the Great Vowel Shift ([S37]–[S40]). Modern `fr-FR` and
  `en-GB` voices get all of this wrong.
- **Carmarthenshire dialect** features are sourced from a St Fagans recording and analysis for Llansawel
  ([S33]). Examples: final *-e* for *-au*, *mowr*, *hwys* for *chwys*, *ôd* for *oed*, *mâs*, and the
  tags *chwel*, *chimod* and *yndife*. All the dialect evidence is from the 20th century, so using it for
  1843 or 1857 is a backward projection, and the ⓘ must say so.

## Iron Age: Garn Goch, c. 150 BC

### Classes present (with evidence)

- **Nothing is known directly.** Garn Goch has never been excavated (`ironage:S1`–`ironage:S7`), so no
  class, household or person is evidenced at this site on any date, and **c. 150 BC is not an attested
  date**. It is simply a moment inside the fort's broad span.
- By analogy from Wales generally (Cadw leaflet summarised in the Iron Age note): mixed farming
  households, with local warrior elites who owned decorated weapons and feasting gear (`ironage:S24`–`S28`).
  **Reconstructed**, and Carmarthenshire is not named in that source.
- **Religious specialists**: Caesar (writing 54–51 BC about Gaul) divides the people who count into
  *druides* and *equites*, with the *plebs* "held almost in the place of slaves". He adds that the druids'
  teaching "is thought to have been found in Britain and carried from there into Gaul" ([S24], BG 6.13).
  This is a Roman outsider writing about Gaul, and it is **not evidence for Garn Goch**. At most it
  supports an imagined figure labelled as analogy.

### Languages by class

- Brittonic, for everyone. No sentence-level reconstruction exists (`language:S32`), so any dialogue is a
  **modern Welsh stand-in** (the project's existing decision).

### Topics by class (all reconstructed or imagined)

- **Farmers**: cattle and sheep, and grain. The Iron Age note records barley, emmer and spelt as the
  regional crops (`ironage` Farming section). The weather: Tacitus calls Britain's sky "foul with frequent
  rain and mist" (*caelum crebris imbribus ac nebulis foedum*) and the land "patient of crops, fertile in
  cattle", slow to ripen and quick to sprout, "for the same reason: much moisture of land and sky"
  ([S23], Agricola 12). That was written c. AD 98 about Britain as a whole.
- **Builders**: Y Gaer Fach looks unfinished (`ironage` scene ideas). An argument about whether to finish
  a rampart is grounded.
- **Traders and elites**: Caesar says the Britons used bronze, gold coins or iron bars "of fixed weight"
  as money ([S24], BG 5.12). The Iron Age note says the Demetae **did not mint coins**, so a barter
  conversation is the grounded choice.

### Attested lines usable as quotes

- **None from the Iron Age anywhere near here.** Caesar and Tacitus can be quoted only as *Roman
  descriptions* (for example on an almanac card), never as the words of people at Garn Goch.

## The Roman forts at Dinefwr, c. AD 80

### Classes present (with evidence)

- **Soldiers.** Fort 1 (3.85ha) "suggests that the fort held a substantial military unit, at the very
  least an *ala quingenaria* (cavalry unit) or a *cohors milliaria* ... and perhaps even a larger
  legionary detachment". It probably dates to "an early Flavian phase of campaigning (perhaps soon after
  AD 74)". Fort 2 (1.54ha) perhaps held "a *cohors quingenaria peditata* (a small infantry unit)". Both
  had timber-and-earth defences and timber buildings ([S19], primary excavation report, Hughes 2007).
  **No inscription names the unit**, so the soldiers' origin (Gaul, Germany, Spain, Thrace…) is
  **unknown**. Single-source for the sizes, but this is the primary report, and it supersedes the
  8 versus 12 acres disagreement in the Iron Age note.
- **Vicus (civilian settlement) people.** Traders and families beside the fort, and a later reuse of Fort
  2's interior "by the inhabitants of the vicus after the departure of the soldiers" ([S19]).
- **Local farmers.** The report frames the forts' grain supply and "the effect of the forts on the local
  agricultural economy" as an open research question ([S19]).
- **Dating.** Most samian ware is South Gaulish, which suggests "a reduction in activity in the later
  Trajanic period and abandonment under Hadrian", with abandonment "by AD140" ([S19]). This agrees with
  the app's "abandoned in the early 2nd century".

### Languages by class

- **Latin**: the army's command language, and the language of any writing (inference from general
  knowledge of the Roman army; no local text survives).
- **Soldiers' home languages**: unknown because the unit is unknown. An ⓘ must say so.
- **Brittonic**: local farmers and probably most vicus people. Tacitus says British speech was "not much
  different" from Gaulish (*sermo haud multum diversus*, [S23], Agricola 11).

### Topics by class (grounded)

- **Supply officers and farmers: grain.** Tacitus says that under governors before Agricola (AD 77/78 on)
  Britons were abused over the grain levy: made "to sit by locked granaries and buy grain" to pay the tax,
  and ordered by roundabout routes to carry grain to remote camps rather than the nearest winter quarters
  (*adsidere clausis horreis et emere ultro frumenta ... in remota et avia deferrent*, [S23], Agricola 19).
  That is province-wide, not Dinefwr, but it fits a fort whose report asks about local grain supply.
  Charred **spelt, barley and oats** were found; barley "is frequently used as a fodder crop", which is
  consistent with cavalry ([S19]).
- **Soldiers: imports and home comforts.** "The only amphora fabric recovered is that of the common south
  Spanish olive oil amphora, Dressel 20" ([S19]), plus South Gaulish samian tableware and "Malvern" jars
  from an Iron Age tradition ([S19]).
- **Soldiers: the locals.** The Vindolanda tablet 164 (AD 97–105?) calls the Britons *Brittunculi*
  ("wretched little Britons", [S22]). It is northern and some twenty years later, so a parallel only.
- **Everyone: roads and weather.** Octavius at Vindolanda (AD 104–120) would not fetch hides "while the
  roads are bad" (*dum uiae male sunt*, [S21]).
- **Local elites: Romanisation.** Agricola had chiefs' sons taught, "so that those who lately rejected the
  Roman tongue now coveted its eloquence" (*qui modo linguam Romanam abnuebant, eloquentiam
  concupiscerent*), and the toga became common ([S23], Agricola 21). Province-wide.
- **Coins**: seven silver denarii were found in 2000 on the estate: three Republican (c. 100–31 BC), one
  Augustan, three late 1st century AD ([S19]). This is the earliest money physically found at the site.

### Attested lines usable as quotes (all non-local, label as parallels)

- *"dum uiae male sunt"*, "while the roads are bad" (Vindolanda 343, [S21]).
- *"nec residunt Brittunculi ut iaculos mittant"*, "nor do the wretched Britons mount in order to throw
  javelins" (Vindolanda 164, [S22]).
- *"Caelum crebris imbribus ac nebulis foedum"* (Tacitus, Agricola 12, [S23]).
- The only local Roman text is the lost milestone RIB 2262, *Imp(eratori) C(aesari) M(arco) Cl(audio)
  Tacito P(io) F(elici) Invicto Aug(usto)*, AD 275–276. It was seen in 1697 as a farmhouse cornerstone
  near Dynevor ([S20]). It is **two centuries too late** for AD 80.

## Talley Abbey's founding, c. 1185

### Classes present (with evidence)

- **Canons regular (White Canons).** Talley was founded by the Lord Rhys "as a dependency of Amiens,
  St Jean", "between 1184 and 1189" ([S13]). The VCH also says it "was founded from the monastery of
  St. John's, Amiens" ([S15]). **Cross-checked.**
- **"Brothers" and servants.** Gerald of Wales's *Speculum Ecclesiae* (written after 1200, as quoted by
  Owen 1893, [S14]) describes the abbot of Whitland luring Talley's "abbot ... with certain of his canons
  and brothers" (*abbatem cum canonicis suis aliquot et fratribus*). The convent was then ejected at night
  "with the brethren and servants" (*cum fratribus et servientibus*) "by an armed band of laymen". The
  Premonstratensian order did have lay brothers (Catholic Encyclopedia via catholic.com: "composed of
  priests and lay brothers", [S18], **search-summary only**). Reading *fratres* here as lay brothers is
  **inference**.
- **The founder's court and patrons**: the Lord Rhys and his sons (medieval note).
- **Neighbouring religious**: the Cistercians of Whitland, who seized Talley's grange "Buthelan" and kept
  it after litigation at Rome and before judges in England ([S14]). Owen dates the episode 1193–1205
  (Archbishop Hubert Walter's tenure).
- **Tenants and workers** on the abbey's lands: not documented for 1185 in anything read here.

### Languages by class

- **Latin**: liturgy and documents. Whitland's monks sang the antiphon *Salve Regina* in triumph after the
  ejection ([S14]).
- **Canons' vernacular at the founding: probably French** (Picard, from Amiens). This is **inference**
  from the mother house. No source states the language of the first canons.
- **Welsh**: tenants, servants and (inference) most local lay brothers or recruits. By the late 13th
  century the house was "supported by the Welsh", and Edward I "targeted" it ([S13]). Williams mentions
  "the Welsh language of the canons and their Welsh patrons" ([S16]) without giving a date, so it is
  **not evidence for 1185**.
- **Anglo-Norman French**: in the Lord Rhys's circle. The DWB says Rhys adopted "Norman ways in dress and
  domestic manners" (medieval note, `medieval:S14`).

### Topics by class (grounded)

- **Canons**: poverty and the site. Gerald calls it "a rough and sterile spot, surrounded by woods on
  every side and beyond measure inaccessible, and sufficiently meanly endowed" (*in solo sterili hispidoque
  nimis, ex omni latere valde silvestri et praeter modum inaccessibili, satis exiliter fundatam*, [S14]).
  Also the building plan, of which only the east end was ever finished (`medieval:S18`, `medieval:S19`).
- **Canons and servants**: the threat from Whitland, and the walk to Canterbury "poor and on foot"
  ([S14]). **This is c. 1193–1205, not 1185.**
- **Later link to Llandeilo**: c. 1222 Bishop Iorwerth, formerly abbot of Talley, confirmed Talley's
  appropriation of the churches of **Llandeilo Fawr and Llanegwad** ([S13], single-source). So in 1282
  Llandeilo's own church was Talley's.

### Attested lines usable as quotes

- Gerald's Latin description of the site (above), and the singing of the *Salve Regina*, both from
  [S14], which prints Latin and Owen's English. Attribute them to Gerald. They are not anyone's speech.
- The 1285 royal charter putting Talley under Welbeck (Charter Roll 13 Edw. I, in [S14]) calls it
  "formerly wealthy" (*dudum opulentam*) but found "impoverished and destroyed" through failures "of rule
  and of honest conduct". This **contradicts** Gerald's "meanly endowed". Both are partisan voices, so
  show both.

### Contradiction

- Founding date: Monastic Wales gives 1184–1189 ([S13]). Owen (1893) argued for 1193–97 from Gerald's
  silence in 1188 ([S14]). The timeline note already records 1184/1185/1189. Owen's is an old argument
  from silence, so keep c. 1185 with its uncertainty.

## The Battle of Llandeilo Fawr, June 1282

### Classes present (with evidence)

- **Welsh lords in revolt.** On 26 March 1282 (*in crastino Annunciationis*) the royal castles of
  Llanbadarn, Llandovery and Carreg Cennen were taken by Dafydd ap Gruffudd; Rhys Fychan son of Rhys son
  of Maelgwn; Gruffudd and Cynan, sons of Maredudd ab Owain; and "Gruffudd and Llywelyn, sons of Rhys
  Fychan, lords of Is Cennen" (*dominos Deyskennen*) ([S2]). Morris gives the same list, also from the
  Annales, and adds that Dafydd destroyed the fortresses rather than hold them ([S1]). **Cross-checked,
  though Morris depends on the Annales.**
- **A loyal Welsh lord**: Rhys ap Maredudd of Dryslwyn "not only abstained from revolt but gave
  assistance to Edward" (DWB, [S11]). In 1283 he "held with the lord king ... and received more lands"
  (Annales, [S2]). **Cross-checked.**
- **The royal army.** On 11 June an expedition left Dinefwr for Carreg Cennen with "1,600 foot from the
  marches of Cemaes, Kidwelly, and Llanstephan, and ... about fifty of Plukenet's horse", plus the
  retinues of William de Valence, some marchers and Gloucester ([S1], from the pay-roll). Pilling gives
  "approximately 8,000 Welsh infantry" and 200 cavalry, with a small band of Forest of Dean miners as the
  "only Englishmen present" ([S9]). The two numbers differ, but **both make the foot overwhelmingly Welsh
  levies**. That the foot were largely Welsh is cross-checked; the numbers are not.
- **At Carreg Cennen after the English arrived**: "Caercynan, or the ruins of the castle, was occupied by
  fifty foot and some workmen, as if the intention were to rebuild for permanent occupation" ([S1],
  single-source). Whether they stayed after the rout is **not known**. Gatehouse says the castle was
  "captured by the forces of Llywelyn ap Gruffudd" in 1282 and "lost again 1283" ([S5]).
- **Royal garrison at Dinefwr**: Dinefwr had been royal since 1277 and in the justiciar's custody from
  1280 ([S1]). After the battle, "the paid cavalry, suspiciously reduced in number" were split, one body
  of about thirty-five lances "remaining under Alan Plukenet to hold Dynevor". Plukenet's corps was
  "composed of Somerset and Devon tenants" ([S1]). Crossbowmen and English foot "were clearly in permanent
  pay in the castles" ([S1]). Wikipedia also has the English heading "back to ... Dinefwr Castle" ([S10],
  a lead). **This is the attested English garrison near Llandeilo in June 1282.**
- **Royal officials**: Robert de Tibetot, justiciar at Carmarthen, "the guiding spirit throughout this
  war", working with the Bishop of St Davids ([S1]).
- **Clergy**: parish priests, including the chaplain at Llangadog struck by English soldiers ([S4]). The
  Talley canons held Llandeilo's church ([S13]).
- **Ordinary Welsh people**: free and unfree, under Welsh law (`medieval:S41`). They are the people whose
  "hiding-places of the woods and the marshes" the ambushers broke out from (Wykes, as quoted by Pilling,
  [S8]).

### Languages by class

- **Welsh**: the population, the rebel and loyal lords, and most of the royal army's foot.
- **Anglo-Norman French**: the magnates and knights (Gloucester, de Valence, Plukenet's Somerset and Devon
  knights), and letters between the great. Peckham wrote to Edward I and to Maud Longespée in French in
  December 1282 ([S4]).
- **Latin**: church, chronicle, royal records and the formal complaints ([S2], [S4]).
- **Middle English**: English foot, crossbowmen and the Forest of Dean miners ([S1], [S9]). Somerset and
  Devon men would speak southern dialects (inference).

### Topics by class (grounded)

- **Welsh lords and freemen: law and land.** Peckham's register keeps "grievances" (*gravamina*) from
  Ystrad Tywi ([S4], Latin; working translation here):
  - A Rhys "the Little" (*Resus Parvus*) of Ystrad Tywi says that after he gave the king "his castle at
    Dynewr", while in the custody of Payn de Chaworth, "six noble men" of his were killed, and he never had
    justice. John Giffard "challenged" him over "his own inheritance at Hirfryn" (*Hirurym*). When he asked
    for "the law of his country or the law of the county of Carmarthen", he got none and "wholly lost his
    land".
  - Llywelyn and Hywel, sons of Rhys, complain that Edward disinherited them, "denying them all the laws
    and customs of Wales, so that they had their lands neither by law nor by grace".
  - The sons of Maredudd ab Owain complain that nobles were made to sit in judgement over their own
    unfree tenants and vice versa, "where their ancestors never suffered such things from the English".
    They add: "all Christians have laws and customs in their own lands; even the Jews among the English
    have laws".
- **Clergy and parishioners: sacrilege.** "In the church of St David, which is called Llangadog
  (*Laungadawe*), they made stables and placed prostitutes, carried off all the goods in it, burned all
  the houses; and in the same church beside the altar struck the chaplain on the head with a sword and
  left him half-alive". Also "the church of Dyngad [Llandingad, Llandovery] and the church of Llanwrda
  they despoiled and burned", and robbed other churches "of chalices and books" ([S4]). Llangadog is about
  6 miles from Llandeilo, **inside the radius**.
- **Farmers: sheep and money.** The Annales record "a great murrain of sheep, which began in the year
  before", around 1281 ([S2], an OCR passage with garbled context). The Brut for 1279: "Edward ordered the
  coining of new money; and that the halfpennies and farthings should be made round. And thus was
  fulfilled the prophecy of Myrddin, when he says, 'The symbol of the exchange shall be split, and the
  half shall be round'" ([S3]). That is a real talking point, prophecy included.
- **Soldiers of the royal army: plunder, fear, the woods.** A week's raiding, the force "loaded with
  loot", "without scouts", "attacked all of a sudden from the hills" ([S1]). The Welsh "broke out from the
  hiding-places of the woods and the marshes" (Wykes, [S8]). Trivet: "many men on the side of the Welsh
  were killed, and Gilbert, himself, lost five knights" ([S8]).
- **Knights at Dinefwr: the dead and the command.** Young William de Valence, "kinsman of the king",
  killed ([S8]). Gloucester stripped of his command on 6 July ([S1], `medieval:S28`).

### Attested lines usable as quotes

- **Annales Cambriae, 1282** (Latin): *"interfectus fuit Willelmus de Valenciis junior haeres
  Penbrochiae in Estratewy XVI Kalendas Julii"*, "William de Valence the younger, heir of Pembroke, was
  killed in Ystrad Tywi on 16 June" ([S2]). A clergy scene could read it out.
- **Brut y Tywysogion, 1282** (Middle Welsh, the last entry of that edition): "Y vlwydyn rac wyneb gwyl
  Veir y gehyded y goresgynnawd Gruffud ab Maredud ... a Rys ab Maelgwn ... dref Aber Ystwyth ar castell"
  ("The ensuing year, on the feast of St Mary of the equinox, Gruffudd son of Maredudd ... and Rhys son of
  Maelgwn ... took the town and castle of Aberystwyth", [S3]). I have written *w* where the OCR shows *O*:
  the edition uses a special *w* glyph. Check against the page image before quoting.
- **The gravamina** above ([S4]), in Latin, can be quoted, labelled as formal complaints.
- **Anglo-Norman letters of December 1282** ([S4]; see the Anglo-Norman section below).
- **Poetry**: the design doc's "a real attested line" for a bardd teulu was **not researched here**.
  Gruffudd ab yr Ynad Coch's elegy for Llywelyn belongs to December 1282 or later, not June, and was not
  fetched. Leave it until it is.

## The aftermath, 1283–87, and the siege of Dryslwyn

### Classes present (with evidence)

- **Imprisoned Welsh lords.** Gruffudd and Cynan ap Maredudd, Rhys Fychan ap Rhys ap Maelgwn, Gruffudd
  and Llywelyn ap Rhys Fychan and others were "held ... and all imprisoned at London", while Rhys ap
  Maredudd "held with the lord king" and gained lands ([S2]).
- **The new Marcher lord.** In 1283 Edward granted Carreg Cennen to John Giffard (Wikipedia
  [S6], citing Davis 2021; Gatehouse [S5]). Cadw names Giffard as the builder and calls the castle
  "Giffard's reward", without giving a year ([S7]). Morris says the king "had also granted him the march of
  Isgenen", and that "in 1284 he was in full possession" ([S1]). **Cross-checked for 1283–84; no charter
  date was found.** Giffard's household is **correctly placed from 1283–84 on**, not in June 1282.
  Peckham's letter to "Mahaud Lungespeye" ([S4]) is probably to Giffard's wife Maud, widow of William
  Longespée ([S6]; Wikipedia on Giffard). That identification is inference.
- **English burgesses at Newton.** A borough "associated with Dinefwr Castle", "first mentioned in 1297"
  (Coflein, [S44]). Heneb says there were 26 burgages by 1298, a twin town of the old Welsh "Dinefwr" and
  the new "Newtown", and charters that let the "English" burgesses "monopolise commercial and
  administrative affairs" and gave them immunity from being fined "by Welshmen" in the royal courts
  ([S45], **search-summary only**; the page loops on redirects). This is **the first attested English
  civilian community at Llandeilo's doorstep**, from the 1290s.
- **Church.** In 1283 "the church of Llangadog was made collegiate" (*Ecclesia de Langadauc facta est
  collegiata*, [S2]). In 1285 Talley was put under Welbeck by royal charter, witnessed among others by
  Gloucester and Robert de Tibetot ([S14]).
- **The 1287 revolt.** Rhys ap Maredudd, "at odds with Robert Tybetot, then the king's justiciar at
  Carmarthen", took Llandovery, Dinefwr and Carreg Cennen on "the Sunday before the feast of St Barnabas"
  (8 June 1287) ([S2]). DWB gives 8 June too ([S11]). **Cross-checked.**
- **The besieging army** at Dryslwyn on 15 August 1287 numbered 10,635 foot, and "besides the
  crossbowmen and mechanics, 3,740 only were English" ([S1], pay-rolls). The Welsh contingents included
  "Welsh of Carmarthenshire, 1,000" and Welsh of North Pembroke and Cardigan, 920 ([S1]). **Carmarthenshire
  Welshmen besieged a Welsh lord's castle for the king.** The skilled mechanics were English, from the new
  northern castles. Carpenters earned about 3d and 4d a day, masons 5d, 6d and 7d (OCR "yi. and 4^." read
  as 3d. and 4d.; check) ([S1]).
- **Siege workers.** The engine's fitting "came to £14. Twenty quarrymen and four carters made and brought
  up the stone bullets" ([S1]). The medieval note (`medieval:S23`) says **24 carters**: a discrepancy,
  possibly OCR on one side. Twenty-six *fossatores* were paid to remove stones and mortar and break through
  the chapel wall ([S1]). William de Montchensy was killed under an undermined wall ([S1], [S2]).
- **After the fall.** Dinefwr was garrisoned by "ten archers and twenty crossbows". Dryslwyn under
  Plukenet had "two knights, twenty-two troopers, twenty crossbows, and eighty archers; only four of the
  horse and forty archers were Welsh" ([S1]). Loot from Rhys's lands was sold: bulls and oxen at about 5s
  each and 126 cows at about 4s each (OCR-uncertain figures) ([S1]).

### Languages by class

- Welsh for the Welsh foot (the majority of the army) and the population. Middle English for the English
  foot, archers, crossbowmen and mechanics. Anglo-Norman for the earls and knights. Latin for the
  pay-rolls and chronicles. After 1284 **Newton's burgesses** add an English-speaking civilian group
  (inference from "English" burgesses; their actual speech is not documented).

### Topics by class (grounded)

- **Siege workers**: stone shot, wages, the mine collapse ([S1]).
- **Canons of Talley**: Welbeck's authority over them from 1285 ([S14]). In 1291 the abbot of Prémontré

## The Rebecca Riots, 1843

### Classes present (with evidence)

- **Tenant farmers and lime carters**, crossing three trusts and three toll bars to the kilns, with tolls
  about 30% of the cost of the lime (`victorian:S7`). Farmers' union meetings were held at Cwmifor and
  Penlan (victorian note, Rebecca addendum).
- **The toll farmer**: Thomas Bullin, "chief collector of tolls and builder of gates in the area, an
  Englishman hated in Wales" (TNA glossary, [S25]). **Toll collectors**, such as William Rees at
  Trevaughan, whose books were seized by disguised men in "white frocks" in August 1843 ([S25]).
- **Turnpike trustees**, accused of placing gates "so as to exempt their own Tenants from paying Toll"
  (George Ellis's memorandum for the Commission, 2 November 1843, [S25]).
- **Gentry and magistrates**: Rice-Trevor and the Dynevor estate; the Llandovery magistrates David Jones
  Lewis and Lewis Lewis (victorian note). **Their Welsh names are a caution**: it is not documented that
  local magistrates were English or non-Welsh-speaking.
- **Clergy**: the rector, Rev. Mr Pugh, who returned half his tithe (`victorian:S7`). Nonconformist
  chapels, including the Baptist chapel graveyard at Cwmifor used for a meeting (victorian note).
- **Soldiers**: 4th Light Dragoons and the 41st Regiment billeted in the town for nearly two years
  (victorian note). Their men's origins and language were **not researched**.
- **Officials and gentry reformers**: the High Sheriff of Cardiganshire, E. C. Lloyd Hall, issued a
  handbill "TO REBECCA AND HER DAUGHTERS" **in Welsh and English** on 20 June 1843 ([S25]). It is in
  Cardiganshire, not the Llandeilo district, but shows that officials addressed Rebecca in Welsh.

### Languages by class

- **Welsh**: farmers, labourers, chapels, and the handbill's Welsh edition ([S25]).
- **English**: trusts, courts, Commission, press, the toll farmer. **Rebecca's own letters could be in
  English**: the St Clears letter of 16 December 1842 is in a Welsh writer's English ([S25]).
- **Bilingual**: some gentry and ministers (inference; not documented for named local people here).

### Topics by class (grounded)

- **Farmers**: tolls on lime and coal, narrow-wheel surcharges, the steep roads needing extra horses
  (Ellis memorandum, [S25]); rents up at least 100% from 1793 to 1843 while prices fell (`victorian:S4`);
  tithes (`victorian:S7`).
- **Rebecca's "daughters"**: the constables, "Bowlin and company", and national grievance. The St Clears
  letter says: "it is a shamefull thing for us welchmen to have the sons of Henegust have a dominion over
  us. Do you not remember the long knives which Henegust hath invented to kill our fore fathers" ([S25]).
  That is the medieval "Treachery of the Long Knives", used as a live grievance in 1842.
- **Officials**: "The penalty for pulling down a Turnpike House is TRANSPORTATION FOR LIFE" (Lloyd Hall
  handbill, [S25]).
- **Chapel and Bible**: the name came from Genesis 24:60 (below).

### Attested lines usable as quotes

- **Genesis 24:60, Welsh Bible as printed 1847/48** ([S26], OCR of two printings reconciled): *"Ac a
  fendithiasant Rebeccah, ac a ddywedasant wrthi, Ein chwaer wyt, bydd di fil fyrddiwn; ac etifedded dy
  had borth ei gaseion."* The OCR has "fU fyrddiwn" in both printings, which I read as *fil fyrddiwn*.
  One printing ends "borth d gaseum", the other "borth ei gaseion". **Check a page image before voicing
  it.** English (KJV, [S27]): "And they blessed Rebekah, and said unto her, Thou art our sister, be thou
  the mother of thousands of millions, and let thy seed possess the gate of those which hate them."
- **Rebecca letter, 16 December 1842** (HO 45/265 f1, [S25], transcript under the Open Government Licence
  v3.0): "all thine property in one night shall be in conflagration if they will not obey to this notice"
  and "Becca her children heeds no more of them than the Grass-hopers which fly in the summer". It is
  signed "Faithfull to Death with the county Becca & children". This is from St Clears, not Llandeilo:
  label it as regional.
- **Lloyd Hall's handbill**, 20 June 1843 ([S25]): "Go each one to your own homes on Wednesday night,
  peaceably and quietly."

## The railway, 1857 into the 1860s

### Classes present (with evidence)

- **Railway directors and officers**: Biddulph (chairman), Smale, Butts, Fowler, Christie, and the
  engineer, traffic manager and contractor, named at the opening dinner ([S28], The Welshman,
  23 January 1857).
- **Townspeople in procession**: "a procession was formed by the members of the 'Ap Tewdur' lodge of Odd
  Fellows, with sashes, knots, and other mystic insignia preceded by inscribed banners and a band of
  music, and followed by the Directors of the Railway and visitors, and gentlemen and tradesmen of the
  town", with the streets "crowded with people in holyday attire" ([S28]).
- **Clergy**: grace at the dinner was said "by the Rev. John Griffiths, vicar of Llandilo" ([S28]).
- **Workers and industrialists**: the "Band of the Dafen Tin Works" played as the train left Llanelli;
  R. K. Penson was building lime kilns at Llandybie with a junction to the railway ([S28]).
- **Farmers**: expected to gain "good prices for their corn and cattle", with "lime and coal" brought
  "almost to their doors" ([S28]).
- **Drovers**: the droving trade and its link to learning English (`victorian:S60`, John Johnes's
  evidence).
- **Schoolchildren and teachers**: Lingen at the Llandilo Union Workhouse School, 31 October 1846 ([S29]).
  The schoolmaster Zerubbabel Davies told the Commission he had "kept school in the parish of Llandilofawr"
  and elsewhere, having "combined preaching with school-keeping" ([S29]).

### Languages by class

- **Welsh**: most townspeople and farmers (county 84.9% Welsh-speaking even in 1911, `language:S26`).
- **English**: directors, officers, the press report, and schoolbooks. The workhouse children's only books
  were "the New London Spelling-book, and Bailey's First Book" ([S29]).
- **Classroom language clash, attested for Llandeilo itself**: "They did not understand the names of the
  figures in English, and the schoolmaster could not explain them in Welsh. The house-master acted as
  interpreter" ([S29]).

### Topics by class (grounded)

- **Farmers**: corn and cattle prices, lime and coal ([S28]).
- **Town and lodge**: the procession, the dinner, the weather: "a murky atmosphere and drenching rain
  spoiled the attempt to put on the baubles of festivity" ([S28]).
- **Arrival**: "The arrival of the train at Llandilo was greeted with the cheers of the multitude, the
  booming of cannon, the merry pealing of bells, and the inspiriting strains of music" ([S28]).
- **Industry**: the anthracite coalfield, which "should not escape the attention of those interested in
  it" ([S28]).
- **Children and teachers**: English-only books in a Welsh-speaking school ([S29]). The Welsh Not remains
  **not locally attested** (victorian note).

## Anglo-Norman French and Middle English (13th–14th century incomers)

### Who spoke them locally (with evidence)

- **Anglo-Norman French**: earls, marcher lords and knights, such as Gloucester, de Valence and Plukenet's
  Somerset and Devon knights ([S1]), and the correspondence of the great ([S4]). "From the mid-13th century
  to the early 15th century, Anglo-French was much used in law reports, charters, ordinances, official
  correspondence, and trade" ([S37]). In English courts it "was not easily understood and left lay people
  confused", until the 1362 Pleading in English Act ([S42]).
- **Middle English**: English foot, archers, crossbowmen, the Forest of Dean miners and woodmen, and the
  mechanics from the northern castles ([S1], [S9]), then Newton's burgesses from the 1290s ([S44],
  [S45]). Officially, English first appeared beside Latin and French in 1258, when "the Crown issued for
  the first time, official letters with the same text in Latin, French and English" ([S42]).

### Attested sample phrases

**Anglo-Norman, December 1282, Archbishop Peckham** ([S4], edition of 1884, OCR):

- To Edward I, about Llywelyn's body: *"Sire, sachez ke ceus ke furent a la mort Lewelin truverent au plus
  prive lu de sun cors menue choses ke nus avoms veues; entre les autres choses i out une lettre de guisee
  par faus nuns de traysun."* "Sire, know that those who were at Llywelyn's death found in the most private
  place of his body small things which we have seen; among the other things there was a letter disguised
  by false names, of treason." (Working translation.)
- To Maud Longespée: *"Mes sachez ke Lewelyn, qui esteyt prince de Gales, ne poet estre asouz se il ne
  mustra signe de repentaunce en sa mort."* "But know that Llywelyn, who was prince of Wales, cannot be
  absolved unless he showed a sign of repentance at his death."
- Everyday formulae from the same letters: *"Madame, je vus mercy mut des lettres..."* ("Madam, I thank you
  much for the letters", to Queen Eleanor, 1283) and *"Madame, Dieu vous eyt en sa garde"* ("Madam, God
  have you in his keeping").
- Spelling shows Anglo-Norman traits: *ke* for *que*, *vus/nus*, *seynur*, *saunz* and *graunt* (au before
  n), *-um/-uns* verb endings (*nus priums*, *savums*). These match the Fordham list: "u for SMF o or ou",
  "k for the [k] sound spelt qu", and "-om -um -oun and -oms, -omes, -ums, -umes for SMF -ons" ([S38]).
  **Cross-checked** between the text and the description.

**Middle English**:

- Proclamation of Henry III, 18 October 1258 ([S41], [S42]): *"Henri, þurȝ Godes fultume King on
  Engleneloande, Lhoauerd on Yrloande, Duk on Normandi, on Aquitaine, and Eorl on Aniow, send igretinge to
  alle hise holde, ilaerde and ileawede, on Huntendoneschire."* ("Henry, through God's help King in
  England, Lord in Ireland, Duke in Normandy, in Aquitaine, and Earl in Anjou, sends greeting to all his
  faithful, learned and lay, in Huntingdonshire.") The translation is mine and the text is English, not
  Welsh. It is the earliest official English and shows the kind of English Plukenet's Somerset men spoke
  a generation later.
- **No Middle English text written in south-west Wales in 1282–1350 was found in this pass** (open
  question).
- **Welsh speakers writing English**: the *Hymn to the Virgin*, Oxford c. 1470, by Ieuan ap Hywel
  Swrdwal, in Welsh orthography: *"O michti ladi, owr leding / tw haf / at hefn owr abeiding"* ([S43]).
  Welsh *ch* is [χ], so *michti* shows the *gh* in "mighty" still sounded (inference, consistent with
  [S40]). It is 190 years after 1282, but it is the only evidence found of how a Welshman heard English
  sounds.

### Pronunciation: what a modern TTS voice gets wrong

**Anglo-Norman with `fr-FR-HenriNeural` / `DeniseNeural`**:

- **Final consonants**: in Old French "all written consonants (including final ones) were pronounced,
  except for *s* preceding non-stop consonants and *t* in *et*" ([S39]). Modern French drops most final
  consonants, so *nus*, *sunt*, *mut* and *faus* will be read silent.
- **Final *-e***: "final *e* was pronounced [ə]" ([S39]). Modern French drops it, so *prive*, *lettre* and
  *signe* lose a syllable.
- ***ch* and *j/g***: [tʃ] and [dʒ] in medieval French; "English has preserved the older sound"
  ([S37], and /t͡ʃ/, /d͡ʒ/ in [S39]). Modern French gives [ʃ] and [ʒ]: *choses* comes out "shoz" instead
  of roughly "tchozes".
- **Nasals**: "the nasal vowels were not separate phonemes ... The nasal consonant was fully pronounced"
  ([S39]). Anglo-Norman *soun* and *round* were "originally pronounced something like ... 'soon', 'roond'"
  ([S37]). Modern French nasalises *saunz* and *graunt* as [sɑ̃], [ɡʁɑ̃].
- ***r***: a "voiced alveolar trill" in Old French ([S39]). Modern French uses a uvular [ʁ].
- **Diphthongs**: Anglo-Norman reduced *ai* and *ei* to *e* (*faire/fere*, [S38]) and kept *ei* where
  Paris moved to *oi* [wa] (*veil* against *voile*, [S37]). Modern French reads *oi* as [wa] everywhere.
- **Advice (inference)**: either respell for the TTS (e.g. write out final syllables) and still carry an
  ⓘ, or accept the approximation with an ⓘ saying so: "modern French voice; 13th-century Anglo-Norman
  sounded its final consonants and *-e*, trilled its *r*, and said *ch* as in English 'church'."

**Middle English with `en-GB-RyanNeural` / `SoniaNeural`**:

- **Long vowels before the Great Vowel Shift** ([S40]): *time* /iː/ (like "teem"), *house* /uː/ ("hoose"),
  *name* /aː/ ("nahm"), *food* /oː/, *sweet* /eː/. A modern voice applies the shifted values.
- **Consonants** ([S40]): "kn-, gn-, wr- retained pronunciation", "final gh represented [x] or [ç]", and
  "initial h remained pronounced". A modern voice drops the k, w and gh.
- **Final *-e***: "Around Chaucer's time, final /ə/ was dropped" ([S40]). So in 1282 it was still spoken.
  A modern voice drops it.
- **Southern dialect**: Old English dialects that "voiced initial fricatives" gave doublets like *fat* and
  *vat* ([S40]). A Somerset or Devon soldier plausibly said [v] and [z] for initial f and s (inference
  from regional pattern, not attested for these men).
- **Advice**: a respelled text ("hoose", "nahm-uh", "knicht") makes the TTS closer. The ⓘ should say
  "modern English voice; 1280s English had the old long vowels, a sounded -e, and sounded k and gh".

## Carmarthenshire dialect notes (for 19th-century speech)

- **Source**: St Fagans / Amgueddfa Cymru's analysis of a recording of **Mrs Martha Williams of
  Llansawel, born 1907**, introduced as "Enghraifft o dafodiaith Sir Gaerfyrddin" (an example of the
  Carmarthenshire dialect) ([S33]). Llansawel is inside the radius. It is the same recording as
  `language:S33`, whose transcript was **not read** until now.
- **Features the museum lists** ([S33], Welsh; my glosses):
  - "Yr *e* yn y sillaf olaf": final *-e* for standard *-au/-ai*, as in *bore*, *dechre* 'dechrau',
    *bydde* 'byddai'. Wikipedia's Dyfedeg article gives the same pattern (*gore*, *ffrindie*, *gafel*),
    per a search summary ([S35]), so this is **cross-checked**.
  - *ow* in *mowr* 'mawr' (though *aw* also occurs).
  - "Colli *h* yn achlysurol yn unig": only occasional h-loss (*yd* 'hyd', but *hyd*, *haul*, *helpu*).
  - *hw* for *chw*: *hwys* 'chwys'. In southern dialects "initial /χw/ is colloquially realised as [ʍ]",
    e.g. *chwech* ([S35], search-summary only).
  - *ô* in *ôd* 'oed'. The museum contrasts *wêd* at Pen-caer (Pembrokeshire) and *oid* at Blaenpennal.
  - *â* in *mâs* 'maes', *câ* 'cae'.
  - Southern vocabulary: *bord*, *cwmpo*, *dala*, *dodi*, *ffilu*, *mâs*. Tags: *chwel*, *chim(b)od*,
    *yndife*. Local words in the transcript: *crwt* 'bachgen', *bwti* 'bron', *gambo* (hay cart),
    *picwarch*, *ystod/stodion* (swathes), *drennydd*, *cagen* 'teisen', *pasged*, *stên* (big enamel
    jug), *ifed/hifed* 'yfed', *gweud*. Also *moyn* 'want', *gweud* 'say' and *wês* 'oes' are listed as
    south-west forms by Wrecsam360 ([S36], search-summary only).
  - Grammar: the soft mutation of *ll* after predicative *yn* (*yn lipa*), "a natural development" in the
    dialects; mutated ordinals in dates (*y ddouddegfed o Orffennaf*).
- **Attested sample of speech** (20th century, about haymaking in her childhood, so roughly the 1910s):
  *"Wel, weda i wthoch chi, och chi yn lladd y gwair i ddechre. Os gallech chi, a bod 'i'n sych, lladd e yn
  y bore."* and *"'Le ma'r crwt 'na nawr 'te wedi mynd?'"* ([S33]). The museum's copyright terms were not
  checked, so paraphrase features rather than reproduce the transcript in the app.
- **The 19th-century problem**: no 19th-century transcript of Carmarthenshire speech was found. Using
  these features for 1843 or 1857 is **reconstruction by projecting back one or two generations**
  (inference). The ⓘ should say "dialect features from a Llansawel speaker born 1907".
- **Contrast for the class layer**: the museum notes Pembrokeshire's "unique features" (*we'*, *wedd*
  for *oedd*) against Llansawel's more "neutral" southern sound ([S34]).
- **TTS**: whether `cy-GB-AledNeural` / `NiaNeural` sound northern or southern was **not tested**. Spelling
  dialect forms into the text (*mowr*, *bydde*, *mâs*) is the only control available.
