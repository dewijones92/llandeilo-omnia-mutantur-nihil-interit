## Summary

For roughly 900 years before the Roman army reached the Tywi valley, this area belonged to
farming communities who built and lived inside earth-and-stone hillforts and smaller defended
farmsteads. The single biggest, most impressive site in the whole 10-mile radius is **Garn Goch**
(Y Gaer Fawr and Y Gaer Fach) near Bethlehem, one of the largest hillforts in Wales — but it has
**never been excavated**, so almost everything about who lived there and how is inference from its
surviving earthworks, not dug-up evidence. Several smaller hillforts and enclosures (Fan Camp at
Llansadwrn, Tir Mawr near Llangadog, a multi-period site at Allt y Ferin, and a probable hillfort
at Grongar Hill) sit within the radius but are thinly recorded — none has been excavated either.
Two further well-known hillforts (Merlin's Hill near Carmarthen, Pen y Gaer near Llandovery) sit
just outside a strict 10-mile line but are worth a mention for context.

The people here are conventionally called the **Demetae**, a tribal name recorded by the Roman
geographer Ptolemy and later surviving into the post-Roman kingdom of Dyfed — but whether "Demetae"
was how these communities actually thought of themselves, or a label Rome imposed on a looser
patchwork of farming groups, is genuinely unresolved. Daily life was built around the roundhouse —
timber-framed, wattle-and-daub or stone-footed walls, conical thatched roof, one south/south-east
facing door — mixed farming of cereals (barley, emmer and spelt wheat) and livestock (cattle and
sheep above all), and a material culture (clothing, tools, trade goods) that has to be reconstructed
almost entirely by analogy with better-preserved sites elsewhere in Britain, because Wales's acidic
soil destroys organic remains and no Iron Age metalwork or textile find is recorded for
Carmarthenshire in the sources checked here. The end-point for this era is the Roman conquest: a
governor's campaign in the mid-70s AD, a fort built at Carmarthen (Moridunum) around AD 75, and —
found only in 2003, by a magnetometer/ground-radar survey rather than by drilling into memory or
myth — two overlapping Roman forts inside what is now Dinefwr Park, excavated in 2005 and once
broadcast live on Channel 4's *Big Roman Dig*.

This document draws a hard line, repeatedly, between (a) what is specifically documented for this
10-mile radius, (b) what is reconstructed by analogy with excavated Iron Age sites elsewhere in
Wales or Britain, and (c) claims from tourism/enthusiast sites that could not be corroborated
against Coflein, Cadw, Archwilio or an academic source, and should be treated with real caution.
Several genuine gaps turned up in this research (no population estimate, no local pollen study, no
Carmarthenshire Iron Age metalwork find, no excavation of any hillfort in the radius) — these are
listed under **Open questions** rather than papered over.

## Sites

### Garn Goch (Y Gaer Fawr and Y Gaer Fach)

- **Welsh names:** Y Gaer Fawr ("the big fort"), Y Gaer Fach ("the little fort"); the hill and
  common together are Y Garn Goch ("the red cairn"). A third, smaller earthwork, Llwyndu Camp,
  lies further west again.
- **Location:** Bethlehem hamlet, Bannau Brycheiniog (Brecon Beacons) National Park, ~3–4 miles
  SW of Llangadog and ~4 miles E of Llandeilo. Approx. **51.902°N, 3.905°W**. Grid refs: Y Gaer
  Fawr SN6912024320 (Coflein NPRN 100866); Y Gaer Fach SN6854624271 (NPRN 100872), ~180m west.
- **Description:** Y Gaer Fawr is a large hillfort, **720m NE–SW by up to 230m** at its widest
  (SW end), following the hill's contour at c. 236m elevation. Ramparts were originally
  **stone-faced, roughly 10m high and 5m thick** in places, now visible mainly as a wide rubble
  bank; the main circuit runs to roughly 1,700m of stone-walled rampart, with a further ~700m of
  earth-and-stone rampart around a northern annexe. The south-west rampart has a large scree mass
  and what Coflein describes as "an elaborate gateway passage." Coflein's own record counts **at
  least 8 entrances** in total (two main prehistoric gates — a north-east pair of parallel
  slab-lined passages, and a collapsed south-west walled gateway — plus 6 recorded posterns);
  Cadw-derived visitor text instead rounds this to "**at least 6** separate entrances... which may
  have supported wooden gates," marked by pairs of large upright stones. **Both figures are given
  in sources below; treat Coflein's more granular count as the more authoritative.**
  Y Gaer Fach is a smaller oval stone-walled enclosure, **~168m E–W by 115m**, with a single
  east-facing entrance opening onto a walled trackway. Coflein describes it as unfinished:
  "survives in a ruinous state and appears incomplete... perhaps abandoned part way through a
  programme of rebuilding that was never completed." Interior features recorded on Coflein include
  a central pond/boggy hollow with rock outcrops, at least one **rock-cut roundhouse platform**,
  several Bronze Age burial mounds, a Neolithic-style stone long cairn (~55m) on the summit (its
  Neolithic date is a stylistic guess — "no megalithic structures... currently visible" per
  Coflein), a possible standing stone identified in 2009, and — much later — the ruins of a
  medieval farmstead (house + 30m barn) built inside the fort, showing the site was reused long
  after the Iron Age.
- **Size — a genuine cross-source discrepancy, explained rather than picked:** Cadw/tourism
  sources give Y Gaer Fawr as **11.2 hectares** (also seen as 11.7ha) and Y Gaer Fach as **1.5ha**.
  Coflein's own record instead states **16.6 hectares total, "surpassed only by Deer Park (22ha)
  and Penycloddiau (20.5ha)" in Wales**. This is very likely not a contradiction but two different
  area definitions: the smaller figure for the main fort alone, the larger for fort-plus-annexe.
  Recommend citing both explicitly (c. 11.2ha for the fort itself; c. 16.6ha including its
  northern annexe) rather than picking one.
- **Dating:** Coflein's own hedged framing: "Given the size and complexity of the hillfort a
  **Late Bronze Age or Iron Age date is most likely** for its main construction and development,
  but it may incorporate earlier monuments." Cadw-derived visitor text frames both forts as
  "probably dating to the Iron Age period (**c. 800 BC – AD 74**)." **A specific claim that the
  main camp was "rebuilt by the Romans between AD 47–78"**, tied to governors Ostorius Scapula and
  Sextus Julius Frontinus and the capture of Caratacus, appears on a local-history site
  (llandeilo.org) and is repeated near-verbatim across several tourism-aggregator pages — but does
  **not** appear in Coflein's own record, and no independent (Cadw, academic, or Archwilio) source
  corroborates it. **Treat this Roman-rebuild claim as single-source/unverified** — either drop it
  from documented content or flag it explicitly as one local tradition, not settled archaeology.
- **Excavation:** No record found of a full research excavation of either fort. What exists is
  **survey only**: A.H.A. Hogg's early-1970s photogrammetric aerial survey (published in
  *Archaeologia Cambrensis* 123 (1974), 43–53); a 2019 drone photogrammetric survey by Mark Walters
  for Bannau Brycheiniog National Park Authority; and, for Y Gaer Fach, a 2014 archaeological
  watching brief by Archaeology Wales Ltd (not a research dig). **This should be stated plainly in
  the game: Garn Goch has been surveyed, not dug.**
- **Purpose — explicitly unresolved:** Bannau Brycheiniog NPA's own text says the fort's purpose
  is uncertain — "defensive refuges, permanent trade centres, religious sites, or multifunctional
  settlements. No definitive answer is provided." A promotional site, garngoch.org (a Community
  Interest Company, not a heritage body), instead argues the whole monument is ~5,700 years old,
  essentially Neolithic rather than Iron Age, that "the stone lines were never walls," and that its
  purpose was chiefly religious/ceremonial (mass gatherings, markets, sacrifice) rather than
  defensive. **This directly contradicts Coflein, Cadw, Wikipedia and Bannau Brycheiniog NPA, all
  of which treat it as a genuine defended hillfort with earlier origins possibly incorporated —
  flag garngoch.org's claim explicitly as a fringe/promotional theory, not a documented fact, if
  used at all (e.g. as an "imagined/contested theory" aside).**
- **Who lived there:** Garn Goch sits on/near the conventional boundary between **Demetae**
  territory (SW Wales) and **Silures** territory (south Wales) — this is an inference from where
  the site sits on the accepted Roman-era tribal map, **not** from any excavated find at the site
  itself, and should be labelled reconstructed, not documented. One tourism source's claim that
  "several hundred people" lived at Y Gaer Fawr has no stated methodology — treat as a rough
  narrative guess only.
- **Geology (useful for terrain/material colour):** the hill is coarse **sandstone** (Ffairfach
  Grit Formation, Early Ordovician; lower slopes Abergwilli/Llandeilo Flags — sandstones and
  mudstones), mantled by glacial till from the Tywi valley glacier, within the Fforest Fawr
  Geopark. So rampart stone should read as grey-brown sandstone, not pale limestone.
- **Provenance:** documented (location, dimensions, entrance count, dating range, survey history)
  and reconstructed/contested (purpose, precise construction phasing, population).

### Roman forts, Dinefwr Park

- **Location:** inside the Dinefwr estate (Dinefwr Park & Castle, National Trust), Llandeilo.
- **Description:** Two overlapping Roman forts, found by ground radar/magnetometer survey in
  **2003**, excavated by **Cambria Archaeology in June–July 2005** (the dig was broadcast live by
  Channel 4's *Big Roman Dig*, 2–3 July 2005). Sources disagree on size and exact founding date:
  - Discover Carmarthenshire / Dylan Rees, *Carmarthenshire* (ISBN 9780708319499): earlier/larger
    fort **~8 acres**, capacity up to 2,000 men, "latter part of the 1st century AD"; smaller fort
    **~3.5 acres** with a vicus (civilian settlement) near its north-east entrance; both abandoned
    early 2nd century AD.
  - roman-britain.co.uk: calls them "**Dynevor A**" (vexillation fortress, ~12 acres / 4.85ha,
    trivallate, garrison up to ~3,000, possibly founded under governor Quintus Veranius, **AD
    57/58**) and "**Dynevor B**" (auxiliary fort, ~150m × 100m / ~3.75 acres / 1.54ha, possibly
    Agricolan, **c. AD 77/78**). Cites a 2003 StrataScan magnetometer survey, 1979 aerial
    photographs and the 2005 excavation. Also records a lost Roman milestone (RIB 2262, dedicated
    to Emperor Tacitus, AD 275/6) and finds of coins and amphora/olive-oil evidence.
  - **This is a genuine unresolved discrepancy** — both sources agree on the 2003 discovery
    method, the 2005 excavation, two overlapping forts and a vicus, but disagree on acreage (8 vs
    12 acres for the larger fort) and founding date (AD 57/58 vs late 70s). The primary excavation
    report ("The Llandeilo Roman Forts: Archaeological Investigations 2003–2007", Dyfed
    Archaeological Trust/heneb.org.uk) exists but could not be fetched in this pass (redirect
    loop) — it should resolve this and is worth chasing before finalising content.
  - No Iron Age hillfort predecessor is documented under the medieval castle hill itself: Wikipedia
    states plainly "no archaeological remains have been dated" to any pre-medieval fortification
    on the castle rock, and Coflein's own Dinefwr Park record does not mention an Iron Age hillfort
    at all (it only notes a speculative, unsubstantiated possible Roman temple under the nearby
    church). A tourism page (Discover Carmarthenshire) asserts "evidence of a prehistoric Iron Age
    hillfort within Dinefwr Park" with no location, size or citation given — **treat as
    single-source/unverified, likely a loose conflation of the wider historic estate with the
    (separately dated) Roman forts.**
- **Provenance:** documented (2003 discovery, 2005 excavation, existence of two forts, vicus) with
  an open discrepancy on size/date; the claim of an Iron Age hillfort here is unverified.
- **Sources:** [S11] [S12] [S13] [S14] [S15]

### Fan Camp, Llansadwrn

- **Welsh/English name:** Fan Camp. **Location:** Llansadwrn, ~4 miles from Llandeilo, grid ref
  SN674314, on a summit between the Afon Dulais and Afon Tywi.
- **Description:** Cadw Scheduled Monument (scheduled 7 June 1955, Legacy ID CM170), classified
  Iron Age hillfort (c. 800 BC – AD 74). Steep natural slopes defend the east side; the gentler
  west side is protected by earthworks — a scarp up to 3.4m high with traces of an inner bank, a
  simple in-turned entrance, and two lesser banks extending north and south.
- **Dating/excavation:** No excavation reported; the Iron Age classification is typological (based
  on form), not from stratigraphic dating or finds.
- **Provenance:** documented (existence, scheduling, form) via a Cadw-schedule-derived source;
  single-source in this research pass (not independently cross-checked against a second source),
  though the description reads as a close paraphrase of the official Cadw entry.
- **Sources:** [S19]

### Tir Mawr defended enclosure, near Llangadog

- **Location:** grid ref SN72102676, near Llangadog, within the 10-mile radius.
- **Description:** Circular, **bivallate** (double-banked) enclosure, internal diameter **~50m**,
  on a west-facing slope with a steep drop to a stream on its south side. Heavily ploughed out —
  only slight ditch/bank traces survive as a field mound. Classified as Iron Age/later prehistoric.
- **Provenance:** reconstructed/documented via a Megalithic Portal entry (which typically derives
  from Coflein/Cadw data, not independently cross-checked here) — single-source in this pass.
- **Sources:** [S20]

### Allt y Ferin, Llanegwad

- **Location:** grid ref SN 522 280 (Coflein NPRN 303983), Llanegwad, ~6–7 miles from Llandeilo,
  within the radius.
- **Description:** A genuinely **multi-period** site: a medieval motte-and-bailey castle built
  into the eastern angle of an earlier, larger **prehistoric promontory enclosure**. The motte has
  a narrow ditch and 2m counterscarp bank; the bailey is sub-rectangular, ~52m NE–SW; the rest of
  the wider enclosure is defined by scarps above steep natural slopes.
- **Dating:** the promontory enclosure's date is not pinned down as specifically Iron Age in the
  material reached here (it predates the medieval motte, "prehistoric" is as precise as this
  research could confirm) — worth fetching the live Coflein record directly to check further.
- **Provenance:** reconstructed (site form and multi-period reuse are recorded by Coflein); precise
  Iron Age dating not confirmed. Single-source in this pass.
- **Sources:** [S21]

### Grongar Hill, Llangathen

- **Location:** Llangathen, overlooking the Tywi valley, within the radius (made famous outside
  archaeology by John Dyer's 1726 poem "Grongar Hill" — not itself evidence for the Iron Age).
- **Description:** The hill's own name derives from Welsh **"gron gaer"** ("circular fort"),
  implying a contour hillfort here; a possible round barrow and a possible second hillfort are also
  noted in the Dyfed Archaeological Trust's Historic Landscape Character Area record for
  Llangathen. No size, dating or excavation detail was found in any source reached.
- **Provenance:** documented existence (via the place-name and a thin HER mention), but essentially
  **undocumented detail** — treat as "a hillfort is here" rather than anything more specific.
- **Sources:** [S18]

### Carreg Cennen

- **Location:** the castle crag above the Cennen valley, within the radius.
- **Description:** Human remains (two adults and a child) and a perforated horse tooth were found
  in stalagmite deposits in the cave system beneath the castle rock (c. 1907); four mid-4th-century
  Roman coins and a spindle whorl were found in debris below the cave mouth (mid-19th-century
  find). A possible Iron Age hillfort on the crag is explicitly caveated in the sources reached:
  "if there was an Iron Age hillfort at the site, all trace of it has been obliterated by
  subsequent [medieval castle] building."
- **Provenance:** the cave finds are documented; any Iron Age hillfort here is speculative and
  unconfirmed — single-source (Wikipedia, itself sourcing Coflein/Cadw; not independently
  cross-checked here).
- **Sources:** [S16]

### Dryslwyn

- **Location:** the castle hill above the Tywi, within the radius.
- **Description:** Multiple sources agree the naturally defensible hilltop *could* have been used
  earlier, but state plainly "no evidence has been found to support this theory." The first known
  fortification is the masonry castle, built by the rulers of Deheubarth in the 1220s.
- **Provenance:** this is an **absence-of-evidence finding** — cross-checked across several
  tourism/history sources reaching the same negative conclusion. Dryslwyn should not appear as an
  Iron Age site in the game.
- **Sources:** [S17]

### Just outside the 10-mile radius, worth a mention

- **Merlin's Hill / Bryn Myrddin**, Abergwili, near Carmarthen: a real, substantial Iron Age
  hillfort — single massive rampart, roughly triangular enclosure ~300m E–W by 180m, dated to
  c. 400 BC, described as one of the few large West Wales hillforts whose size implies territorial
  control, and possibly still active when Rome founded Moridunum (Carmarthen) nearby in AD 75.
  Straight-line distance from Llandeilo is roughly 11–12 miles (an exact figure could not be
  pinned down before this session's search quota ran out) — **likely just outside** a strict
  10-mile radius, but notable for its size, its legendary "Merlin" association (a visitor
  attraction), and its closeness to the Roman civitas capital. [S22]
- **Pen y Gaer**, near Llandovery/Cynghordy: an Iron Age hillfort (c. 800 BC – AD 74) overlooking
  Llandovery, semicircular rampart to the west, steep natural slopes elsewhere. Llandovery is
  ~11–12.4 miles from Llandeilo by road/straight line — **outside** the 10-mile radius. [S23]

## People & society

- **The Demetae**: the conventional name for the Iron Age/Romano-British people of Pembrokeshire
  and Carmarthenshire (i.e. this area), attested by the Roman geographer **Ptolemy**, who names two
  of their towns: Moridunum (Carmarthen) and Luentinum (identified with the Dolaucothi gold mines
  at Pumsaint). The name survived into the post-Roman **Kingdom of Dyfed** and the modern Dyfedeg
  Welsh dialect. **Cross-checked** across Wikipedia and roman-britain.co.uk. Etymology is debated:
  one reading ties it to Brythonic roots related to Welsh *defaid* (sheep) or *defod*
  (custom/wealth) — a sheep-herding people; other, weaker readings exist but are single-source and
  low confidence.
- **Genuine self-identity or Roman label?** Whether "Demetae" reflects how these communities
  actually saw themselves before Rome arrived, or is a Roman administrative label applied to a
  looser patchwork of farming groups (formalised as the *civitas Demetarum* from c. AD 75), is
  **not resolved** in the sources checked here — treat this as an open question the game can be
  honest about, rather than asserting tribal unity as settled fact.
- **Settlement pattern:** across Wales, Iron Age settlement runs on a spectrum from large hillforts
  down to small enclosed farmsteads and unenclosed hut groups, with **the enclosed farmstead as the
  dominant type** overall; regional variation is strong (the Marches favour large shared-culture
  hillforts; west and north Wales show denser small enclosures). Whether hillforts were high-status
  central places, refuges, or simply large farming settlements is **explicitly debated** among
  archaeologists — recent scholarship increasingly frames it as a continuum of enclosure sizes
  rather than a strict hierarchy. Cadw's own public leaflet describes an Iron Age Wales dominated
  by "a growing... preoccupation with defence," visible in large numbers of hillforts and defended
  farmsteads, practising "a successful mixed subsistence economy," with local warrior aristocracies
  who owned decorated weapons, jewellery and feasting equipment. Cadw names Montgomeryshire,
  Pembrokeshire and Gwynedd as areas nearly as densely settled by defended farmsteads as modern
  farms — **Carmarthenshire is not named**, so density here specifically is not directly evidenced.
- **Population:** **no reliable estimate was found** for Iron Age Wales, Demetae territory, or this
  specific area, despite deliberate searching — a genuine evidence gap, listed under Open questions
  rather than invented.
- **Provenance:** mixed — tribal name and its Roman-era attestation are documented; tribal
  self-identity, settlement density here specifically, hillfort function, and population are all
  reconstructed/debated/unknown.
- **Sources:** [S24] [S25] [S26] [S27] [S28]

## Homes (3D-ready details)

All of the following except where marked "Castell Henllys" or "St Fagans" is **general Iron Age
Britain reconstruction by analogy** — no excavated roundhouse dimensions were confirmed for the
Tywi valley or Llandeilo area specifically in this research (a genuine gap).

- **Shape/structure:** circular timber-framed roundhouse; **conical thatched roof**; walls of
  wattle-and-daub (hazel/willow wattle woven between upright posts, daubed with clay, straw and
  animal dung), turf, or drystone footings depending on region and available material.
- **Door orientation:** commonly a **single entrance facing east or south-east**, a widely observed
  British pattern (for morning light and shelter, and possibly solar/seasonal alignment — this
  specific interpretation is debated/sometimes seen as over-read in the literature, so present it
  as "commonly observed," not as a certain ritual fact).
- **Interior:** central hearth; roof structure typically had at least one internal ring of posts
  for larger examples.
- **Castell Henllys** (Pembrokeshire, run by Dyfed Archaeological Trust/the National Park; built
  from 1981, an ongoing experimental-archaeology programme; built directly into original excavated
  Iron Age post-holes — **the only UK site with roundhouses on their actual original footprint**):
  Roundhouse One = **10m diameter**; Roundhouse Two = **6m diameter**; Roundhouse Three ≈ same size
  as One but without an inner post-ring. Walls: wattle (hazel/willow) and daub (clay, straw, dung).
  Roof: thatched with river reed/Welsh reed. One rebuild used roughly 90 hazel bushes and 34 oak
  trees, giving a real sense of the timber demand a roundhouse represents. **Strong, well-attested
  visual reference — recommend using its documented dimensions and materials directly for the
  game's roundhouse model, labelled as "reconstructed (Castell Henllys, Pembrokeshire), not local."**
- **St Fagans (Amgueddfa Cymru) — Bryn Eryr farmstead**: **this reconstruction is of a real
  excavated farmstead near Llansadwrn on Anglesey, north Wales — NOT the Llansadwrn near Llandeilo
  (a naming trap hit during this research; the two parishes share a name).** Still a useful general
  SW-Wales-style visual reference, clearly labelled as north Wales, not local: two conjoined
  ("figure-of-eight") roundhouses; walls of **rammed clay 1.8m thick** (the first UK reconstruction
  to use this technique); roof thatched with home-grown spelt-wheat straw. Excavated 1985–87 by
  Gwynedd Archaeological Trust; reconstruction built 2015–2018+ by 1,000+ volunteers, funded by the
  Heritage Lottery Fund. Dating spans Iron Age (earliest, largest house) through to just before the
  Roman invasion (second house) and into the early medieval period (a third structure) — i.e. a
  genuinely long-lived farmstead, useful for showing continuity across the game's own era
  boundaries.
- **Provenance:** general construction details = reconstructed-by-analogy (Britain-wide); Castell
  Henllys and St Fagans dimensions/materials = documented (as reconstructions of real excavated
  evidence, though Bryn Eryr's evidence is north Wales, not local).
- **Sources:** [S27] [S28] [S30] [S31] [S32] [S33] [S34]

## Landscape

- **No pollen study specific to the Tywi valley or Llandeilo area was found** — a genuine gap.
  The nearest comparable evidence is from Pembrokeshire (also Demetae territory, useful by regional
  analogy only, **not Tywi-valley-specific**):
  - **Castell Henllys**: pollen record shows "a largely open woodland landscape with grazing" by
    the end of the Iron Age through the Romano-British period.
  - **Great Castle Head, Dale** (coastal promontory fort): "mainly open pastoral landscape, though
    with some cultivation and woodland."
  - **Pwll-y-hywaid**, near Whitland (close to the Carmarthenshire border, geographically the
    nearest of these to Llandeilo): woodland clearance in the earlier Iron Age, then a "marked
    expansion in activity" in the later Iron Age/Romano-British period.
  - General Wales-wide pattern (single synthesis source): the main woodland-clearance phase was the
    **mid-to-late Bronze Age**, with the Iron Age itself showing regional divergence — some areas
    kept clearing, others saw regeneration before renewed clearance — trending toward more open
    terrain overall by the end of the Iron Age/Romano-British period.
- **Geology at Garn Goch** (the one site with confirmed local detail): coarse sandstone (Ffairfach
  Grit Formation), giving grey-brown rock rather than pale limestone; glacial till from the Tywi
  valley glacier mantles the lower slopes.
- **Provenance:** reconstructed by regional analogy; single-source (one synthesis paper) for the
  Pembrokeshire pollen comparisons — should be cross-checked before treating as settled if the
  game leans heavily on "how wooded was it here" as a documented fact. Recommend depicting the
  Tywi valley as **a mixed, moderately open landscape of farmland and pasture with surviving
  woodland blocks**, explicitly labelled as inferred from nearby (not local) evidence.
- **Sources:** [S27] [S29]

## Farming & food

- **Crops:** barley; wheat — **emmer** (older) and **spelt** (introduced c. 500 BC, becoming the
  dominant Middle Iron Age crop alongside emmer; less-productive einkorn also continued); plus
  beans, peas and flax.
- **Livestock:** **cattle and sheep** as the principal stock (Cadw, Wales-specific), plus pigs,
  dogs, small horses and domestic fowl; all breeds smaller than their modern equivalents.
- **Processing/storage:** grain ground with **saddle querns** (two stones, grain rubbed between
  them by hand) — rotary querns (faster, two-stone, hand-rotated) emerge generally from c. 400 BC;
  bread/food baked in simple domed ovens inside the roundhouse; grain kept in storage pits and in
  raised **two- or four-post structures** (granaries or drying racks), found within the smaller
  defended farmstead enclosures across Wales (Cadw).
- **Provenance:** the Wales-specific crop/livestock/storage claims are sourced to a single but
  authoritative source (Cadw's own "Hillforts: Above and Below" leaflet); general
  quern/oven/processing detail is aggregated from general search synthesis rather than one named
  academic paper, and should ideally be re-verified against a named source (a Prehistoric Society
  roundhouse factsheet and a CPAT food-and-farming leaflet were located but not fully extracted in
  this pass — worth a follow-up fetch).
- **Sources:** [S27] [S38] [S39]

## Clothing (colours, materials)

**All general Iron Age Britain reconstruction by analogy — no Iron Age textile has survived
anywhere in Wales in the sources checked (Wales's acidic soil destroys organic remains), and no
Carmarthenshire find was identified.** State this limitation directly in the game rather than
implying local textile evidence exists.

- **Fibre and colour:** wool from fleeces in natural undyed colours — black, brown, grey, white —
  plus fabric dyed **red** and **blue**, sometimes woven into checked patterns (cross-checked
  across Cotswold Archaeology and the IK Foundation's Nordic Iron Age textile survey, both drawing
  on chemically-analysed excavated textiles elsewhere in Britain/Scandinavia). **Dyer's weed
  (Reseda luteola)** was the commonest source of yellow dye, confirmed by chemical analysis of
  surviving textiles (not from Wales specifically).
- **Tools of production:** triangular fired-clay (sometimes stone, on earlier sites) loom weights,
  bone weaving combs, spindle whorls (wood, bone, clay, stone or amber) on wooden drop spindles,
  bone needles — all standard, frequent finds on Iron Age British sites generally.
- **Garments:** reconstructed as wool tunics and cloaks, fastened with brooches — general Iron Age
  Britain convention, not a Carmarthenshire-specific attestation.
- **Provenance:** reconstructed-by-analogy throughout; recommend the ⓘ marker on every clothing
  depiction explicitly say "no Iron Age textile survives in Wales; based on finds elsewhere in
  Britain."
- **Sources:** [S35] [S36] [S37]

## Tools & trade

- **Farm tools:** iron **sickles** for harvesting; **ards** (simple wooden ploughs with an iron
  share) for turning soil; **rotary querns** (from c. 400 BC) for grinding grain — all standard,
  general-Britain reconstruction.
- **Local metalwork finds — a genuine evidence gap:** a Portable Antiquities Scheme (PAS)-derived
  academic summary states only **4 Roman-period objects in total** have been recorded from the
  whole of Carmarthenshire — **no Iron Age torc, brooch or weapon find from this county surfaced in
  this research.** This looks like a real gap in the local record, not merely an unsearched
  question; worth a direct follow-up on the PAS/finds.org.uk Carmarthenshire county page if the
  game wants to depict specific local metalwork.
- **Coinage — cross-checked, and an initial conflicting claim corrected:** **Amgueddfa Cymru
  (National Museum Wales)'s own coin fact sheet states plainly that Welsh tribes, the Demetae
  included, did not mint their own coins**, though neighbouring English tribes' coins are
  occasionally found in Wales. A metal-detecting-magazine claim that the Demetae minted coins at
  Moridunum was checked against this and **rejected as incorrect** — the museum source is judged
  authoritative. The general British pattern is that only south-eastern tribes issued coinage in
  the Late Pre-Roman Iron Age; west Wales, including this area, did not use coined money.
- **Trade networks:** well-evidenced cross-Channel trade with Armorica/Gaul exists for
  **Hengistbury Head, Dorset** (iron, silver and bronze traded for wine, tools and pottery; Caesar
  records the Veneti controlling this route) — **but no source found placing an equivalent trade
  network in west Wales specifically.** Treat any claim of long-distance trade reaching this area
  as unevidenced unless a local find (e.g. imported pottery or amphora) turns up in further
  research.
- **Local ore:** no iron or lead ore working dated to the **Iron Age** near Llandeilo was found.
  The well-documented **Dolaucothi gold mines** (Pumsaint, ~15 miles from Llandeilo, identified by
  Ptolemy with the Demetic town of Luentinum) are a **Roman-period** operation, not Iron Age — flag
  Dolaucothi for the game's Roman-arrival content rather than its Iron Age farming/trade content.
- **Provenance:** coinage claim documented (Amgueddfa Cymru); trade networks and local ore are
  reconstructed-by-analogy or explicitly absent-of-evidence for this specific area.
- **Sources:** [S38] [S39] [S40] [S41] [S42]

## Beliefs

- **Documented Welsh archaeology (not local to this area, but the best-evidenced Iron Age
  religious material anywhere in Wales):** **Llyn Cerrig Bach**, Anglesey — chariots, weapons,
  tools, slave chains and decorated metalwork deposited into the lake between 300 BC and AD 100,
  read by archaeologists as votive/gift offerings; the **Cerrig-y-Drudion bowl**, a La Tène
  decorated vessel interpreted as a ceremonial "crown." (Amgueddfa Cymru, "Ancient druids of
  Wales.")
- **Druids:** the image of druids as human-sacrificing priests comes specifically from **Tacitus**,
  describing the Roman assault on Anglesey — "groves consecrated to their savage cults," altars
  "slaked with captive blood." Amgueddfa Cymru's own article is explicit that this is a
  **Roman-authored account and is archaeologically unverified in Wales.** Present any druid content
  in the game as "what a Roman historian claimed," clearly separated from documented archaeology.
- **No Tywi valley or Llandeilo-area votive find was identified** — the general idea of rivers,
  springs and lakes as sacred places is a Wales/Britain-wide reconstruction, not evidenced at a
  specific local site.
- **Provenance:** the Llyn Cerrig Bach and Cerrig-y-Drudion evidence is documented but geographically
  distant (north Wales); local religious practice here is entirely reconstructed-by-analogy.
- **Sources:** [S43]

## Burial

- **Academic source, cross-checked:** Davis (2018), "Iron Age Burial in Wales: Patterns, Practices
  and Problems," *Oxford Journal of Archaeology* — a key finding is that Wales's very low count of
  known Iron Age burials is **not simply explained by the old "archaeologically invisible"
  excarnation/scattering theory**; it partly reflects poor preservation and research bias, though
  genuine diversity of burial practice is emerging from recent work. **Cremation dominates the 8th
  to 4th centuries BC.**
- **Nearest Carmarthenshire evidence (outside the 10-mile radius, but the closest documented local
  case):** scattered human metacarpal (hand) bones from **Coygan Camp**, near Laugharne, excavated
  by G. J. Wainwright (1963–65, published 1967, Cambrian Archaeological Association) — cited
  alongside similar excarnation-candidate evidence from Nash Point (Glamorgan) and Dinorben
  (Denbighshire).
- **No Iron Age burial site within the 10-mile Llandeilo radius was identified in this research** —
  a genuine gap; Coygan Camp is the nearest documented regional comparator, not a local one.
- **Provenance:** documented at the regional (Carmarthenshire/Wales) level; no burial evidence
  found specifically within the radius.
- **Sources:** [S44] [S45]

## Language

- **Common/Insular Brittonic** was spoken across Iron Age Britain, the ancestor language of modern
  Welsh, Cornish and Breton — general, well-established, cross-checked.
- **A specific naming point for the game:** "**Llandeilo**" itself is **not** an Iron Age name — it
  derives from **St Teilo**, a 6th-century (post-Roman, early medieval) saint, so there is no
  surviving Iron Age place-name for this exact spot. The wider region in this period, and for
  centuries after, was known as **Ystrad Tywi** ("the vale/valley of the Tywi").
- **Provenance:** documented (Brittonic as the spoken language of the period; the post-Roman origin
  of the name "Llandeilo"; "Ystrad Tywi" as the region's older name).
- **Sources:** [S46] [S47] [S48]

## Almanac facts

Short items for the almanac panel, each tagged with provenance and source.

- **Food** — Barley and wheat (emmer, and spelt from c. 500 BC) were the staple grains, with beans,
  peas and flax also grown; cattle and sheep were the main livestock. *Reconstructed (Wales-wide,
  Cadw).* [S27]
- **Food** — Grain was ground on saddle querns and, from around 400 BC, on faster rotary querns.
  *Reconstructed (general Britain).* [S38][S39]
- **Clothing** — Wool in natural black, brown, grey and white, plus dyed red and blue, sometimes in
  checked patterns; no Iron Age textile has survived anywhere in Wales to confirm this locally.
  *Reconstructed (general Britain/Scandinavia analogues).* [S35][S36]
- **Homes** — A roundhouse: timber frame, wattle-and-daub or stone-footed walls, conical thatched
  roof, one door usually facing east or south-east. *Reconstructed (general Britain; Castell
  Henllys gives real dimensions of 6–10m diameter).* [S30][S31]
- **Religion** — What's often called "druidic" belief comes mainly from Tacitus, a Roman writer
  describing the conquest of Anglesey — archaeologically unverified in Wales. Real votive offerings
  (weapons, chariots, tools) were placed in the lake at Llyn Cerrig Bach, Anglesey. *Documented
  (north Wales), reconstructed for local practice.* [S43]
- **Money/exchange** — The Demetae, like other western British tribes, did not mint or generally
  use coins; exchange was likely by barter and gift/tribute. *Documented (Amgueddfa Cymru).* [S40]
- **Health** — No specific local evidence found; general Iron Age Britain life expectancy and
  disease patterns are inferred from skeletal evidence elsewhere in Britain, not from any local
  find. *Open question for this area.*
- **Travel** — The Tywi valley and its tributaries were the natural routes through this landscape;
  hillforts and enclosures sit on hills overlooking these valleys and river crossings (Garn Goch,
  Fan Camp, Tir Mawr, Allt y Ferin all fit this pattern). *Reconstructed from site locations.*
  [S1][S19][S20][S21]
- **Population** — No reliable estimate exists for this area or for Demetae territory generally in
  the sources checked; treat as genuinely unknown rather than inventing a figure. *Open question.*
- **Homes/settlement** — Garn Goch has never been excavated; everything about its interior life is
  inferred from its surviving earthworks and two short surveys (1970s aerial photogrammetry, 2019
  drone survey), not from dug evidence. *Documented (absence of excavation).* [S1][S2]
- **Roman arrival** — A Roman fort was built at Carmarthen (Moridunum) around AD 75, and two
  overlapping Roman forts were found inside Dinefwr Park in 2003, only discovered by a ground
  survey and excavated in 2005. *Documented (with an open discrepancy on the Dinefwr forts' exact
  size/date).* [S14][S15]

## Open questions/contradictions

- **Dinefwr Iron Age hillfort claim**: asserted by one tourism page (Discover Carmarthenshire) with
  no location, size or citation, and not corroborated by Wikipedia or Coflein's own Dinefwr Park
  record. Recommend treating as unverified.
- **Demetae as genuine self-identity vs Roman label**: not resolved by the sources checked here —

- **No population estimate** exists for this area's Iron Age population in any source found.
- **No pollen study specific to the Tywi valley** exists in the sources found; landscape/forest

- **No confirmed excavated roundhouse** (with real dimensions) was found specifically for
