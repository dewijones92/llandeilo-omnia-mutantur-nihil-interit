# Music through time and places

Research for the agreed "music through time and places" work: what music was made and heard in and
around Llandeilo in each era, by whom and where, and how the app could play it honestly and legally.
Written 2026-10-02 from sources read in this session (brief: [`briefs/music-through-time.md`](briefs/music-through-time.md)).

Labels: **cross-checked** (two independent sources agree), **single-source**, **search-summary only**
(seen only in a search engine's summary, so a lead), **inference** (my reasoning, not a source's
claim), **OCR** (read from a scanned text layer, so a letter or number may be wrong). The "local link"
column uses three values: **documented** (a source places it in our 10-mile circle), **regional** (a
source places it in south-west Wales or Wales generally, not here), **analogy** (evidence from
elsewhere, used because nothing local survives). Distances from Llandeilo are my estimates from grid
references and are approximate (inference).

## Summary

- **Nothing musical survives from the Iron Age or Roman valley.** The nearest real instruments are a
  trumpet fragment from Llyn Cerrig Bach on Anglesey (150 BC to AD 50) [S35], the Deskford carnyx
  head from north-east Scotland (c. AD 80-200) [S34] and a lyre bridge from Skye (c. 300 BC) [S33].
  Any Iron Age or Roman sound in the app is analogy and must say so.
- **The medieval picture is unusually well documented, in general terms.** The laws of the Dimetian
  Code (the south-west Wales version of the Welsh laws) set out the household bard, the chief of song
  (pencerdd), "three legal harps" and their tuning keys [S3]; the Brut records the Lord Rhys's 1176
  Cardigan gathering with contests for "harpers, fiddlers, pipers" [S1]; Gerald of Wales names the
  three instruments as "the harp, the pipe, and the crwth" and describes part-singing [S4]. None of
  it names a tune played at Dinefwr.
- **Two genuinely local medieval links.** Gruffudd ap Nicolas, who farmed the lordship of Dinefwr in
  1439, judged the poets at the mid-15th-century Carmarthen eisteddfod, and "some maintain that it
  lasted three months at his cost at Dynevor" [S5]. His son Owain was "heir of Bryn y Beirdd" [S5],
  and Edward Jones in 1794 gives "Court Brynn y Beirdd, the Court-hill of the Bards, near
  Llandeilo-vawr" [S8], which Coflein records as a 16th-century-or-earlier house in Dyffryn Cennen
  [S7]. That these three are one place is inference from the name.
- **Robert ap Huw's manuscript (c.1613) is the only surviving source of the old harp music**, a
  north Wales compilation, not local [S12][S14]. Its reading is genuinely unsettled: Greenhill wrote
  in 1995 that "no set of precise definitions for all its symbols ... has yet been proposed" [S17].
  Playing from it is an accepted scholarly and performing practice, always as interpretation [S16][S17].
- **The Georgian evidence is good and local.** In 1791 Mary Morgan heard a "treble harp" at Tregib,
  near Llandeilo, borrowed from Golden Grove, "the most charming instrument that ever was played upon"
  [S19][S20]; in 1797 the Duke of Rutland heard a blind harper at the Bear Inn, Llandeilo [S20]; in
  1809 Fenton was guided by "old Llandebie Harper" Morris [S20]. Lord Dynevor subscribed to Edward
  Jones's 1794 harp book [S8].
- **Two local hymn writers**: Dafydd Jones of Caeo (1711-1777), drover and translator of Watts, buried
  at Crug-y-bar [S21]; and Thomas Lewis (1759-1842), the Talley blacksmith who wrote "Wrth gofio'i
  riddfannau'n yr ardd", printed 1823 [S22].
- **The Victorian town is documented in detail.** The Llandilo Harmonic Society first met in the
  Shire Hall in November 1857 [S25]. The Llandilo Eisteddfod of 1865 lists the choirs (Tabernacle
  Llandilo, Llandilo Church choir, Llansadwrn, Llansawel, Dryslwyn and others), the pieces ("Manheim",
  "Yr Haf", "Nashville", "St. Barnabas", "Worthy is the Lamb" with the Amen chorus, "Manchester",
  "Clychau Aberdyfi") and a fife-band contest [S26]. Bands played at the 1857 railway opening
  [effects:S7]; the 1850 church reopening had services but no recorded music [effects:S10].
- **Playable today**: many CC BY-SA 3.0 recordings released by Sain to Wikimedia Commons under a
  verified permission ticket [S36] (triple harp, crwth, hymns, a cymanfa ganu, brass band, and a song
  called "Ffair Llandeilo"), a public-domain 1899 "Ar Hyd y Nos" [S37] and a public-domain 1953
  "Worthy is the Lamb" [S38]. The composition's status must still be checked piece by piece.

Nothing found places any instrument, song or musical find at Garn Goch, the Dinefwr forts or

**Contradiction, the 1176 winner's father.** The 1860 Brut edition gives "son to Cibon the fiddler",
with "mab i Cibon Grythwr" in square brackets as an editor's insertion from a variant manuscript
[S1]. The Wikipedia article (citing a BBC blog) says "the son of Eilon the Crythwr" [S2]. The 1860
translation renders *crythwr* (crwth player) as "fiddler". Keep both names until a modern edition is
read.

**Correction to the medieval note.** `medieval:S16` and `medieval:S44` cite Wikipedia for the 1176
gathering and for Gerald on music. The primary texts are now read [S1][S4] and can replace them.

**Caution.** The laws are prescriptive texts in manuscripts later than Hywel Dda, and the 1841 text
is a 19th-century edition and translation [S3]. They say what a court should have, not what was
played at Dinefwr on a given day.

## Talley Abbey: Premonstratensian chant (c. 1185 to 1536)

| Music | Who | Setting | Local link | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The Office and Mass sung by the white canons in a chant described as "a variant on the 'Gregorian' chant of the Roman Rite", each hour ending "with an anthem sung in honour of the Blessed Virgin Mary" | Canons | Abbey church, Talley | Documented that Talley was Premonstratensian (`medieval` and `models` notes); the chant itself is regional at best | [S10] single-source (modern Order) |
| A 1130 statute required uniform books across the Order: "missal, gospel-book, epistle-book, collectar, gradual, antiphoner, hymnal, psalter, lectionary and calendar"; English visitation reports insist the chant be *secundum formam nostre religionis* | The Order | All houses | Regional (English houses) | [S9] single-source |
| Psalmody sung "viva voce ... sed non cum protractione dictionum" with a pause long enough to take breath (mid-13th-century customs) | Canons | Choir | Analogy (Prémontré's customs) | [S9] |

King is plain about the limit: "It is, however, impossible to determine the content of these early
rites, prayers and chants, as no manuscripts of the period have survived" [S9]. By the 17th century a
Belgian abbot could answer "quid sit cantus Premonstratensis nescimus" [S9]. **So no Talley melody can
be documented.** The honest choice is plainchant from a public-domain source, labelled as "the kind of
chant sung here; Talley's own books are lost", with a Marian anthem at the end of each hour as the
one Premonstratensian habit that is attested [S10].

**Correction to the brief.** The brief placed Lewys Glyn Cothi at Pumsaint. The Dictionary of Welsh
Biography says he took his name from the forest of Glyn Cothi and was born "perhaps at Pwllcynbyd in
the parish of Llanybydder" [S6]. It names Gruffudd ap Nicolas and Jasper Tudor among his patrons but
no Dinefwr or Rhys poem; a poem by him to a house inside the circle was not found.

**Contradiction, the eisteddfod's date.** DWB: "reports vary between 1451 and 1453" [S5]. Edward
Jones (1794, OCR, garbled): an eisteddfod at Carmarthen "about the year 1460" [S8]. The vicar of
Llandeilo, opening the 1865 Llandilo Eisteddfod, put it "in the reign of Edward IV, patronized by
Gruffydd ap Nicholas, an ancestor of the present Lord Dynevor" [S26], which would be 1461 or later,
after DWB's last record of Gruffudd. Keep "c. 1451-53, venue disputed".

These were poets who sang their verse, often with a harp, but no source read here says what the
harp played under a *cywydd*. Robert ap Huw (below) is the nearest evidence.

## The Robert ap Huw manuscript (compiled c. 1613)

| Music | Who | Setting | Local link | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| About 71 pages of harp music in tablature (British Library Add. MS 14905); composers placed "from c.1340 to c.1485"; copied partly from a book by the 16th-century harper Wiliam Penllyn; part of "a repertoire of over 300 pieces" catalogued in 16th-century manuscripts | Professional harpers (*cerdd dant*) | Court and gentry halls | Regional (Welsh bardic music; the compiler was from Anglesey) | [S11][S12] cross-checked (S12 is a lead) |
| 31 compositions in tablature plus exercises on the "twenty-four measures" | | | | [S12] (citing the *Welsh Academy Encyclopaedia of Wales*) |
| Played on a harp with "horsehair or low-tensioned gut strings" and bray pins (*gwrachïod*), in Pythagorean tuning; tunings named *is gywair*, *cras gywair*, *go gywair* | Modern players | | Analogy (reconstruction) | [S16] single-source |

**Is reconstruction accepted?** Yes, as interpretation, with open disagreement:

- It has a long scholarly literature: Dolmetsch (1937), Dart in the *Galpin Society Journal* (1968),
  Whittaker (1974, 2004), Ellis (1977, 1980), Greenhill (1976, a five-volume Bangor dissertation
  1995-2000), and a whole volume of *Welsh Music History* (vol. 3, 1999) with papers by Harper,
  Greenhill, Taylor, Whittaker and Robert Evans; Sally Harper's *Music in Welsh Culture before 1650*
  (Ashgate, 2007) [S14 bibliography]. The bibliography is single-source; the books themselves were
  not read.
- Greenhill (1995): the music's meaning "has remained, for the most part, elusive right through modern
  times:- no set of precise definitions for all its symbols and groupings of symbols has yet been
  proposed"; the chords "are organised on some system alien to the tonic-dominant principle" [S17].
- Bill Taylor frames his work as interpretation: with "replica instruments and using authentic
  fingernail technique, the modern player is well-equipped to interpret the music"; but "some of the
  patterns remain slightly ambiguous to us" and the manuscript has "several mistakes" [S16].
- The performers named as leading the revival are Bragod and Bill Taylor [S12]; Paul Dooley recorded
  pieces in Greenhill's arrangements [S17] and on his own CD [S14].

**Contradictions.** Dart's article is dated 1963 in the Wikipedia citation [S12] and 1968 in Lindahl's
bibliography [S14]; Crossref gives March 1968, vol. 21 [S13], so 1968 is right. IMSLP labels the
facsimile "Manuscript, 1567" [S15] against the usual c. 1613 for the compilation [S12]; the 1567 may
refer to a source date inside the manuscript, not checked.

**Contradiction, a date.** Freeman heads the Tregib entry "1795" [S20]; Morgan's book is *A Tour to

The 1865 report is the richest single source for how the district sounded: a church choir beating
the chapels at a glee, a Glamorgan choir winning the Handel, a speech urging the audience to "acquire
the English language without loss of time", and seats collapsing all morning to laughter [S26].

**Hymn-tune identities are not established.** "Manheim", "Nashville", "St. Barnabas" and "Manchester"
are tune names in the report [S26]. Which tunes these were, and their composers, was not checked
(hymnary.org blocked automated reading). Do not render them until identified.

**Crugybar**, a well-known Welsh hymn tune, carries the name of Crug-y-bar in Caeo. It was "first
published in 1883, appearing in Moliant Seion" (search-summary only, hymnary.org) [S29]; the National
Library holds Mansel Thomas's orchestral arrangement but says nothing of the tune's origin [S28]. A
place-name tune is not evidence the melody came from there.

**Not usable or not found.** No openly licensed recording of music from the Robert ap Huw manuscript
was found; Bill Taylor's, Paul Dooley's and Bragod's recordings are commercial [S14]. The 1865 hymn
tunes have no recordings checked. The Welsh Folk-Song Society journals were not found on the Internet
Archive in this pass.

**How the app could do it (proposal, for Dewi).** Where the period has notation (Robert ap Huw,
Edward Jones, Prys, 19th-century tune books), render it ourselves with sampled or synthesised
instruments, so both composition and recording are ours or public domain; where a CC recording fits
better than a synthesis (a cymanfa, a brass band, a triple harp), use it with attribution, and record
its SA or NC terms in the asset manifest. Every medieval piece is "reconstructed"; Iron Age and Roman
sound is "analogy".

## Open questions

1. Which tunes were "Manheim", "Nashville", "St. Barnabas" and "Manchester" in 1865, and are their
   composers out of copyright? [S26]
2. Which melody is sung today to Thomas Lewis's "Wrth gofio'i riddfannau'n yr ardd", and to Dafydd
   Jones's hymns? [S21][S22]
3. Is DWB's "Bryn y Beirdd" the Coflein Cwrt Bryn-y-Beirdd in Dyffryn Cennen? [S5][S7][S8]
4. Did any poet address a surviving poem to Dinefwr, Gruffudd ap Nicolas's household or the Rhys
   family, with an attested line we could quote? (The conversations note asked the same.)
5. 1176: was the winner "son to Cibon" or "son of Eilon"? A modern edition of the Brut (Thomas Jones)
   would settle it. [S1][S2]
6. Who wrote "Ffair Llandeilo" (Hergest), and is it a song about the town's fair? Where is Côr Telynau
   Tywi based? [S36]
7. Did any Llandeilo chapel hold a cymanfa ganu in the 19th century? Search Welsh Newspapers Online by
   decade (the site's facet links could not be driven from the command line in this pass). [S27]
8. When were the Llandilo brass, volunteer and town bands founded? Only 1890s-1910s mentions were
   listed, by relevance, not date. [S27]
9. Is there any evidence for music at Dinefwr or Newton House in the 18th and 19th centuries beyond
   Lord Dynevor's 1794 subscription? [S8]
10. Where is a facsimile of Prys's *Salmau Cân* (1621), and which of its tunes are still known? [S18]
