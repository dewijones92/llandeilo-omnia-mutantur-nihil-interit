# Invasions, raids and conquests that reached the Tywi valley

Research for the owner's request "norman, viking etc invasions", written 2026-10-02 from sources read
that day. The brief is in [`briefs/invasions-and-raids.md`](briefs/invasions-and-raids.md). WebSearch
was used 5 times; the chronicles and J. E. Lloyd's *History of Wales* were downloaded in full from
archive.org and searched as text, and the Annales Cambriae B text for 1257 was read from page images.

Independently checked 2026-10-02 (see [reviews/invasions-and-raids-check-2026-10-02.md](reviews/invasions-and-raids-check-2026-10-02.md)); corrections applied.

Distances are measured from the app's centre (SN 6290 2225, `src/domain/geo.ts`); "inside" means
within 16.1km (10 miles). Where a place has no Coflein or report grid reference, the distance is from
an approximate position and says so.

## Summary

- **No Viking raid is recorded inside the area.** Every Norse raid on south-west Wales in the
  chronicles read falls on St Davids, Llanbadarn, St Dogmaels, Llanilltud, Llancarfan or "Dyfed" in
  general. Llandeilo Fawr was an important church, the kind of place raiders sought out [S4] (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*:
  was [S5]), though Lloyd frames these as coastal raids ("no part of the coast was wholly secure"),
  which weakens the inference for an inland church; no chronicle read names it. The nearest Viking-age fights are at Pwll Dyfach (1042, near Carmarthen) and
  Aber Tywi (1044, the Tywi estuary), both outside. [S1][S3][S4][S5]
- **No Ogham stone lies inside the area.** The Carmarthenshire Ogham stones are all far from the area (the nearest, Llangeler, is about
  27km away and north of Carmarthen, not west; *Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*);
  the nearest to the east are at Llywel and Trecastle in Breconshire. A "Llandeilo" stone with Ogham
  is at Llandeilo near Maenclochog in **Pembrokeshire**, a different place. [S8][S4]
- **The Normans are documented in Ystrad Tywi, but no Norman castle is recorded inside the area** in
  1093-1116. The castles were Rhyd y Gors (a ford a mile south of Carmarthen, from 1093), then
  Carmarthen (by 1109), and Llandovery in Cantref Bychan (before 1116). Cantref Mawr, Llandeilo's
  cantref, "enjoyed during this period a greater amount of freedom than any other portion of South
  Wales" (Lloyd). [S5][S1]
- **The "French" devastation of Ystrad Tywi** is in both the Brut and the Annales Cambriae B text,
  with Dyfed, Ceredigion and Ystrad Tywi "deserte manent" (left deserted). The Rolls Brut files it
  under 1093; Lloyd dates it to 1095. Inside the area by region, but unlocated. [S1][S3][S5]
- **The strongest new key date is 1257.** A royal army under Stephen Bauzan marched from Carmarthen,
  reached Llandeilo Fawr and **camped there** ("usque ad llanthelou uaur peruenerunt: ibique sine
  aliquo timore pernoctantes"), was harried from the woods for a day, lost its baggage at a wood, and
  was destroyed at "kemereu" (Cymerau) about midday on Saturday 2 June 1257; Bauzan himself was
  killed (Lloyd; Matthew Paris) [S5][S17]. The camp at Llandeilo is documented in Annales Cambriae B
  alone (Lloyd follows it); the Brut has the army go to Dinefwr. *Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: was "cross-checked across
  Annales Cambriae B and the Brut". The march, the Welsh victory and the heavy losses are
  cross-checked. Both battle sites are inside the area on every
  proposed location, but **where Cymerau was is contested**, and the wood's name may itself be a
  misreading. [S3][S1][S5][S7][S15][S16]
- **1116, Gruffudd ap Rhys**: Narberth, Llandovery, Swansea and Carmarthen were attacked, all outside
  the area (Llandovery is about 11 miles). He retreated into "the impenetrable woods of the Great
  Cantref" (Lloyd), which is the area, but unlocated. Documented nearby, news here. [S1][S5]
- **1213**: a royal-backed army led by young Rhys (Rhys Ieuanc), with Fawkes of Bréauté, seneschal of
  Cardiff, and Owain ap Gruffudd (Lloyd adds Engelard of Cigogné), camped at Trallwng Elgan near Talley,
  inside the area, and defeated Rhys Gryg "not far from Llandeilo" (Lloyd); Rhys Gryg burned Llandeilo
  himself and young Rhys took Dinefwr. Documented here. (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: was "under Fawkes".)
  [S1][S5][S20]
- **Civil War, 1642-48**: no fighting is recorded in the area in anything read. Golden Grove was the
  seat of the Royalist commander, the 2nd Earl of Carbery, and Jeremy Taylor "took refuge at Golden
  Grove". Documented nearby, news here. [S10][S11]
- **Fishguard, 1797**: no source read says anything about the news reaching Llandeilo. Not supportable
  as a local event. [S12][S13]

## A warning about chronicle years

The Rolls Series Brut [S1] heads its entries with years that often run one or two years behind the
true date in this period (it files Rhys ap Tewdwr's death, 1093, under 1091, and Aber Tywi, 1044,
under 1042). The 1860 Annales Cambriae [S2] adds editorial years in the margin, some lost in the
scan. Gough-Cooper's B text [S3] deliberately uses neutral tags, not years, and gives external dates
in footnotes. So every date below is given as **Lloyd's corrected year** [S4][S5] where he gives one,
with the Rolls Brut year beside it, and a date from the Brut alone is marked "Rolls year". Jones's
modern translations of the Peniarth 20 and Red Book Bruts were **not read** in this pass; the Rolls
edition prints variant readings from several manuscripts (its A to E), which covers part of the
"versions" question but not all. The Bruts and the Annales are related texts from a common Latin
tradition, so agreement between them is not fully independent; agreement with an English chronicle
(Asser, the Oseney annals) is.

## 1. Irish settlement in Dyfed, 4th to 6th centuries

- **The tradition.** *The Expulsion of the Déisi*, an Irish text that "dates approximately to the 8th
  century, but survives only in manuscripts of a much later date", has Eochaid son of Artchorp settle
  in "Demed" (Dyfed), and derives the kings of Dyfed from him; the Harleian genealogies give the same
  names back to Triphun but a different, Roman, pedigree above him; some scholars, such as Eoin
  MacNeill, "regard it as late fiction" [S9] (Wikipedia lead, not checked against its sources; *Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*:
  the same article later calls MacNeill one "who treats the literary accounts as largely historical",
  so the attribution contradicts itself). Lloyd
  (1911) records that some south Welsh Goidels bore the tribal name of the Uí Liatháin, and that "in
  the eighth century Tewdos ap Rhain, king of Dyfed, was claimed by the Deisi of our county Waterford
  as a descendant of one of their chieftains" [S4]. **Single-source each; the two agree on the
  Dyfed dynasty claim.**
- **Ogham stones.** The Carmarthenshire stones with Ogham listed by BabelStone (from the Celtic
  Inscribed Stones Project's sigla) are Castell Dwyran (Voteporix), Eglwys Cymyn, Llandawke, Llangeler,
  Llanwinio, and two doubtful (St Ishmael, Henllan Amgoed) [S8]. **All are far from the area** (Llangeler is north of Carmarthen rather
  than west; *Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*). The
  nearest to the east are Aberhydfer and Pentre Poeth near Trecastle, Breconshire [S8]. Lloyd notes that
  "few Latin inscriptions of this period have been discovered between the Towy and the Tawe" [S4].
  **Single-source list** (the CISP database itself returned a bot challenge and could not be read).
- **Trap:** BabelStone's "Llandeilo Stone" (LDEIL/1, ANDAGELLI) is at "St Teilo's Church, Llandeilo
  (Llandilo), Maenclochog, Pembrokeshire" [S8], not Llandeilo Fawr.
- **Lloyd's own picture is dated.** He thought Brythonic settlers "pressed up the vale of the Irfon
  into the region of Llandovery, Llandeilo and Llanelly", edging out the Goidels [S4]. That is his
  conjecture ("it is only possible to conjecture"), not evidence, and a 1911 view.
- **Inside the area?** No evidence of Irish settlement inside the area was found. **Not supportable
  here.** The Ogham "stronghold is the region of Dyfed" [S4].

- **Aber Tywi, 1044: Irish or Norse?** The Brut says "a fleet of the people of Ireland" and "the Irish"
  [S1]; Annales Cambriae B says "accepta classe gentilium intrat hostium tewy", taking a fleet of
  *gentiles* (heathens, the annals' word for Vikings), he entered the mouth of the Tywi [S3]. Side by
  side, not resolved: probably a Hiberno-Norse fleet from Ireland, but that reading is ours.
- **Llandeilo itself: nothing.** Neither the Rolls Brut nor either Annales Cambriae text read mentions
  Llandeilo or Teilo in any raid (whole-text search of [S1][S2][S3]). Lloyd says the raiders "were
  chiefly attracted by the plunder of the monasteries", and that the places raided "were all the sites
  of important churches" [S4], which Llandeilo was; that makes a raid **possible, not recorded**.
- **The Lichfield Gospels.** How the book left Llandeilo for Lichfield (there by the late 10th
  century) is unknown [medieval:S7]. A local page offers "a likely guess" of tribute to a Saxon king
  [medieval:S1]. **No source read connects its move to a Viking raid.** Not supportable.
- **Hywel Dda and the Norse.** Nothing in the passages read records a Norse raid on Ystrad Tywi in his
  time. Not searched further; an open question.
- **Inside the area?** **No Viking raid is recorded inside the area.** The scene could show news
  arriving (from St Davids, or the Tywi estuary in 1044), labelled as news.

## 3. Anglo-Saxon and Mercian incursions

- **778 and 784, Offa.** Annales Cambriae B: "vastacio britonum dextralium apud offa" and "vastacio
  britonum cum offa in estate" (devastation of the southern Britons by Offa; with Offa, in summer) [S3].
  Lloyd dates them 778 and 784 and calls them raids "made upon Welsh territory" [S4]. "Dextrales"
  means the south (Deheubarth's root), not Ystrad Tywi in particular. **Unlocated.**
- **895, Anarawd with the English.** Annales Cambriae: "Anaraut cum anglis uenit vastare ceredigeaun
  et stratewy" (Anarawd came with the English to lay waste Ceredigion and Ystrad Tywi) [S3]; the 1860
  edition prints "Strattui" and notes MS C's "Anaraud cum Saxonibus vastavit Keredigiaun" [S2]; the
  Rolls Brut has "Anarawd came to devastate Ceredigion and the Vale of Tywi", without the English [S1].
  Lloyd: Anarawd "had at any rate English troops at his command" when he "plundered Ceredigion and
  Ystrad Tywi, a blow directed most probably at his brother Cadell" [S4]. Asser explains the
  background: Anarawd had left his alliance with the Northumbrians and submitted to Alfred [S6].
  **Cross-checked** (AC, Brut, Lloyd); **the English are in AC only.** This is the only Anglo-Saxon
  force recorded in Ystrad Tywi in what was read, and it came as a Welsh king's ally.
- **983, Ælfhere.** Lloyd: Ælfhere of Mercia "acting with Hywel ab Ieuaf of Gwynedd in an attack upon
  Einon ab Owain of Deheubarth, which the latter repelled with much slaughter" [S4]; the Brut has
  "Brecheiniog, and all the territory of Einon ... devastated by the Saxons, Alvryd being their leader"
  [S1]. **Unlocated** within Einon's lands.
- **992, Edwin ab Einon with Æthelsige.** The Brut: Edwin "with Edylfi (or Eclis) the Great, a Saxon
  prince from the seas of the South, devastated all the kingdoms of Maredudd, to wit, Dyved, and
  Ceredigion, and Gower, and Cydweli" and St Davids a third time [S1]; AC "duce Edelisi Anglico" [S2];
  Lloyd names Æthelsige but says "it is not easy to say who the
  Æthelsige was of the expedition of 992" [S4] (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: was "Lloyd identifies Æthelsige"). **Ystrad Tywi is not named.**
- **1012, Eadric Streona.** "Menevia was devastated by the Saxons, to wit, by Entris and Ubis" [S1];
  Lloyd reads Eadric Streona [S4]. St Davids, outside.

## 4. The Normans, 1093 to 1116

- **1093.** Rhys ap Tewdwr killed near Brecon (already the key date `rhys-ap-tewdwr`). Annales Cambriae
  B: "Circiter kalendas Iulii franci primitus demeciam et keredigean tenuerunt et castella in eis
  locauerunt et abinde totam terram britonum occupauerunt" (about 1 July the French first held Dyfed
  and Ceredigion, put castles in them, and from there occupied the whole land of the Britons) [S3].
  Lloyd: William fitz Baldwin, sheriff of Devon, "was commissioned by Rufus to plant another Norman
  post, which he established at Rhydygors, a ford on the Towy a mile south of the old Roman fort of
  Carmarthen" [S5]. **Rhyd y Gors is outside the area** (about 13 miles, from an approximate position; no grid
  reference found).
- **1094.** The Britons "demolished all the castles of Ceredigion and Dyved, except two, to wit,
  Pembroke and Rhyd y Gors. And the people and all the cattle of Dyved they brought away with them,
  leaving Dyved and Ceredigion a desert" (Rolls year 1092) [S1]; Lloyd agrees on Pembroke and
  Rhydygors [S5]. **Cross-checked.**
- **1095.** "The French devastated Gower, Cydweli, and the Vale of Tywi; and the countries remained a
  desert" (Rolls year 1093) [S1]; AC B: "franci deuastauerunt goher et kedweli et stratewi; demecia
  ceretica et stratewi deserte manent" [S3]. Lloyd: from Glamorgan and Brecknock "attacks were directed
  upon Gower, Kidwelly and Ystrad Tywi" [S5]. **Cross-checked; unlocated within Ystrad Tywi.** The
  event-effects note already records the Brut line under its Rolls year [effects:S13]; Lloyd's date is
  1095.
- **1096.** "William, son of Baldwin, died, who founded the castle of Rhyd y Gors, by the command of the
  king of England. And after his death the custodians left the castle empty" (Rolls year 1094) [S1];
  Lloyd: "the last except Pembroke in this region" [S5]. **Cross-checked.**
- **1102.** Henry I gave "the Vale of Tywi, Cydweli, and Gower" to Hywel ap Goronwy (Rolls year 1100)
  [S1]. (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: the Rolls translation as printed reads "he granted to Howel and Goronwy"; the
  1102-03 entries on Hywel son of Goronwy support the reading here.) **Single-source** (Lloyd refers to Hywel holding Gower, without the grant year in the passage
  read).
- **1105-1106.** "Rickart, son of Baldwin, stored the castle of Rhyd y Gors; and Howel, son of Goronwy,
  was driven from his dominion, the man to whom king Henry had deputed the conservancy of the Vale of
  Tywi and Rhyd y Gors"; Hywel then burned houses and killed "many of the French"; the next year he was
  "killed, through treachery, by the French, who had the custody of Rhyd y Gors" (Rolls years 1102 and
  1103) [S1]. Lloyd: "the castle of Rhydygors and the claims of Richard fitz Baldwin disappear together
  in the year 1106", and by 1109 "the local fortress is Carmarthen" [S5]. **Cross-checked.**
- **Cantref Mawr under Henry I.** Lloyd: it "enjoyed during this period a greater amount of freedom
  than any other portion of South Wales. Its wooded glens and solitary moorlands alone gave scope ...
  for the development ... of a life moulded in accordance with Welsh ideals"; Henry let Rhydderch
  ap Tewdwr hold land there and gave a portion to Owain ap Caradog to keep him in check [S5]. In the Pipe Roll of 1130, as Lloyd quotes it, "Homines
  de Cat (for Cantre) maur debent xl. s. pro homine Episcopi saresburiensis quem occiderunt" (the men of Cantref
  Mawr owe 40 shillings for a man of the bishop of Salisbury whom they killed) [S5]. **Single-source
  (Lloyd quoting the Pipe Roll).** *Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: "Cantre maur" is Lloyd's emendation of the roll's
  "Cat maur"; he adds that the bishop of Salisbury was lord of Kidwelly, divided from Cantref Mawr by
  the Tywi at Abergwili [S5]. Llandeilo's commote of Maenordeilo was in Cantref Mawr
  [medieval:S38].
- **No Norman castle inside the area is recorded** in anything read for 1093-1116. Llandovery castle,
  Richard fitz Pons's in Cantref Bychan, is about 11 miles off (approximate position) [S5].
- **1116, Gruffudd ap Rhys.** The Brut (Rolls year 1113): he "made an attack ... upon the castle that
  was near Arberth, and burned it"; then Llandovery, the castle of "Rickert, son of Ponson ... to whom
  king Henry had given Cantrev Bychan", where the garrison under Maredudd ap Rhydderch held, though
  "the outwork of the castle ... he burned"; then a castle "near Abertawy" of Henry Beaumont [S1].
  Lloyd: Narberth destroyed; Llandovery "only the outworks were taken, the keep remaining intact";
  Swansea the same; at Carmarthen, held by local chiefs in fortnightly turns, Owain ap Caradog fell
  and Gruffudd "was able to set fire to the town"; then Ceredigion and failure at Aberystwyth, after
  which "he found shelter once again in the impenetrable woods of the Great Cantref" [S5].
  **Cross-checked.** One difference: the Welsh text has "gwedy llosgi y rac castell" (after burning the
  outer castle) at Carmarthen [S1] where Lloyd has "the town" [S5]. The earlier note found no "1116
  Battle of Llandeilo Fawr" [timeline:S33]; this pass agrees: **none of the 1116 attacks was inside the
  area.**

## 5. Later invasions and campaigns

- **1158-1163, Henry II's campaigns.** Walter Clifford "carried a booty out of the territory of Rhys
  ... for the castle of Llanymddyvri was his property" [S1]. Lloyd: in 1159 five earls "marched
  together to hunt Rhys out of his lair" at "his castle of Dinweiler" in Cantref Mawr, and "parted
  company without having effected anything"; in 1162 Rhys "attacked and took the castle of
  Llandovery"; in 1163 Henry "passed through Carmarthen, and, crossing the defiles of the Gwili at last
  came upon his foe at Pencader" [S5][S1]. Dinweiler is **not located** in anything read. Pencader is
  outside (about 14 miles, approximate).
- **1213, a royal-backed army near Llandeilo.** The Brut (Rolls year 1213): young Rhys (Rhys Ieuanc),
  backed by the king, led it (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: not Fawkes); he "collected a vast army out of Brecheiniog, and came in a hostile manner to the Vale of Tywi, and

## The 1257 campaign in detail (Coed Llathen and Cymerau)

The fullest account is Annales Cambriae B (National Archives MS E164/1), read from Gough-Cooper's
transcription, pp. 82-83 [S3]:

- "die mercurii proxima post pentecosten" (the Wednesday after Pentecost, 30 May 1257) Stephen Bauzan
  "et multi barones cum multitudine forcium bellatorum apud kermerdin pernoctauerunt" (spent the night
  at Carmarthen).
- Next day, "cum pluribus equis loricatis" (with many armoured horses), "ad deuastandam terram de
  stratewy iter arripuerunt et non sine impedimento usque ad llanthelou uaur peruenerunt: ibique sine
  aliquo timore pernoctantes" (they set out to lay waste the land of Ystrad Tywi, and not without
  hindrance reached Llandeilo Fawr, and camped there without any fear).
- Maredudd ap Rhys Gryg and Maredudd ab Owain, with the Welsh of Ceredigion and Ystrad Tywi, gathered
  "in siluis et in nemoribus et in conuallibus circa anglicos cum magnis clamoribus" (in the woods and
  groves and valleys around the English, with great shouting), and "per totam diem veneris telis
  armorum et sagittarum" harried them all Friday with darts and arrows.
- "die sabbati, videlicet in uigilia sancte trinitatis, scilicet iiii Nonas Iunii" (Saturday, the eve
  of Trinity, 2 June), their guide Rhys Fychan ("Res filius Resi michil") left them "et ad castrum suum
  scilicet dinouour cum paucis de suis ... occulte fugit" (secretly fled to his castle, Dinefwr, with a
  few of his men).
- The knights, "uersus kardigaun audacter iter aripientes" (boldly setting out towards Cardigan), were
  attacked from the woods "a prima hora diei usque ad meridiem" (from the first hour to midday); "apud
  coeth llatheu" they lost "omnia uictualia, omnes caballos, arma et necessaria" and all their palfreys.
- "Circa horam uero meridiei ad kemereu pugnando peruenientes" (about midday, fighting, they reached
  Cymerau), the Welsh fell on them; "plus quam tria milia saxonum in illa die occubuerunt" (more than
  three thousand "Saxons" fell that day), and "pauci uero aut nulli" of the armed knights escaped.
- A further entry: on Trinity Sunday "apud goeriam" (Gower) 194 men and six women were killed.

**The Brut** (Rolls year 1256): Rhys Fychan "collected vast aid ... of the barons and knights from
England, and came to Caermarthen. And from thence, in Whitsun week, he took his course to Dinevwr; and
when he had entered the castle, the garrison seized him; and then they ... slew upwards of two thousand
of the army" [S1]. Lloyd notes that the Rolls editor's "mutual engagement" is a mistranslation of the
place name Cymerau [S5].

**Lloyd** [S5] follows AC B: set out from Carmarthen on Thursday 31 May; the hills round Dinefwr held by
the Welsh; harassed on Friday; on Saturday Rhys changed sides and fled into Dinefwr; the baggage and
sumpter horses lost, then "about midday, at a place called Cymerau, a general attack" overwhelmed the
expedition. "No such disaster as this had befallen the royal authority in South Wales for a
generation." Lloyd also says Bauzan's force was "considerable" but gives no number.

**Cross-checked**: the march from Carmarthen, the Welsh victory and the heavy losses (AC B, the Brut,
Lloyd, and per the battlefield report Matthew Paris and the Osney and Tewkesbury annals [S7]). **AC B
alone** gives the camp at Llandeilo, the day-by-day sequence, "coeth llatheu" and "kemereu" (Lloyd
follows AC B, so he is not independent).

**Bauzan was killed.** Lloyd: "the expedition, with its leader, was overwhelmed" [S5]. Matthew Paris
lists among the fallen "dominus Stephanus Baucan, regi carissimus" [S17]; he also places the disaster
in a narrow, marshy place near a castle and tells of a betrayal, an independent English account that
fits Dinefwr without naming it (as summarised by the independent check) [S17]. Wikipedia's *Battle of
Cadfan* and *Stephen Bauzan* agree (leads) [S14][S18]. *Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*.

**Who died.** The chronicles' "Saxons" is the annalist's word. Chapman (quoted in [S7]) and both
Coflein records [S15][S16] say the force was "an English-led force of knights and predominately Welsh
infantry drawn from the Marcher lordships", with heavy losses "particularly among the predominantly
Welsh infantry". Wikipedia's *Battle of Cadfan* says instead the army was "made up mainly of soldiers
from England" with "some Welsh soldiers" [S14]; side by side, Chapman and Coflein preferred. So the
dead must never become "3,000 English killed". *Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*.

**The wood's name is itself in doubt.** The MS reads "coeth llatheu", and Gough-Cooper's footnote

later name built on the misreading. This is an editor's suggestion, **single-source**, and is not
discussed in the battlefield report [S7]. *Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: Coflein, quoting Remfry's translation "at the wood

earlier "about 6 miles west" (Llanarthne is about 9.7km) was this note's own inference, not a
source's, so it is dropped. Contested; both readings must stay visible.
