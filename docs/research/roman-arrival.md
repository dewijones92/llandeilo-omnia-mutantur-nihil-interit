# The Roman army arrives in the Tywi valley, c. AD 74

Research for a cutscene of the Roman army reaching Llandeilo and building the first fort in what is
now Dinefwr Park. Written 2026-10-02 from sources fetched that day; the brief is in
[`briefs/roman-arrival.md`](briefs/roman-arrival.md). WebSearch was used 10 times; everything else
came from direct fetches (Coflein record pages and its server-side search, Perseus, The Latin
Library, Lacus Curtius, Heneb's PDF of the excavation report). Each Coflein record was read in full.
Distances are measured from the map centre in `src/domain/geo.ts` (E 262900, N 222500) or from Fort 1
(Coflein SN 62187 22534), using the grid references quoted.

It builds on [`era-iron-age.md`](era-iron-age.md) (the forts, the Demetae, Garn Goch) and its
independent check [`reviews/era-iron-age-check-2026-10-02.md`](reviews/era-iron-age-check-2026-10-02.md),
and on the forts section of [`conversations-by-class.md`](conversations-by-class.md).

Independently checked 2026-10-02 (see [reviews/roman-arrival-check-2026-10-02.md](reviews/roman-arrival-check-2026-10-02.md)); corrections applied.

## Summary

- **No source describes the Roman arrival at Llandeilo.** Tacitus gives one sentence to Frontinus
  (he "subdued by his arms the powerful and warlike tribe of the Silures") and never names the
  Demetae. Everything about this valley's conquest is inferred from archaeology: the forts, the road
  and the marching camps east of Llandovery.
- **The date is a range.** The excavator puts Fort 1 "perhaps soon after AD 74", with "an earlier
  date ... [not] ruled out" [S1]; Coflein says "during the AD 70s" [S2]. There is **no
  dendrochronology or radiocarbon date**: the dating rests on a small samian and coin sample [S1].
  Label it "c. AD 74 to 77, perhaps soon after 74". *Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: this need not be the *first* Roman
  arrival in the valley. Heneb dates the Llandovery fort "probably in the AD 50s" [S64], against
  Coflein's 70s [S14], so an earlier arrival in the upper valley is a live reading.
- **The most plausible approach is from the north-east, down the Tywi from Llandovery**, after
  crossing from Brecon Gaer past the Y Pigwn marching camps. This is **an inference**: no source says
  which way the builders of Dinefwr came. Coming up from Carmarthen is the main alternative.
- **The force is unknown by name.** No inscription names any unit at Dinefwr. Fort 1's size implies
  "at the very least an *ala quingenaria* ... or a *cohors milliaria* ... and perhaps even a larger
  legionary detachment" [S1]; Coflein says "more than a thousand, drawn from several different
  units" [S2].
- **The column, the camp and the trumpets can be shown honestly by analogy**: Josephus describes a
  Roman army on the march in AD 67 in exact order [S45], and how it builds and breaks camp [S46-S49].
  These are documented elsewhere, not here.
- **What the locals did is unknown.** No source records resistance or welcome in Demetae territory.
  The idea that south-west Wales was barely garrisoned (Jarrett 1969) is, in the excavator's words,
  refuted by the Llandeilo forts [S1].

## The campaign: what the sources say

### What Tacitus actually says

- **Frontinus.** *Agricola* 17: "Julius Frontinus was equal to the burden, a great man as far as
  greatness was then possible, who subdued by his arms the powerful and warlike tribe of the Silures,
  surmounting the difficulties of the country as well as the valour of the enemy" (*validamque et
  pugnacem Silurum gentem armis subegit*) [S3][S4]. That is the whole record. Dahm calls it "the
  only solid evidence for the achievements of Frontinus' tenure as governor" [S10].
- **Agricola's arrival.** *Agricola* 18: Agricola crossed "about midsummer" (*media iam aestate*),
  found the Ordovices had "destroyed nearly the whole of a squadron of allied cavalry quartered in
  their territory", and attacked them "though summer was past" (*quamquam transvecta aestas*) with
  "a force of veterans and a small body of auxiliaries" [S3][S5]. This is north Wales, not here.
- **The Silures before Frontinus.** *Annals* XII.39: the Silures were changed "neither by severity
  nor by clemency" (*non atrocitate, non clementia mutabatur*) and had to be held down by legionary
  camps [S7]. *Annals* XIV.29: Veranius "ravaged the Silures with modest raids" (*modicis
  excursibus Siluras populatus*) before he died [S8]. *Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: the passage is the annal for AD 61
  and gives no date for his governorship; the AD 57-58 date needs its own source (the only one read
  is roman-britain.co.uk, "during A.D. 57/58" [S67], an enthusiast site).
- **The Demetae are not named by Tacitus.** A text search of *Agricola*, *Annals* XII and *Annals*
  XIV finds "Silures" several times and "Demetae" never [S3][S7][S8]. Wikipedia makes the same
  observation [S59]. Their name comes from Ptolemy, who lists them "more toward the west" with two
  towns, Luentinum and Maridunum [S9].

### What is inference

- **That Frontinus's army built the Dinefwr fort.** Dahm reports Jones and Mattingly arguing
  Frontinus "carried out major campaigns of subjugation of all the major Welsh tribes; the Silures,
  Demetae, Ordovices and Deceangli" [S10]. That is a modern reconstruction. roman-britain.co.uk puts
  Carmarthen in "a network established during the rapid conquest of north central and west Wales by
  Julius Frontinus, Governor A.D. 74–77" [S35], again as inference.
- **That the Demetae did not resist.** No source found says so. Hughes cites Jarrett's suggestion
  "that southwest Wales was almost exempt from military occupation" and says the Llandeilo forts
  "refute" it; the forts at Llandovery, Llandeilo and Carmarthen, "within a days march of each other,
  must have provided an effective control over the surrounding population" [S1]. A large fort (3.85ha)
  for over a thousand men does not suggest a district taken for granted. **Present the Demetae's
  response as unknown.** The Iron Age note's idea scene (scouts seen from a rampart, an argument about
  fighting or waiting) is the right shape: both reactions imagined, neither asserted.
- **That gold drew Rome here.** Cadw asks it as a question: "was this the reason they came here in
  the first place?" [S32]. Keep it a question.

Inside the forts, Hughes traces a road "heading northeast out of the front entrance of Fort 2" and
roadside ditches beyond the north-west gate that probably link "with the observed sections of the
Roman road heading westwards towards Carmarthen" [S1]. **The road's construction date is not
known.** It joins forts built in the 70s, so it is probably Flavian, but on the day of arrival the
army would have had no finished road. That is inference.

**Names.** Coflein's segment titles use "Via Julia". No source read here gives that name any Roman
authority: Dahm repeats it [S10]. Codrington (1903): "On this hint the author of the spurious
Itinerary of Richard of Cirencester gave the name Via Julia ... The only authority for this name is
the reference above mentioned" (a 13th-century poem by Necham) [S65]; Coflein says the road is only
"sometimes referred to as the Via Julia" [S44]. (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: replaces "search results (not read in
full) point to 18th-century antiquaries".) **Do not use "Via Julia" in-world.** Coflein uses "Sarn Helen" for the road north from
Llandovery to Pumsaint (NPRN 402902, an 8.5m wide raised embankment at SN 76243621 to SN 76033667)
[S38]; that road is outside the radius, and the name is not used here. (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: the claim that
Sarn Helen's name "is medieval or later" had no source and is removed.)

**Spacing.** Fort 1 is 19.4km from Llandovery and 20.9km from Carmarthen, about 13 and 14 Roman
miles. Hughes: "the obvious place for a Roman fort that were frequently spaced a day's march apart"
[S1]. Vegetius says recruits should cover 20 Roman miles in five summer hours at the military step
[S52]. Vegetius wrote around AD 400 about older practice, so he is late evidence.

### Which way the army came

Evidence for an approach **from the north-east (Brecon, then Llandovery, then down the Tywi)**:

1. **Y Pigwn** (SN 82750 31200, NPRN 92004), on the Brecon Gaer to Llandovery road (RR62b, NPRN
   304504): "Two superimposed Roman temporary camps ... It can be assumed that they mark the passage
   of two Roman armies at the time of the conquest of the area in the 70s AD ... both facing towards
   the south-west" [S29][S28]. (Coflein's own text gives the road record as "NPRN 304405", apparently a
   typo for 304504; noted by the independent check.) 15ha and 9.8ha, the larger one earlier [S29]. People's Collection: "they
   represent different campaigns by the Roman army in southern Wales in the first century AD" [S31].
   *Cross-checked that they are campaign camps; the 70s date is Coflein's assumption.*
2. **Arosfa Garreg** (SN 80180 26295, NPRN 84422), on the Black Mountain 17.7km east of the centre:
   a 17.8ha temporary camp "of the type constructed by armies whilst on campaign" [S30]. Coflein gives
   **no date and no route**, so it shows only that an army camped on the high ground east of the
   valley at some time.
3. **The base behind the advance.** The legion probably moved from Usk to Caerleon about AD 75
   [S41][S42]: an inference, since S41 does not name the legion or say where it went, and S42 says only
   that Caerleon was founded about 75 for the Second Augustan (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*),
   and the Llandovery fort sits where the Brecon road meets the Tywi road [S14].
4. **Supply.** Hughes judges the Malvernian jars at Llandeilo were most likely imported "along routes
   through central Wales" [S1]. That is about supply after the fort existed, not the arrival.

Evidence for an approach **from the south-west (Carmarthen)**: Carmarthen's fort is of similar date
(about 75) and commands "the lowest river crossing of the Towy" [S33]; its name means "sea fort"
[S35], so supply by sea is possible. No source says the conquest of this valley began there.

Fort 1's front (*praetentura*) "probably" faced north-east, up the valley towards Llandovery [S1].
This is consistent with an approach from that side but does not prove it.

**Verdict:** show the army coming down the valley from the north-east, with an ⓘ: "Most likely
direction, inferred from forts and marching camps east of Llandovery. No record says which way they
came." *Reconstructed.*

### Camps and fortlets inside the radius

Coflein searches for marching or practice camps in Carmarthenshire returned Arosfa Garreg (just
outside) and practice camps near Llandovery and Cynghordy (outside) [S30]. **None is recorded inside

camp" or "part of the later designed landscape" [S1] (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: was "the 18th-century park",
which S1 does not say). A "possible Roman fortlet" at Cwmargenau, Llanwrda (SN

## The force

### Units at Dinefwr

- **No inscription or stamped tile names a unit.** The only Roman inscription seen at Llandeilo is the
  lost milestone of the emperor Tacitus (RIB 2262), AD 275-276, "seen in 1697 reused as the cornerstone
  of a small farm-house near Dynevor" [S44][S66], two centuries too late. A second stone, of Postumus,
  came from Trecastle Hill (found 1769) and was later kept "at Newton near Llandilo" [S44]. (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*:
  was "the only local Roman inscription".)
- **Size.** Fort 1, 240 x 160m inside its inner ditches (3.84ha), "suggests that the fort held a
  substantial military unit, at the very least an *ala quingenaria* (cavalry unit) or a *cohors
  milliaria* (a large infantry or mixed unit) and perhaps even a larger legionary detachment" [S1].
  Coflein: "a garrison of more than a thousand, drawn from several different units" [S2].
  *Cross-checked as an estimate; neither source names a unit.*
- **Cavalry.** A charred barley deposit in Fort 1's ditch: "Barley is frequently used as a fodder
  crop", "consistent with the view that the fort held at least an *ala quingenaria* (cavalry unit) or a
  *cohors milliaria* (a large infantry or mixed unit)" [S1]. It does not single out cavalry.
  *Single-source, hedged.* (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*.)
- **No military equipment** is listed among the 2003-2007 finds (a grep of the report finds no
  weapons, armour or harness) [S1]. So **every detail of dress and kit is analogy.**
- **The "parrot's beak" ditch** at Fort 2's gate was once linked with *legio II Adiutrix*, but Hughes
  says its presence at Llandeilo and elsewhere "must now call this association into question" [S1].
  It belongs to the later fort, not the arrival.

### Kit (documented elsewhere)

- **Legionaries.** Current Archaeology on the British Museum's *Legion* exhibition (2024):
  legionaries "carried long, heavy javelins called *pila* ... short stabbing swords and long shields
  (*scuta*)"; auxiliaries carried "smaller, flat ovals, and they had simple spears in place of
  javelins" [S56]. **Plate armour** (the modern name is *lorica segmentata*) was "part of the standard
  equipment of Roman legionaries from the 1st to the 3rd century AD"; a near-complete Augustan
  example from Kalkriese (AD 9) shows it was already in use [S55]. **Mail** was used too; the same
  exhibition showed "a chainmail shirt found in the barracks at Arbeia Fort" [S56].
- **No uniform.** "Roman soldiers actually had no formal uniform other than the belt denoting their
  military status, meaning that units probably had a much less standardised appearance than
  traditional images suggest" [S56]. Show variety.
- **Caution on Trajan's Column.** The neat split (legionaries in plate, auxiliaries in mail) comes
  largely from the Column. Wikipedia, citing Bishop (2002), says the reliefs are safest read as
  "impressions" and that plate armour turns up at auxiliary sites too [S57]. *Lead only; Bishop not
  read.*
- **Josephus on kit (AD 67, Judaea).** Foot soldiers have "breastplates and head-pieces", a "spear
  and a long buckler", and carry "a saw and a basket, a pick-axe and an axe, a thong of leather and a
  hook, with provisions for three days"; horsemen have a long sword, a long pole, a shield and three
  or more darts in a quiver [S50]. Whiston's translation also gives foot soldiers "swords on each
  side", the left one longer; that does not match the usual sword on the right, so do not follow
  that detail literally.
- **Helmets** were not researched in this pass (see Open questions).

### The column on the march

Josephus, *Jewish War* III.6.2, Vespasian leaving Ptolemais in AD 67 "in that order wherein the
Romans used to march" [S45], in order:

1. Light-armed auxiliaries and archers, "that they might ... search out the woods that looked
   suspiciously".
2. The fully armed Romans, foot and horse.
3. "Ten out of every hundred, carrying ... what was necessary to measure out a camp".
4. Road-makers, to "make the road even and straight", level rough ground and "cut down the woods".
5. The commanders' baggage ("carriages"), with a cavalry guard.
6. The general with picked foot, horse and pikemen.
7. The legion's own 120 horsemen.
8. Mules carrying the siege engines.
9. Cohort commanders and tribunes.
10. "The ensigns encompassing the eagle ... these sacred ensigns are followed by the trumpeters."
11. The main body, "six men in depth", with a centurion behind.
12. Servants leading the soldiers' baggage on "mules and other beasts of burden".
13. Auxiliaries ("mercenaries"), then a rearguard of foot and many horse.

This was a full army with legions. Dinefwr's builders may have been a smaller, mostly auxiliary
force, so scale it down; the sequence (scouts, surveyors, road party, standards, main body, baggage,
rearguard) is the part worth keeping. *Documented elsewhere; single-source (Josephus).*

### The marching camp

- **Josephus III.5.1-2**: they "do not begin to fight till they have walled their camp about"; the
  ground is levelled; the camp is "four-square by measure" with towers, "four gates, one at every side",
  streets inside, the commanders' tents in the middle and the general's tent in the very middle; "if
  occasion require, a trench is drawn round the whole, whose depth is four cubits, and its breadth
  equal" [S46][S47]. Each company is brought "their wood, and their corn, and their water", and they
  eat together [S48].
- **Wales, from the earthworks**: about thirty marching camps are known in Wales, "usually constructed
  with a single season's campaign in mind", "1.3ha up to almost 20 ha", "typically square in shape with
  rounded corners", with "a single ditch and rampart" and elaborate gates; inside, "rows upon rows of
  tents ... a unit (*contubernium*) of eight men sleeping in each tent" [S31]. Y Pigwn's gates have
  *claviculae*, curved arcs of rampart behind the gap [S29][S31]. *Cross-checked (S29, S31, S46).*
- Josephus's wooden towers and artillery on the rampart describe a camp in hostile Judaea; low banks
  as at Y Pigwn and Arosfa Garreg (about 2m wide, 0.5m high today [S30]) are the local evidence.

## The local people in AD 74

- **Who.** "Demetae" is a name from Ptolemy [S9]; whether it was their own name is open
  ([`era-iron-age.md`](era-iron-age.md), People & society). roman-britain.co.uk suggests the tribe was
  later run from Carmarthen [S58].
- **Settlement.** The Iron Age note's sources describe small farming communities in enclosed
  farmsteads, with no excavated roundhouse from the Tywi valley (ironage:S27, ironage:S28). Nothing
  new was found for AD 74 specifically.
- **Garn Goch** (SN 69120 24320, 7.2km from Fort 1) has never been excavated, so whether it was in use
  in AD 74 is **unknown** (ironage:S1). The claim that it "was probably rebuilt and strengthened to
  meet any threat" from Rome comes from one local site and its copies (ironage:S6, ironage:S10) and is
  unverified. Its line of sight to the road on the north side of the valley was not checked.
- **Native pottery at the fort.** "Malvern" jars, "undoubtedly an Iron Age" tradition but imported in
  the Roman period, perhaps as containers "for some commodity - perhaps salt?" [S1]. Hughes frames the
  forts' effect on "the local agricultural economy" as an open question [S1].
- **Money before Rome.** Three Republican denarii (c. 100 to 31 BC) and one Augustan were found at Home
  Farm [S1]. They need not have arrived before the army; Roman soldiers carried old coin. Do not show
  local people using coins.
- **What a farmer saw** is not recorded anywhere. Reasonable, labelled reconstruction: horsemen and
  light troops first, then a long column, then men felling trees and digging a camp, then within
  weeks a timber fort on the ridge. Their feelings and words are **imagined** and need the ⓘ.
- **Not in AD 74**: the Llys Brychan villa near Bethlehem is 3rd-4th century [S60]; the Dinefwr
  "temple" and the possible villa at North Heronry Dam are undated Roman finds. None belongs in the
  arrival scene.

## Season and time

- **Season.** Roman campaigns were summer work: Agricola acted "when summer came" (*ubi aestas
  advenit*) [S3][S6], and his late-season attack in *Agricola* 18 is presented as unusual (most
  advisers wanted to wait) [S5]. Welsh marching camps were built "with a single season's campaign in
  mind" [S31]. *Reconstructed: summer is the honest default, not a dated fact.*
- **Year.** Within c. 73/74 to 77 on the governors' dates (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: was c. 74); Hughes notes that if the proportions of samian
  forms held in a larger sample, "a foundation date in the very early Flavian period might seem
  possible" [S1]. Pre-Flavian forms are absent [S1].
- **No scientific date.** The report has no dendrochronology or radiocarbon dating; post-holes up to
  0.4m across held timbers, but no surviving wood is reported [S1].
- **Time of day.** Nothing in any source.

## Sounds

- **Trumpets in camp.** "Their times also for sleeping, and watching, and rising are notified
  beforehand by the sound of trumpets, nor is any thing done without such a signal" [S48]. Breaking
  camp: the first call to strike tents, a second to load and stand ready (the camp is burnt), a third to
  march; then a herald "asks them thrice, in their own tongue, whether they be now ready to go out to
  war", and they answer "We are ready" with "a loud and cheerful voice", raising their right hands
  [S49]. *Documented elsewhere (Josephus, AD 67). A strong scene beat.*
- **On the march.** "They all march without noise, and in a decent manner, and every one keeps his own
  rank" [S50]. So: no singing or chanting. The trumpeters march behind the standards [S45].
- **Which instrument.** Vegetius (c. AD 400): the *tuba* calls soldiers to battle, retreat and work;
  the *cornu* is sounded when the standards move; both together in battle; the *classicum* is the
  commander's call, played by *bucinatores* on the cornu (*quotiens mouenda sunt signa, cornicines
  canunt*) [S53]. *Documented elsewhere and three centuries later; treat as a guide only.*
- **What a cornu sounded like.** A study by Christophe Vendries and IRCAM, based on the Pompeii cornua
  in Naples, found 90-100 dB at five metres, one harmonic series of six notes, "many ... not in harmony
  with each other", and that it "probably wasn't very good for playing melodies"; closest modern
  parallel the flugelhorn [S54]. *Single-source (a magazine report of the study).* Use a few rough,
  loud notes, never a tune.
- **Feet.** Josephus: a centurion "had shoes all full of thick and sharp nails as had every one of the
  other soldiers" and slipped on stone paving, falling "with a very great noise, which was made by his
  armor" [S51]. Current Archaeology: a hobnailed soldier's sandal from Vindolanda [S56].
  *Cross-checked that hobnails were normal.* On a muddy valley track the sound would be a dull tramp,
  not a ring; that is reconstruction.
- **Carts and animals.** Josephus mentions officers' "carriages", siege-engine mules and baggage mules
  [S45]. No source for their sound was found. Creaking wheels, hooves and braying are reasonable
  reconstruction.

## Open questions

- **Direction of approach.** No source says it; the north-east is an inference. A published synthesis
  (Burnham and Davies, *Roman Frontiers in Wales and the Marches*, RCAHMW 2010; Davies 2000, cited by
  Hughes) was not reached and might discuss the order of conquest in the south-west.
- **Arosfa Garreg's date and route.** Coflein gives neither. If it is Flavian and on a route towards
  Llangadog, it changes the likely approach.
- **The Llandovery fort's start date: contested.** Coflein says the 70s [S14]; Heneb says "probably in
  the AD 50s" [S64], and Wikipedia cites the Dyfed HER (4072) for a possible 50s start [S16]. If
  Llandovery began under Veranius or Didius Gallus, "the army arrived c. 74" may be the second Roman
  arrival in the valley. The HER record itself was not read. (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: was a lead only.)
  roman-britain.co.uk even suggests the Dinefwr fort was established under Veranius, but it is an
  enthusiast site and carries little weight [S67].
- **Agricola 77 or 78**: unresolved; show both.
- **Helmets** of the 70s for legionaries and auxiliaries were not researched. Needed before modelling.
- **Unit identity**: unknown, and likely to stay so without an inscription or stamped tile.
- **Sightlines** from Garn Goch, Fan Camp or Grongaer to the valley road: not checked (could be done
  from the terrain data).
- **Weather and season of AD 74 itself**: no source; the event-effects note may hold climate proxies.
