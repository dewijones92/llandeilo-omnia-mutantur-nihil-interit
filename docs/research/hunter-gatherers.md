# Hunter-gatherers in and around the Tywi valley, from the ice to the first farmers

Research for the agreed todo "Early people hunting and gathering" (Dewi: "early people hunting
stuff"), written 2026-10-02 from sources fetched that day. The brief is in
[`briefs/hunter-gatherers.md`](briefs/hunter-gatherers.md). WebSearch was used 16 times; every other
source was fetched directly and read as text. The Heneb Historic Environment Record was queried
through Archwilio's own search service with a box around the map circle, and each record read in
full; three records (DAT1755, DAT7521, DAT109211) are **restricted** and could not be read.

## How dates are given

- **Every date is in calendar years** (BC, or "cal BP", years before AD 1950). Where a source gives a
  radiocarbon age ("BP" uncalibrated), I calibrated it myself with the IntCal20 curve [S44] and say
  so as "my calibration". My script reproduces a published calibration exactly (Ogof-yr-Ychen,
  8597 ± 54 BP: mine 7736 to 7536 cal BC, Schulting's 7735 to 7541 cal BC [S30]), so it is fit for
  this, but a calibration with an assumed error (marked "assumed ±100") is only a guide.
- **Waun Fignen Felen's dates are radiocarbon years.** Smith and Cloutman's 1988 study is built on
  "pollen analysis and radiocarbon dating" and gives the elm decline as "ca. 5000-5500 years BP" [S19],
  an event the deep-time note puts at c. 4300 to 3250 BCE in calendar years, which fits a
  radiocarbon age, not a calendar one. So Coflein's "eighth millennium
  BP" [S17], which draws on that work, is very probably radiocarbon too. This settles the question the
  deep-time check left open ("does not say whether BP is calendar or radiocarbon"), at the level of
  strong inference rather than a stated fact.
- **The Godwin/Iversen pollen-zone dates in the deep-time note are uncalibrated** (as its check
  found). Converted from the tabled boundaries (my calibration, ±50 assumed; the boundaries are a
  scheme, not measurements): the start of the Boreal (hazel expanding, "7,700 BC") is c. 9250 to 8850
  cal BC; the start of the Atlantic wildwood ("5,500 BC") is c. 6400 to 6250 cal BC; its end
  ("3,000 BC") is c. 3950 to 3650 cal BC.

Confidence labels: **cross-checked** (two independent sources agree), **single-source**, **gap**.
Distances are from the app's map centre (E 262900, N 222500, `src/domain/geo.ts`), whose circle has a
radius of 16.1km.

## Summary

- **The ten miles are not empty of early finds, only of dated ones.** The Heneb HER records, inside
  the circle: a scatter of 453 struck flints and chert pieces at Love Lodge Farm, Ffairfach, "from the
  Mesolithic period onwards", **1.2km from the centre** [S1]; a "large flint scatter of Mesolithic date"
  at Nantgaredig, 13.5km west, with one implement the HER queries as "Aurignacian? or late
  Palaeolithic" [S5][S6]; a cave under **Carreg Cennen Castle** (5.2km) with human bones of two adults
  and one or two children and a **perforated horse tooth** sealed in stalagmite, "conventionally dated
  to the Upper Palaeolithic" but never radiocarbon dated [S8][S9]; and a quarried cave at Pant-y-llyn
  (6.2km) where twelve skeletons were said to lie with **elk and wild boar** bones in 1813 [S10]. The
  timeline note's "no dated Palaeolithic or Mesolithic site" stays true, but "nothing found" would be
  wrong, and these deserve a place in the app with their uncertainty shown.
- **People reached Wales by c. 12,500 BC in calendar years**, which supports the app's `people-return`
  date. A cut-marked cattle-family bone at Kendrick's Cave (north Wales) is dated "~14,500 cal. bp"
  (c. 12,550 BC) and the engraved reindeer in Cathole Cave, Gower, is at least 14,505 ± 560 years old by
  uranium-series dating (calendar years) [S25][S28][S29]. Gough's Cave in Somerset is a little earlier,
  15,070 to 14,610 cal BP [S25]. Cross-checked. This answers the timeline check's worry that "12,500" was
  a radiocarbon figure: the calendar evidence lands in the same place.
- **What they hunted then:** horse, red deer and wild cattle (diet at Gough's Cave; tooth beads of
  aurochs, red deer and brown bear at Kendrick's), with reindeer, elk and brown bear also present
  [S25][S27][S42]. In the **Younger Dryas** reindeer "once again became the dominant fauna" in south-west
  British caves and there are **no radiocarbon dates for people anywhere in the British Isles** [S25].
- **Mesolithic Wales (from c. 9500 BC):** red deer, roe deer, wild cattle and pig in the woods; fish,
  eels, otter, wolf and birds at Goldcliff; hazelnuts everywhere [S24][S30][S33]. Diet varied by place:
  the Caldey Island dead ate a lot of marine food, while the Early Mesolithic people buried at Worm's
  Head, then about 20km from the sea, ate almost none [S30].
- **Fire on the uplands, re-dated:** at Waun Fignen Felen (20.1km east, just off the map) the clearing
  and burning that the app dates "about 8,000 years ago" (6000 BC) is ca. 8000 radiocarbon BP [S19],
  which is **c. 7200 to 6600 BC** in calendar years (my calibration, assumed ±100). The app's event and
  almanac entry should move about a thousand years earlier.
- **No house, bow, dog, garment or word of their language survives from Mesolithic Wales.** Heneb
  says "there are no examples of houses known in Wales" [S20]. Those parts of any scene come from
  analogy (Howick, Star Carr, the Danish bows) or are imagined, and must say so.

## Phase 1: the Late Glacial warm spell, c. 13,000 to 10,900 BC

**Environment.** At Llanilid, near Bridgend, the nearest calibrated Late Glacial record, July
temperatures reached "around 20°C during the early Interstadial", then fell in two steps, the second
around 13,100 cal BP (c. 11,150 BC), cutting back juniper scrub and then birch woodland; warming in
western Britain "may have begun earlier" than in Greenland, perhaps before 14,500 cal BP [S26][S27].
Much of the Cambrian Mountains was ice-free by about 15,000 years ago (Glasser et al. 2018, cited by
[S27]). So the Tywi valley was open country with juniper and patches of birch when people arrived.
Cross-checked (Llanilid read directly; the ice dates single-source through [S27]).

**People.** The earliest calendar-dated evidence in Wales:

| Site | Evidence | Calendar date | Source |
|---|---|---|---|

| Kendrick's Cave, Llandudno | cut-marked bovine bone | "~14,500 cal. bp" (12,310 ± 50 BP; my calibration 12,860 to 12,130 BC) | [S25] |

**Two peoples.** The Gough's Cave individual traces "all its ancestry to Magdalenian-associated
individuals", while the Kendrick's Cave person has none of it and traces to groups "represented at
sites such as Villabruna, Italy"; they also differed in burial (cannibalised and processed at Gough's,
apparently buried at Kendrick's) and diet (terrestrial at Gough's; marine or freshwater food at
Kendrick's, with "conflicting dietary interpretations" of how much) [S25]. Single-source but
peer-reviewed and open access.

**What was hunted (animals present).** Horse, red deer and wild cattle at Gough's; reindeer, brown
bear, wolf, foxes, arctic hare and lemmings in the Tenby caves [S25][S27]; Priory Farm Cave
(Pembrokeshire) yielded "glacial and post-glacial fauna (cattle, horse, reindeer)" [S30]. An elk
skeleton at Poulton-le-Fylde, Lancashire, with barbed bone points beside it and 17 lesions, died "around
13,500 years ago" (whether this is calendar or radiocarbon is not stated in the source read) [S41][S42].
The Creswellian summary on Wikipedia (a lead) lists horse or red deer as the main prey, with arctic
hare, reindeer, wild cattle and others [S43].

**Tools.** Creswellian (British Late Magdalenian) backed points and blades [S27][S43]; barbed bone
points (Poulton) [S42]; perforated tooth beads (Kendrick's) [S25]. Whether these people used bows is
unknown: the oldest arrow shafts with a nock are "none older than approximately 10,000 BC" [S37].

## Phase 2: the Younger Dryas, c. 10,900 to 9,700 BC

**Environment.** At Llanilid the stadial ran "between ca 12,600 and 11,400 cal yr BP" (c. 10,650 to
9,450 BC), with mean July temperatures of 10 to 11°C and "a scrub tundra with Betula, Salix and a range of
open-habitat taxa" [S26]. The Greenland dates used by the app (c. 10,900 to 9,700 BC) are a little
earlier than the Welsh ones (already noted by the timeline check).

**People.** "Currently, there are no radiocarbon determinations documenting human presence in the
British Isles during the Younger Dryas" [S25]. Single-source; Wikipedia's Creswellian article (a lead)
also says Britain "was at times unoccupied" then [S43].

**Animals.** Reindeer "once again became the dominant fauna in the cave sites of southwest Britain"
[S25]. Collared and Norway lemmings and other cold voles persisted until the Holocene warming, then died
out in Britain [S27].

**Analogy.** On the continent, reindeer hunters of this period at Stellmoor, near Hamburg, left pine
arrows with foreshafts and flint points, grooved at the base for a bowstring (Wikipedia, a lead) [S38];
Sachers dates the oldest nocked shafts to about 10,000 BC [S37]. Not evidence for Wales.

## Phase 3: the early Holocene and Early Mesolithic, c. 9,700 to 7,000 BC

**Environment.** At Llanilid the Holocene began at "ca 11,400 cal yr BP" (c. 9,450 BC) with "an abrupt
temperature rise of the order of 9°C" and "rapid expansion of Betula woodland" [S26]. Amgueddfa Cymru:
"Juniper was the first tree to return, quickly followed by birch, hazel, pine, willow and alder. In the
lowlands, oak, elm and pine dominated ... while in the uplands pine and birch were more common" [S24].
Hazel "played an important role in the woodland" around Waun Fignen Felen before the peat [S19].
Much land now under the Bristol Channel and Cardigan Bay was still dry, and the sea rose through
the period [S20][S22].

**People in Wales.**

**Burial.** Caves were used "for the deposition of human remains in the earlier Mesolithic and in the
Neolithic, separated by an intervening gap of nearly two millennia" [S30].

**Analogy: shelters and dogs.** Star Carr, Yorkshire, has "perhaps the oldest known Mesolithic house in
the British Isles", about 11,000 years ago, 3.5m wide on a ring of posts, with "a boat paddle, beads,
arrowheads and antler headdresses" [S20], and a Preboreal domestic dog [S40]. Howick, Northumberland:
a round house "averaging 6m in diameter" with a sunken floor up to 0.5m deep, posts and three
building phases; 21 radiocarbon dates put "primary construction ... c. 7800 BC (Cal.)"; its hearths held
"hazelnut and acorn shell" and burnt bone of "wild pig, fox, birds and either domestic dog or wolf"
[S36]. Heneb: "there are no examples of houses known in Wales" [S20].

## Phase 4: the later Mesolithic wildwood, c. 7,000 to 4,000 BC

**Environment and fire.** At Waun Fignen Felen "Mesolithic man was probably responsible for making a
small clearing in the woodland at ca. 8000 years BP", heath appeared "with circumstantial evidence of
maintenance by burning", alder spread "possibly taking advantage of environmental damage caused by
Mesolithic man", and heath lasted "until ca. 5500 years BP" [S19]. In calendar years (my calibration,
assumed ±100): clearing c. 7200 to 6600 BC; heath until c. 4550 to 4050 BC. Coflein: "local heathland
and open birch woodland were burnt, to encourage large game. The change to fire management played a
role in the inception of peat" [S17]. Cross-checked (two independent texts on the burning). At Goldcliff
there was also "regular burning of woodland and areas of reed", deliberate or not "still open to
debate" [S23].

**Goldcliff, Severn Estuary (the richest Welsh site).** Mesolithic activity from c. 5800 BC to the last
occupation, Site J, at c. 4800 BC; bones of "red deer, roe deer, boar, and aurochs, as well as otter and
wolf, and some birds", fish "particularly eels"; wooden objects; hearths "laid with heat-fractured
stones"; and the first Mesolithic fish traps found in Britain, wattle fences dated 5310 to 4912 BC
[S33][S34]. Of 856 footprint-tracks recorded 2001 to 2017, 342 were human and 270 bird (46% of the birds
common crane); 29% of the human trails were made by children, some perhaps aged four or younger, walking
to a fishing area [S35]. Tracks of deer, aurochs and "a probable dog or wolf"; the bird prints lie on
silts "laid down at the height of summer" [S33]. Cross-checked between the excavators' own article [S33],
the thesis [S35] and Heneb [S23].

**Other Welsh evidence.** At Ogof Garreg Hir (Caldey) later Mesolithic tools came with red deer, roe
deer, wolf, pig, hare, fox and bird [S30]. Submerged forests hold "auroch, red deer and brown bear" at
Whitesands and pig at Lydstep [S22]. A man's footprints at Uskmouth, about 6,250 years ago, show "he was
accustomed to walking without shoes" [S24].

**What Amgueddfa Cymru says of the way of life.** "This heavily wooded landscape was home to red deer,
roe deer, wild cattle and pigs, all of which were potential prey"; "it is likely that parts of the
woodland in upland Wales was deliberately cleared"; "Fish is believed to have been important"; and "it
is likely that people spent part of their year travelling in pursuit of herds ... During the warm summer
months it is likely that they were to be found in inland areas, while in winter they returned to the
lower lying coast or river valleys" [S24]. That seasonal round is the museum's hypothesis, not a finding;
the evidence that supports a version of it is Waun Fignen Felen's brief, repeated upland visits with
flint from the coast [S17] and Goldcliff's summer footprints [S33]. Heneb also places later Mesolithic
seasonal hunting sites in the uplands at Llyn Brenig [S20].

**Bows (analogy).** The Holmegaard bows from Zealand are elm (*Ulmus glabra*), about 152cm long, and are
the oldest certain bows: "circa 6,500 BC" by Sachers [S37], "c. 7000 BC" or "about 8,000 BP" by Wikipedia
[S38][S39]. Microliths were the arrow tips and barbs [S24][S32].

**The end.** Amgueddfa puts "the introduction of farming around 6,000 years ago" [S24]; isotopes show "a
sharp shift towards the dominance of terrestrial foods in coastal locations at the start of the
Neolithic" [S30].

## Open questions

1. **The Carreg Cennen cave is the best chance of a dated Late Glacial or Mesolithic presence inside the
   ten miles.** The bones (National Museum Wales, accession 31.384 [S8]) and the perforated horse tooth
   have never been radiocarbon dated. Coflein says the remains are "conventionally dated to the Upper
   Palaeolithic" [S9]; the HER quotes Campbell's "perhaps 8000 or more years ago" [S8], which would be
   Mesolithic. Caution: at Little Hoyle the five dated humans from the old excavations all came out
   earlier Neolithic [S27]. A question for Amgueddfa Cymru.
2. **Two adults and one child, or two children?** Coflein [S9] and the HER [S8] differ.
3. **The Kendrick's horse jaw:** "~12,900 cal. bp" (OxA-X-2185-26, 11,050 ± 90 BP; my calibration
   13,100 to 12,770 cal BP) [S25] against Heneb's "some 13,500 years ago" [S21]. The 2022 figure is newer
   and gives its lab number; Heneb's page is undated.
4. **Holmegaard's date:** c. 6500 BC [S37] against c. 7000 BC and "about 8,000 BP" (which is c. 6000 BC) on
   two Wikipedia pages [S38][S39].
5. **Amgueddfa Cymru gives two dates for the first people:** "around 15,000 years ago" [S24] and "around
   12,500 years ago" (timeline:S1). The first matches the calibrated evidence; the second is probably a
   radiocarbon age.
6. **Restricted HER records:** the Nantgaredig scatter (DAT1755), the Love Lodge fieldwork (DAT109211)
   and the Dinas site that produced the hyena tooth (DAT7521). Heneb could be asked for them, and for
   N. Page (2004), *A Review of the Lithic Evidence from the Regional SMR*, which every lithic record cites.
7. **Is Allen's 1938 "flint chipping site" in Llanegwad parish the Glyneiddian scatter?** Probably, not
   confirmed [S5][S7].
8. **Is the Crug-glas "hand axe" really a Palaeolithic hand axe?** Kept privately, never described [S2].
   If so it is far older than this period and may have been moved by ice or by people later.
9. **Salmon and sewin in the Mesolithic Tywi:** no evidence found. The Worm's Head people show "the lack
   of any significant consumption of fish" while Kendrick's and Caldey show marine or freshwater food
   [S25][S30]. Fishing on the Tywi would be imagined.
10. **Where did the flint come from?** The Love Lodge pieces are "majority ... flint" [S1]; whether the
    Tywi valley has any local flint was not researched. Waun Fignen Felen's came from a beach 29km away
    [S17].
11. **Group size, clothing, language:** no source found for Wales. Goldcliff's 21 identified trails
    (9 in [S35], plus 12 from an earlier study) are many visits, not one group.
12. **The Poulton elk's "13,500 years ago":** calendar or radiocarbon is not stated in the source read
    [S42].
13. **Horses after the Late Glacial** were not attested in any source read; when wild horse left Wales is
    open (as in the deep-time note).

## Implications for the app (for whoever next edits content)

Not changed here; recorded so a later pass can act on them.

- `src/content/events.ts` `people-return` (12,500 BC): **supported in calendar years** by Kendrick's
  (~14,500 cal BP) and Cathole (at least 14,505 ± 560) [S25][S28]; its sentence "No dated site inside these
  ten miles" remains true, and could add that undated finds exist (Carreg Cennen cave, Love Lodge).
- `mesolithic-burning` (6000 BC) and almanac `mesolithic-fire` (6000 to 4000 BC): the source's "ca. 8000
  years BP" is radiocarbon [S19], so **c. 7000 BC** (c. 7200 to 6600) is the calendar start, and heath
  lasted to c. 4500 BC. "About 8,000 years ago" in the text should become "about 9,000 years ago".
- Candidate new items, each with its tier: the Carreg Cennen cave (here, undated), the Love Lodge scatter
  (here, undated), the Younger Dryas absence of people (Britain-wide), Goldcliff's footprints and fish
  trap (Wales, analogy).
