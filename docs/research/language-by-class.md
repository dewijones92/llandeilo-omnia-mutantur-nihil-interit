## Summary

Llandeilo sits in a part of Wales (historic Carmarthenshire, ancient Demetae/Dyfed
territory) where a Brittonic Celtic language has almost certainly been spoken
continuously since before the Roman period, evolving in place through Primitive Welsh,
Old Welsh, Middle Welsh and Modern Welsh into the Welsh spoken in the town today. What
changed by era was not "which language" so much as **which languages sat on top of Welsh,
who used them, and for what**: Latin for the Roman army/administration and then for the
Church and law; Anglo-Norman French and English for conquest-era lords and later
officialdom and gentry; English again, more totally, from the 19th century onward through
schooling, industry and administration. Ordinary farmers and labourers in this district
remained majority Welsh-speaking right through the 19th century and into the 20th; the
gentry and professional/official class anglicised much earlier, roughly from the Acts of
Union (1536/1543) onward, accelerating in the 18th–19th centuries. Llandeilo's own claim to
linguistic fame is real and specific: a marginal note in the Lichfield/Llandeilo Gospels,
the "Surexit memorandum," is among the very earliest surviving connected text in the Welsh
language, written by (or for) the church at Llandeilo itself.

What is **not** known, and should be shown as unknown in the game: what language(s), if
any single one, were spoken here before Celtic speech arrived or became dominant; exactly
when that happened; and any specifics of pre-Roman-Iron-Age speech in this exact valley.
Scholarship is split on where and when Celtic itself formed (traditional "Iron Age from
central Europe" view vs. the "Celtic from the West" Atlantic Bronze Age hypothesis), and
that split should be shown as a live debate, not resolved.

Census data (cross-checked across Carmarthenshire County Council and ONS sources) shows
Llandeilo's Welsh-speaking population falling from just over half (55.1% in 2001, 50.3% in
2011, ward-level) in step with, but somewhat behind, the county's steeper 2011→2021 decline
(43.9% → 39.9%, the largest of any Welsh county). A specific 2021 ward-level figure for
Llandeilo itself was not located in this research pass (see Open Questions).

## Detailed notes per era

### What is and isn't known about pre-Celtic languages

There is **no direct written or spoken evidence** of any language in Britain, or in this
specific area, before the arrival or dominance of Celtic speech. What can be said, per a
cross-section of sources: if toponymic evidence recorded by the Greek explorer Pytheas
(4th century BC) is a reliable guide, a Celtic language was already dominant, if not
universal, in Britain by that time [S1]. Some river names and landscape terms across
Britain and western Europe do not fit known Celtic patterns, which has led some linguists
to posit an older, otherwise-unattested substrate language or languages — but this remains
speculative. The most widely discussed fringe theory, the "Vasconic substratum" hypothesis
(linking a pre-Indo-European substrate to Basque), is treated as controversial and rejected
by most specialists [S1]. **Nothing specific can be said about pre-Celtic speech in the
Llandeilo/Tywi valley area itself** — this should be shown in the game as an honest "we do
not know," not filled in with invented reconstruction.

### Arrival/development of Celtic and Brittonic: the debate

Two broad pictures compete in current scholarship:

- **Traditional view**: Celtic languages spread into Britain from continental Europe in
  the pre-Roman Iron Age (roughly linked to Hallstatt/La Tène material culture), arriving
  as a intrusive language relatively late.
- **"Celtic from the West"** (Barry Cunliffe and John T. Koch, from Cunliffe's *Facing the
  Ocean* (2001) and the *Celtic from the West* volumes, 2010–2016): proposes Celtic
  languages instead developed much earlier, in the Atlantic Bronze Age (c. 2000–750 BC),
  spreading along Atlantic-facing trade networks from Iberia and outward, with no need for
  a later mass migration into Britain. Koch has argued the fragmentary Tartessian
  inscriptions of southwest Iberia are actually Celtic, pushing the language family's
  origin further back and further west. This proposal is explicitly **controversial** and
  contested by other Celticists [S2].

For the game: this should be shown as a genuine, unresolved scholarly debate rather than
picking a side. Either way, there is good agreement that by the time of direct Roman
contact, a Brittonic (British Celtic) language was spoken across most of Britain, including
this area, by the local tribe recorded as the **Demetae** [S30].

### Brittonic in the Iron Age and Roman period; Latin among the military/administration

The Demetae occupied territory corresponding to modern Dyfed (Carmarthenshire, Pembrokeshire,
part of Ceredigion); Ptolemy's 2nd-century *Geography* names their civitas capital as
**Moridunum** ("sea fort"), identified with Carmarthen, and also names the settlement of
**Luentinum** [S30]. A Roman fort was established at Carmarthen around AD 75, on or near
an Iron Age hillfort site (Merlin's Hill), lasting as a garrison until about AD 120, after
which the adjoining civilian *vicus* grew into a town [S29][S30]. Roman military presence
also reached the immediate Llandeilo area: two Roman forts have been identified on the
Dinefwr estate grounds, dated to around AD 74 [S27]. Nearby, **Garn Goch** (Y Gaer Fawr), a
very large Iron Age (or possibly Late Bronze Age) hillfort of 16.6 hectares overlooking the
Tywi valley, shows the pre-Roman scale of local settlement and defensive organisation, with
evidence of use going back to the Neolithic [S31].

The population of the Roman-period countryside overwhelmingly spoke Brittonic; Latin was
the language of the army, of formal administration, and of Romanised town life. No local
Roman-period inscriptions from the Llandeilo/Dinefwr forts specifically were found in this
research pass (an open question). More broadly for Roman Britain, "British Latin" — the
Latin actually spoken in the province — is reconstructed mainly through the Latin loanwords
it left in Brittonic/Welsh, since Britain lost direct contact with the Latin-speaking world
earlier than most of the former Western Empire [S3].

### Latin loanwords into Welsh

Roughly 350 years of Roman rule left a substantial layer of Latin loanwords in Welsh,
covering basic and everyday vocabulary as well as learned/religious terms: **coes** ('leg')
from *coxa*; **braich** ('arm') from *bracchium*; **barf** ('beard') from *barba*; **pysg**
('fish') from *pisces*; **rhwyd** ('net') from *rete*; **fflam** ('flame') from *flamma*;
**cawl** ('soup/broth') from *caulis*; **ysgol** ('school') from *schola*; **eglwys**
('church', not independently verified with etymology in this pass but standardly derived
from *ecclesia*); **pont** ('bridge', standardly from *pons/pontem*, not independently
verified in this pass — flagged for follow-up) [S32 general pattern; individual etymologies
S3 and general Welsh-linguistics consensus — GPC itself was not queried for individual
headwords in this research pass, see Open Questions].

Linguist Peter Schrijver's reconstruction of British Latin (*The Rise and Fall of British
Latin*, 2002/2007) — summarised well by a specialist blog cross-checked against the book's
stated thesis — identifies specific sound features recoverable from these loanwords: vowel
prothesis before /sC/ clusters (*schola* → *ysgol*, matching French *école*/Spanish
*escuela*); early loss of /h/ (*hōra* → *awr* 'hour'); an o→a vowel shift (*occāsiō* →
*achos* 'cause'); syncope of unstressed vowels (*populus* → *pobl* 'people'); retention of
/w/ where other Romance languages shifted to /v/ (*vīnum* → *gwin* 'wine'); no
palatalisation of C/G as seen elsewhere in Vulgar Latin; and lenition of intervocalic
/p,t,k/ to /b,d,g/ (*catēna* → *cadwyn* 'chain'). Schrijver's conclusion, as reported: British
Latin was "to all intents and purposes identical with the type of Romance underlying Old
French," making it linguistically closer to continental Vulgar Latin/early Romance than is
often assumed, while being somewhat archaic in a few respects (retained /w/, no
palatalisation) [S3].

Also notable: the Welsh word for the planet Venus, **Gwener**, derives not from the Latin
nominative *Venus* but from the oblique-case stem *Vener-* (as in genitive *Veneris*),
suggesting British Latin/Brittonic borrowing took place after the classical case system had
begun breaking down in spoken usage [S32].

### The transition Brittonic → Primitive/Old Welsh (c. 5th–6th century)

Welsh is conventionally said to become distinct from Common Brittonic around AD 550, a
period called **Primitive** or **Archaic Welsh**, lasting to around the mid-8th century,
after which the **Old Welsh** period runs to roughly 1150 [S5][S20]. The most significant
change driving this split is **apocope**: the loss of final unstressed syllables, which
collapsed Brittonic's more elaborate word-endings and pushed the language toward the
shape recognisable as Welsh. By the 6th century, Common Brittonic was "swiftly diverging"
into what would become Welsh, Cumbric (in the Hen Ogledd, the Brittonic-speaking north),
Cornish and Breton [S5].

### Old Welsh

Old Welsh (c. 800–1150) survives almost entirely as **marginal notes, glosses on Latin
texts, and place/personal names embedded in Latin documents**, rather than substantial
continuous texts of its own — Latin remained the language of writing proper. The single
most important exception, and the one most directly relevant to Llandeilo, is the Surexit
memorandum (see below). Other early Old Welsh material outside this project's immediate
area includes marginal glosses and the *Juvencus englynion* (englynion, i.e. short-form
Welsh poems, added in the margins of a Latin manuscript of Juvencus's Latin verse Gospel
paraphrase, now in Cambridge) — noted here as a lead for further research; this document did
not manage to independently verify its contents in this pass (see Open Questions).

### The Llandeilo Gospels marginalia (the 'Surexit' memorandum)

The **Lichfield Gospels** (also called the **Llandeilo Gospels**, the **St Chad Gospels**,
or the **Book of St Teilo**) is an 8th-century illuminated Gospel manuscript, art-historically
dated by Peter Lord to around 730, placing it after the Lindisfarne Gospels and before the
Book of Kells [S6]. Only 236 pages (118 leaves) survive; Luke's Gospel is mostly missing and
John's Gospel entirely lost, and the original binding — apparently stripped of precious
metalwork — is gone [S6].

**Connection to Llandeilo**: the book was at Llandeilo Fawr for roughly two hundred years,
from the early 9th century. According to the manuscript's own marginalia, a Welshman named
**Gelli** bought the book "for the price of his best horse" and gave it to the church of
St Teilo at Llandeilo. It later left Llandeilo under unknown circumstances and had entered
Lichfield Cathedral's possession by the late 10th century [S6][S4].

**The memorandum itself**: on the final page of Matthew's Gospel, a 9th-century Llandeilo
scribe recorded a legal settlement in a mix of Latin and Old Welsh — opening with the Latin
word *Surexit* ("he arose"), continuing mostly in Welsh with Latin legal terms retained for
technical vocabulary. It records a land dispute: **Tudfwlch**, son-in-law of Tudri, claimed
the land of **Telych**, which was held by **Elgu**, son of Gelli, and Elgu's kin. After
prolonged dispute, the case was settled with Elgu paying compensation (a horse and cattle)
to prevent further feud, with witnesses ("good men") present [S4][S5]. Full text as given
by a cross-check of two sources (Exploring Celtic Civilizations project summary and the
Wikipedia Old Welsh article, which broadly agree):

> *surexit tutbulc filius liuit hagener tutri dierchi tir telih haioid ilau elcu filius
> gelhig haluidt iuguret amgucant pel amtanndi ho diued diprotant gener tutri o guir
> imguodant ir degion guragon tagc rodesit elcu guetig equs tres uache, tres uache
> nouidligi namin ir ni be cas igridu dimedichat guetig hit did braut grefiat guetig nis
> minn tutbulc hai cenetl in ois oisau*

English translation (paraphrased/attested by the same cross-checked sources): "Tudfwlch
son of Llywyd, son-in-law of Tudri, arose to claim the land of Telych, which was in the
hand of Elgu son of Gelli... after dispute, they came to agreement: the good men said 'let
there be peace'; Elgu then gave a horse, three cows, three newly-calved cows, so that there
should be no enmity between them from that settlement until Judgement Day; Tudfwlch and his
kindred will not want it ever again, for ever and ever." [S4][S5]

Scholarly dating places the writing of the marginalia itself in the early-to-mid 9th
century, though it may be a copy of a text originally drafted in the 6th or 7th century
[S4]. Either way, this is described across sources as **the earliest surviving connected
document in the Welsh language** [S6][S4] — a genuine, locally-grounded, high-value fact for
the game: this exact land dispute, over land called Telych, was recorded, in Welsh, by
Llandeilo's own church community.

### Middle Welsh (c. 1100–1400): the Gogynfeirdd and Welsh law texts

By the Middle Welsh period, Welsh had become a full literary language, not just a spoken
one glossed onto Latin. The **Gogynfeirdd** ("fairly early poets," also called Poets of the
Princes) were court poets active c. 1100–1350, composing elaborate praise poetry, elegy, and
religious verse for Welsh princes and nobility, in Middle Welsh [S8][S9]. Meilyr Brydydd
(fl. 1100–1137) is the earliest whose work survives. Key manuscript witnesses include the
Black Book of Carmarthen (c. 1250) — the single earliest surviving manuscript entirely in
Welsh, and produced in this immediate region — the Hendregadredd Manuscript (c. 1282–1330),
and the later Red Book of Hergest (c. 1400) [S9].

A directly relevant, cross-checked example: the poet **Peryf ap Cedifor** composed an elegy
(c. 1170–1171) mourning three of his brothers killed alongside **Hywel ab Owain Gwynedd** at
the Battle of Pentraeth. An excerpt, as quoted on Wikipedia's Hywel ab Owain Gwynedd article:

> Welsh: *"Tra fuam yn saith, trisaith — ni'n beiddai, / Ni'n ciliai cyn ein llaith..."*
> English: *"While we were seven, not thrice seven would defy us, / [none] would rout us
> before our death..."* [S10]

This is genuine attested Middle Welsh poetry and can be used (with the ⓘ marker, dated and
sourced) as a documented sample, though it is not tied to Llandeilo specifically.

**Welsh law** (Cyfraith Hywel, "the Law of Hywel," traditionally attributed to the 10th-century
king Hywel Dda though no manuscript survives from his own time) is preserved in over 40
manuscripts dating from the mid-13th to 16th centuries, in three main redactions —
**Cyfnerth**, **Blegywryd**, and **Iorwerth** — and in two languages, Welsh and Latin
[S11]. Most law manuscripts are in Welsh, and even the Latin copies (such as Peniarth 28,
mid-13th century) are now generally thought to be translations *from* a Welsh original, with
Welsh technical terms often retained untranslated inside the Latin text [S11]. **The
Blegywryd Redaction is specifically associated with Deheubarth** (south Wales, this
region's medieval kingdom), and manuscripts from that redaction claim at least equality of
status for the king of **Dinefwr** — Llandeilo's own castle and the historic seat of
Deheubarth's rulers — against the rival claim of primacy for the king of Aberffraw
(Gwynedd) made in Iorwerth Redaction manuscripts [S11]. This is a strong, locally-specific,
well-sourced fact: Dinefwr was not just a fortress but a named seat of legal-political
authority in the Welsh law tradition itself.

A documented example of the substance of the law (English translation, from a cross-checked
source, original Welsh not independently verified in this pass): on inheritance, "the law of
the church says that no-one is entitled to patrimony save the father's eldest son by his
wedded wife. The law of Hywel adjudges it to the youngest son as to the eldest..." [S11] —
illustrating a genuine point of difference between Welsh native law and Latin/church law
that would have been talked about by both.

Latin remained the language of the church and of some formal/scholarly writing throughout
this period; it was not a spoken vernacular for ordinary people, but educated clergy would
have used it functionally alongside Welsh.

### Anglo-Norman French and English among incomers after the Norman and Edwardian conquests

The Norman conquest of England (1066) was followed by a piecemeal, lord-by-lord Norman
push into Wales rather than a single campaign; William FitzOsbern overran Gwent, and the
Earl of Shrewsbury invaded Deheubarth [S12]. Marcher Lords were granted quasi-royal
authority in the territories they seized, creating the Welsh Marches as a buffer zone
distinct from unconquered "Pura Wallia" [S12]. Deheubarth itself was contested repeatedly:
**Rhys ap Tewdwr**, its last effectively-independent native king, was killed in 1093, and
Norman lordships came to cover much of the former kingdom, though native rulers (notably
**Rhys ap Gruffydd, "The Lord Rhys," 1155–1197**) regained and rebuilt power in the region,
including rebuilding **Dinefwr Castle** [S13].

The Cambro-Norman settler communities that resulted from this conquest and settlement were,
per one cross-referenced source, originally Norman-French-speaking but became
**English-speaking communities** over time, rather than remaining French-speaking or
assimilating into Welsh [S12] — i.e., the incomer elite's language shifted from Anglo-Norman
French toward English before Wales was even fully conquered by the English crown. This
matches the general pattern across medieval England, where Anglo-Norman French persisted
longest among the highest aristocracy and in law/administration, while English reasserted
itself as the vernacular of the wider incomer population from the 14th century onward.

The final loss of Welsh political independence in this region came with **Edward I's
conquest**: following the death of Llywelyn ap Gruffudd (1282) and the defeat of his
brother Dafydd's rebellion (1283), Deheubarth's remaining lords became vassals of the
English crown, and the region was reorganised by the **Statute of Rhuddlan** into the
historic counties of Cardiganshire, Carmarthenshire and Pembrokeshire [S13]. Dinefwr Castle
itself was garrisoned and repaired by the English crown in 1282–83 — ditches cleared, a
tower, bridge and hall repaired, a new gate installed [S13]. From this point, English
administrators and law, and the Marcher/royal officialdom, sat linguistically on top of a
continuously Welsh-speaking general population — a layering that, in modified form, would
persist for centuries.

### Early Modern Welsh and the 1588 Bible (William Morgan)

**William Morgan** (1545–1604), a native of Penmachno (north Wales) and graduate of St
John's College, Cambridge, undertook the full Bible translation into Welsh from around 1578,
building on William Salesbury's earlier 1567 New Testament and Psalms, but translating the
Old Testament afresh from Hebrew and Greek [S14]. **Y Beibl Cyssegr-lan** was published in
London in 1588 by the Queen's Printer's deputies [S14][S15].

The 1588 Bible's significance for the language is treated as one of the pivotal facts of
Welsh linguistic history: it gave Welsh a single, high-prestige, standardised written form —
described by one source as occupying "a similar place in the Welsh language to that of the
venerated King James Version in English" [S15] — and by conferring liturgical status on
Welsh, it secured the language's continued use for worship and everyday communication for
centuries afterward, even as gentry and officialdom anglicised [S15]. This document did not
manage to independently source a quoted passage of the 1588 text itself in this research
pass (an Open Question — worth fetching, e.g., the Lord's Prayer or a well-known verse, for
use as an attested Early Modern Welsh sample phrase).

### Gentry anglicisation: the Acts of Union (1536/1543) language clause

The **Laws in Wales Acts 1535 and 1542** (commonly called the Acts of Union) integrated
Wales's legal, political and administrative systems with England's. Of the whole Act, "only
150 words... were concerned with the use of the Welsh language" [S16]: Section 20 banned
Welsh in court proceedings and barred Welsh monoglots from holding public office; officials
and MPs representing Wales had to speak English, and law courts had to operate in English
[S16][S20]. This provision was not repealed until the **Welsh Language Act 1993** [S20].

Notably, cross-checked across two sources, **the Acts were popular with the Welsh gentry**
despite the language restriction: gentry families saw the Acts as bringing legal equality
with English subjects and as reducing the power of the Marcher lords over them [S16]. This
is the key mechanism by which Welsh gentry anglicisation began in earnest from the Tudor
period: it was not simply imposed from outside but was, for the landowning class, a path to
status, office, and legal parity, and many gentry families educated their sons in England
and adopted English as a language of business and polite society over the following two
centuries, while remaining, in many cases, bilingual and still landlords over an
overwhelmingly Welsh-speaking tenantry.

### 18th–19th century: farmers and labourers vs. gentry and officialdom

By the 18th and early 19th centuries, the social pattern was stark and well-documented:
ordinary farmers, tenant-labourers and tradespeople across rural Carmarthenshire were
overwhelmingly Welsh-speaking (the 1911 census — the earliest solid figure located in this
research pass — puts Carmarthenshire at **84.9% Welsh-speaking**, with 20.5% Welsh
monoglot, against 43.5% for Wales as a whole [S26]), while the gentry, magistrates, larger
landowners, and (increasingly) the professional and clerical/legal class conducted formal
and written business in English. Welsh Nonconformist chapel life (not researched in depth
in this pass) reinforced Welsh as the language of religion and community for the labouring
and farming population even as officialdom anglicised further.

**The Rebecca Riots (1839–1843)**, centred on west and mid Wales with Carmarthenshire as a
key focus (the first toll-gate destroyed at Yr Efail Wen, Carmarthenshire, in 1839), were
driven by agricultural hardship — poor harvests, collapsing prices, static rents alongside
rising tithes and turnpike tolls [S19]. The rioters used the Welsh name **Merched Beca**
("Rebecca's daughters") and drew on the Welsh folk-justice tradition of *ceffyl pren*
("wooden horse," a form of communal shaming ritual) [S19]. The Wikipedia account consulted
does not explicitly frame the riots as an English-speaking-authority-vs-Welsh-speaking-
farmer conflict in so many words, but the social-class and cultural-linguistic backdrop —
Welsh-speaking farming communities resisting a toll and tithe system administered in
English by largely anglicised trustees and landowners — is consistent with the wider
pattern of the era; this specific framing should be treated as a reasonable, sourced
inference rather than a directly quoted claim (flagged for care in the game's ⓘ labelling).

**The 1847 "Blue Books"** (*Reports of the Commissioners of Inquiry into the State of
Education in Wales*), commissioned after unrest including the Rebecca Riots and Chartist
activity, were compiled by three commissioners — R. R. W. Lingen, Jellynger C. Symons, and
H. R. Vaughan Johnson — all non-Welsh-speaking Anglicans, reporting via local, mostly
Anglican informants [S17]. The report made sweeping, hostile judgements about the Welsh
language, Nonconformity, and the morals of the Welsh people [S17]. It became known in Wales
as **"Brad y Llyfrau Gleision"** ("Treachery of the Blue Books"). Historian Kenneth O.
Morgan is quoted comparing its significance to "the Glencoe and the Amritsar of Welsh
history" [S17]. Its lasting effect, per this source, was to entrench a belief among
ordinary Welsh people that social advancement required English rather than Welsh — a
psychological and cultural "complex" said to persist into the modern era [S17].

**The "Welsh Not"** — a wooden token passed between children caught speaking Welsh in
school, with the child holding it at the end of a set period punished, often physically —
is well attested as a practice in Welsh schools primarily in the early-to-mid 19th century,
with evidence from around 1790 onward and declining after 1850 [S18]. However, this
document flags an important, source-based nuance: **historians disagree on how widespread
and how causally significant it actually was**. Martin Johnes's 2024 study — described as
the first comprehensive academic treatment — argues there is "little evidence to suggest
that the Welsh Not caused the decline of Welsh," partly because most children were not
attending day school at all during the period it was most common [S18]. The game should
show the Welsh Not as a real, attested practice and a powerful symbol, while being honest
that its role in causing language decline (as opposed to reflecting/enforcing an official
anglicising policy) is scholarly-contested — not settled fact.

### 20th century decline and revival

Wales-wide census figures for Welsh speakers (age 3+), cross-checked via Wikipedia's
history article against cited census reports, show a long decline: 49.9% (1891) → below
50% by 1901 → 43.5% (1911) → 38.7% (1921) → 36.8% (1931) → 26% (1961) → 18.7% (1981) →
18.7% (1991, stabilising) → 20.8% (2001, the first rise in over a century) → 19% (2011) →
17.8% (2021, 538,300 speakers) [S20]. Carmarthenshire tracked this decline from a much
higher base and with a lag, remaining majority Welsh-speaking longer than any other
historic county through most of the 20th century, with 84.9% in 1911, 82.3% in 1931, and
75.2% in 1951, before falling to minority status only in the 2011 census (43.9%) [S26].

The pivotal moment for organised revival was **Saunders Lewis's** BBC Wales radio lecture
**"Tynged yr Iaith"** ("The Fate of the Language"), broadcast 13 February 1962, which
predicted Welsh's extinction absent direct action and directly catalysed the founding of
**Cymdeithas yr Iaith Gymraeg** (the Welsh Language Society) later that year (formally
established 4 August 1962 at Pontarddulais, adopting a constitution 18 May 1963) [S21].
Sustained campaigning contributed to a sequence of legal milestones: the **Welsh Language
Act 1967** (permitting Welsh in Welsh courts); the **Welsh Language Act 1993** (royal assent
21 October 1993 — establishing "a principle of equality of Welsh and English in public
services and justice," creating the Welsh Language Board, and repealing the 1536 language
clause) [S22][S16]; and the **Welsh Language (Wales) Measure 2011**, which gave Welsh
official status for the first time and created the Welsh Language Commissioner [S20][S21].
**S4C**, the Welsh-language television channel, launched 2 November 1982 after a
high-profile campaign including a hunger-strike threat by Gwynfor Evans [S20].

## Local dialect (Carmarthenshire / Dyfed Welsh, 'Sir Gâr' features)

Carmarthenshire Welsh belongs to the traditional dialect group called **Dyfedeg**
(sometimes "Demetian," after the Demetae), one of four traditional Welsh dialect groupings,
spoken across Ceredigion, Pembrokeshire and Carmarthenshire, and historically noted as
closely connected to the neighbouring Gwenhwyseg dialect of southeast Wales [S28]. A
specific, cross-checkable feature of southern Welsh dialects, located in this research pass:
**palatalisation of "s"** next to *i* — standard *mis* ('month'), pronounced [miːs], is realised
as [miːʃ] in parts of the south [S34-Welsh-dialects][S35][S36]. *Corrected 2026-10-02:* this said
"before high front vowels" and "including this area"; the sources put the *s* after (or next to)
the *i*, and none places the feature in Carmarthenshire specifically. There is also lexical/grammatical divergence from the north in
everyday phrases: "Do you want a cuppa?" is *"Ych chi'n moyn dishgled?"* in the south of
Dyfed, versus northern *"Dach chi isio paned?"* — showing the characteristically southern
verb **moyn** ('to want'), against northern **isio**, and the southern noun **dishgled**
('cupful') [S34-Welsh-dialects]. This document did not manage, in this research pass, to
independently verify further specific vocabulary/grammar features beyond these two (an
Open Question); a named early primary source exists and should be followed up: **W.
Meredith Morris, *A Glossary of the Demetian Dialect of North Pembrokeshire (with Special
Reference to the Gwaun Valley)* (Tonypandy, 1910)** [S28], plus the modern academic
references cited by Wikipedia's Welsh dialects article, **Alan R. Thomas's *Linguistic
Geography of Wales* (1973)** and **Beth Thomas & Peter Wynn Thomas, *Cymraeg, Cymrâg,
Cymrêg: Cyflwyno'r Tafodieithoedd* (1989)** [S34-Welsh-dialects].

A specific recorded example of the living local dialect exists but was not analysed in
this pass: an audio recording of **Mrs Martha Williams of Llansawel** (born 1907),
digitised by St Fagans National History Museum and hosted by People's Collection Wales,
explicitly described as "an example" of the Carmarthenshire Welsh dialect [S33]. This is a
strong candidate for follow-up (the recording itself, if accessible, could ground actual
period-appropriate local phrasing for the 20th-century content, and is a genuinely local,
named individual rather than a generic dialect description).

## Pronunciation notes for TTS

- **A modern Welsh voice reading Old/Middle Welsh text (e.g. the Surexit memorandum) is a
  significant approximation.** Old Welsh orthography does not map cleanly onto modern Welsh
  spelling-to-sound rules (e.g. *guetig* for what became modern *wedyn*-type forms;
  *tutbulc* for modern *Tudfwlch*). The in-game ⓘ should say explicitly that a modern Welsh
  voice is being used to *read* the old spelling as best as possible, and that actual
  Old Welsh pronunciation is a matter of scholarly reconstruction, not directly recoverable
  from modern orthoepy.
- **Southern/Dyfedeg features a modern Welsh TTS voice will typically get "wrong" for this
  specific area**: standard/broadcast Welsh TTS voices are usually closer to a northern or
  neutral educated register. Two concretely sourced local features a period-farmer/labourer
  character's speech should reflect, and that a generic voice will likely miss or
  regularise: (1) palatalisation of *s* → *sh* next to *i* in casual southern speech
  (*mis* → 'mish') [S34-Welsh-dialects][S35][S36]; (2) the southern verb **moyn** for 'to want'
  and forms like **dishgled** rather than northern equivalents [S34-Welsh-dialects]; *moyn*
  cross-checked [S37] (S38 is a lead only). *Corrected 2026-10-02:* this line said "before front vowels", which its
  own example contradicts (in *mis* the *s* follows the *i*); S35 and S36 give "before or after the
  vowel *i*". None of these sources ties the feature to Carmarthenshire specifically, only to
  southern Welsh (S35: "some southern accents"; S36 describes it for Gwentian), so the app says
  "southern" and does not claim it for Llandeilo. Where
  precise phonetic control over the TTS voice is not possible, the ⓘ should flag this
  mismatch rather than silently presenting standard-Welsh-accented audio as authentically
  local.
- **Church-Latin voice choice (project decision, `it-IT-DiegoNeural`)**: this is an Italian
  voice reading plain Latin text with Italian/Church Latin pronunciation conventions
  (e.g. soft *c*/*g* before front vowels, *v* as /v/). This is a reasonable, common
  approximation for **medieval clergy** Latin, but is **anachronistic for Roman-era**
  military/administrative Latin, which by the reconstructions used in this document (see
  Latin loanwords section) retained a hard *c*/*g* and a /w/-like realisation of *v*, closer
  to Classical/Restored pronunciation than to Church Latin [S3]. Per the project's existing
  decision log, Roman-era lines already carry an ⓘ flagging this; this research confirms
  that flag is linguistically well-founded, not just a general caveat.
- **French-voice Anglo-Norman**: a modern French TTS voice reading Anglo-Norman French will
  impose modern French phonology (e.g. modern vowel qualities, loss of certain consonants)
  onto a medieval insular dialect of French that sounded distinctly different even from
  contemporary continental French. No specific reconstruction of Anglo-Norman phonology was
  researched in this pass — flag as an approximation if used.
