# Anglo-Norman French and Middle English for 1282 and 1185

Research for writing the English garrison at Dinefwr and a magnate's retinue in June 1282, and the
canons of Talley c. 1185, in the languages they actually used. Written 2026-10-02 from sources
fetched that day. The brief is in [`briefs/anglo-norman-and-middle-english.md`](briefs/anglo-norman-and-middle-english.md).
It builds on the "Anglo-Norman French and Middle English" section of
[`conversations-by-class.md`](conversations-by-class.md), cited here as `classes:Sn`.

**Method.** Most sources are full-text scholarly editions downloaded from the Internet Archive and
searched directly, so quotations come from the OCR text layer. OCR renders thorn (þ) and yogh (ȝ)
as junk such as `]?` or `^`; I have restored them where the reading is clear and flagged the rest.
WebSearch was used 8 times. The Middle English Dictionary (quod.lib.umich.edu) refused automated
access ("robot" page and HTTP 403), so **no MED entry was read** in this pass. No section was
written without search.

## Summary

- **Three languages, sorted by job, not only by class.** In 1282 the records of the war are Latin
  (pay-rolls, writs) [S1][S3]; letters between commanders and officials are Anglo-Norman French
  [S2] (and Peckham's, `classes:S4`); the ordinary English soldier's speech was English
  (inference, see point 1). **Cross-checked.**
- **The knights were probably English speakers who also used French.** Two scholars working 70
  years apart read Walter of Bibbesworth's French primer for gentry children as showing that
  English was the gentry's first language by the later 13th century [S9][S11]. A third argues
  French was still acquired naturally, in schools and noble households, until about 1400 [S12].
  Robert of Gloucester, writing c. 1300 in Gloucestershire, says "heiemen" keep French and "lowe
  men" keep English [S4]. All agree knights could speak both; they disagree on which came first.
- **Real lines exist for both languages and both are local enough.** Two Anglo-Norman letters by
  commanders in Wales survive in print: William de Valence (whose son was killed at Llandeilo) in
  1278, and Roger Lestrange announcing Llywelyn's death on 11 December 1282 [S2]. For Middle
  English, Robert of Gloucester's chronicle (c. 1300, Gloucestershire dialect) gives speech,
  greetings and a famous passage on French and English [S4].
- **The right Middle English is south-western.** Plukenet's horse at Dinefwr were Somerset and
  Devon men [S1], and masons and carpenters from Wiltshire, Somerset and Dorset were sent to
  Carmarthen and Dinefwr in 1277 [S1]. Their English voiced initial *f* and *s* as *v* and *z*
  [S13], which the texts show (*vor* "for", *vaire* "fair") [S4][S5][S6]. **Cross-checked.**
- **Talley's canons spoke Picard, not Anglo-Norman** (inference). Talley's mother house was St-Jean
  at Amiens (`classes:S13`, `classes:S15`), in Picardy. Old Picard kept *k* where Paris French had
  *ch* (*keval* for *cheval*) [S17]. So "Anglo-Norman for the Talley canons" in the design outline
  should read "Old French, probably Picard".
- **The voices will be approximations, and must say so.** A modern French voice will drop final
  consonants and final *-e*, nasalise *-aun*, use a uvular *r* and read *oi* as "wa"; a modern
  English voice will shift every long vowel and drop *gh*, *k* in *kn-* and the final *-e*. edge-tts
  escapes its input, so only respelling can steer it [S19].

## 1. Who spoke what in 1282

### The groups near Llandeilo

- **The garrison at Dinefwr.** After the rout of 16 or 17 June, about thirty-five lances stayed
  "under Alan Plukenet to hold Dynevor" [S1, p. 166]. "Plukenet's corps was already composed of
  Somerset and Devon tenants" [S1, p. 167]. The troop leaders were "tenants-in-chief from the
  south-west counties of England" [S1, p. 163]. Crossbowmen and English foot "were clearly in
  permanent pay in the castles" [S1, p. 165]. Already in `classes:S1`; re-read today.
  **Single-source** (Morris, built on the pay-roll), but consistent with point 5: the same families
  (Courtenay, Mohun, Beauchamp, Montacute) hold land in the 1327 Somerset tax roll [S16].
- **Workmen.** In 1277 "masons and carpenters from Wiltshire, Somerset, and Dorset were similarly
  sent to Carmarthen and Dynevor" [S1, p. 139]. **Single-source.**
- **The foot were mostly Welsh.** In September 1282 "the 700 Kidwelly men and seventy English"
  joined an attack, and Morris says the Welsh and the English foot were paid at different rates for
  it [S1, p. 170; the OCR garbles the pennies, so the amounts are not given here]. So English
  speakers were a small part of any force here (`classes:S1`, `classes:S9`).
- **Others later, not in June.** "A large corps of 1,500" Gascons came "in the winter of 1282-3"
  [S1, pp. 35–36]; Langtoft also names "Baskles e Gascons" [S8, vol. 2]. Do not put Gascon or Basque
  speakers at Dinefwr in June 1282.

### What each class spoke

| Group | Everyday speech | Writing | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| English foot, crossbowmen, workmen (Somerset, Devon, Wilts, Dorset) | South-western Middle English | Usually none | Inference; strong |
| Knights and troop leaders (Plukenet's men) | English and French; which first is disputed | French for letters | Cross-checked that both were used |
| Magnates (de Valence, Gloucester, Lestrange) | French and English | French letters [S2] | Cross-checked |
| Clerks, paymasters | As their class, plus Latin | Latin rolls [S1][S3] | Cross-checked |
| Welsh levies and townsfolk | Welsh | Latin, Welsh | See `classes:` |

Evidence for each line:

- **Latin for records.** The pay-roll Morris used is a Latin roll: the English foot at Carmarthen in
  1287 are "retenti ad vadia regis" (retained at the king's wages) [S1, p. 207], and at the 1287
  siege of Newcastle Emlyn the roll pays £10 "ad opus peditum ex Cardigan qui permanserunt in
  obsessione novi castri sine vadiis" (for the foot of Cardigan who stayed at the siege of the new
  castle without wages) [S1, pp. 216–217]. The Welsh Rolls are calendared from Latin writs (Tibetot appointed "captain
  in West Wales", 1282) [S3, pp. 212–213]. **Cross-checked** (two record series).
- **French for letters between the great.** William de Valence to his steward, 1278, and Roger
  Lestrange to Edward I, December 1282, are both in French [S2]. Peckham wrote to the king in
  French in December 1282 (`classes:S4`). The Anglo-Norman Dictionary lists the two 1278 and 1282
  letters as an Anglo-Norman source text, "Two Docs Wales" [S15]. **Cross-checked.**
- **English as a mother tongue, even for the gentry** (one reading). Menger (1904): "French never
  became the language of the populace"; Bibbesworth's treatise "for the benefit of children of the
  aristocracy who might wish to learn French ... points toward English and not French as the mother
  tongue of the English aristocracy of the time" [S9, pp. 1–2]. Rothwell (1976): "French was a
  language needing to be learned by very many Englishmen, not a vernacular in almost universal use
  in England at the opening of the thirteenth century" [S11]. Bibbesworth himself writes "the
  proper way to speak and answer that every gentleman needs to know", so the children will "not
  [be] made fun of by others" [S10, Dalby's translation].
- **A different reading.** Ingham (2009) calls the "second, acquired language" view an "internet
  myth", and says French was acquired naturally: "English speakers used French at school until c.
  mid-C14 as a vehicle language through which to learn Latin", and "French was frequently used in
  aristocratic households and court at least until c. 1400; knights/gentry often sent children to
  noble households" [S12]. He also says Bibbesworth "teaches lexis, not syntax" [S12].
- **A contemporary's view, c. 1300.** Robert of Gloucester: the "heiemen" (high men) keep the French
  the Normans brought, "for unless a man knows French, people think little of him", while "lowe men"
  keep to English; but "it is good to know both" [S4, vol. 2, lines 7540–7547]. Full text under
  point 2.
- **English speakers called themselves English even when writing French.** Langtoft, a canon of
  Bridlington who wrote his chronicle in French verse, writes of the 1282 war "si nos Englays
  eusent eu espye / Entre les Galais" (if we English had had a spy among the Welsh) [S8, vol. 2].

**Contradiction, side by side.** Menger and Rothwell: French was by then a learned, second language,
even for the gentry. Ingham: it was still acquired naturally in childhood by the gentry. Robert of
Gloucester: the high men "hold" French. For writing, the difference matters little: a knight
of 1282 can speak either, and should speak French in formal or written matters and to a magnate,
and English to his own men. *The choice per line is ours, and is labelled imagined.*

**Bibbesworth's date is uncertain.** Menger: "toward the end of the thirteenth century" [S9];
Rothwell: "the later years of the thirteenth century" [S11]; a text quoted (and in other respects
rebutted) by Ingham gives "1250x1260" [S12]; a search summary gave "mid-13th century" (not relied
on). Unresolved here.

## 2. Attested phrases

All translations are mine unless stated. Spelling is the edition's; where the OCR is unsure I say
so. **None of these was said at Llandeilo**: each is a real line from the period, usable as a
quote with its source in the ⓘ.

### Anglo-Norman French

**William de Valence to Henry de Bray, 1278** (about the building of Llanbadarn castle; Valence was
the father of the young William de Valence killed in the Tywi valley in June 1282) [S2, pp. 506–507]:

- *"Gillame de Valence seynur de Pembrok a soun cher amy mester Henri de Bray seneschal de
  Bergeveny saluz e amour."* "William de Valence, lord of Pembroke, to his dear friend Master Henry
  de Bray, steward of Abergavenny, greeting and love." The standard letter opening.
- *"Sachez ke jo ay este a Lampadirvawr e ay veu le ouerayne"* "Know that I have been at Llanbadarn
  Fawr and have seen the work".
- *"les deners le Roy ke la sunt sunt ja tot fayliz"* "the king's money that is there is already all
  spent".
- *"jo vous pri e le vous maund de part le Roy"* "I pray you and command you on the king's behalf".
- *"saunz nul delay"* "without any delay"; *"pur le pru le Roy"* "for the king's profit".
- Closing: *"Saluz ke de vous"* (OCR; probably "Saluz [e] de vous", sense uncertain).

**Roger Lestrange to Edward I, after 11 December 1282** (the official despatch on Llywelyn's death)
[S2, p. 507]:

- *"A son trenoble seignur Edward par la grace Deu Roy de Engleterre, seignur de Yrlaund, e Duc de
  Guyene, Rog[er] le Estraunge si li plest saluz honurs et reverences"* "To his most noble lord
  Edward, by the grace of God king of England, lord of Ireland and duke of Guyenne, Roger Lestrange,
  if it please him, greeting, honours and reverence."
- *"Sachez, sire, ke vos bones gens ... se combatirent av Leweln le finz Griffin en le paes de
  Buelt"* "Know, sire, that your good men ... fought with Llywelyn ap Gruffudd in the land of
  Builth".
- *"issi ke Leweln le finz Griffin est mort et se gent desconfit et tote la flour de se gent morz"*
  "so that Llywelyn ap Gruffudd is dead and his people routed and all the flower of his people
  dead".
- *"sicum le port[our] de ceste lettre vus dirra, et le creez de ceo ke il vus dirra de par moi"*
  "as the bearer of this letter will tell you; and believe him in what he tells you from me". The
  OCR has "port®" and "pchein" (for *prochein*, "next"); the expansions are mine.

**Langtoft's chronicle on the war of 1282** (French verse, written by a Yorkshire canon before 1307)
[S8, vol. 2, pp. 176–178]:

- *"Grevouse est la guère, e dure l'endurer; / Quant [aillours] est l'esté, en Gales est yver."*
  "Grievous is the war, and hard to endure: when it is summer elsewhere, in Wales it is winter."
  (Accents and brackets are the 1868 editor's.)
- *"Courent sur les genz, ardent en passaunt, / Abbatent ses chasteus"* "They fall on the people,
  burn as they pass, throw down his castles" (of the Welsh in 1282).

**Speech in an Anglo-Norman romance** (*Boeve de Haumtone*, first half of the 13th century by Menger's
dating; text from Stimming's edition as printed by Menger) [S9, pp. 26, 161–162]:

- *"Beau sire emperur"* "Fair lord emperor"; *"Fol, kar vus teisez!"* "Fool, be quiet!"
- *"Dount venez vus, beau fiz, si fortement hastaunt?"* "Where are you coming from, fair son, in
  such a hurry?" (the OCR reads "yus" for *vus*).
- *"par le men ascient"* "to my knowledge, I swear"; *"jure par dampnedeu"* "swears by the Lord God".

**A 1292 petition** (quoted by Ingham) [S12]: *"E pout adunke aver change sy yl vousit, e ne fit nent"*
"And he could then have changed if he wished, and did nothing".

**Archbishop Peckham, December 1282 and 1283**: *"Sire, sachez ke ..."*, *"Madame, je vus mercy mut
des lettres"*, *"Madame, Dieu vous eyt en sa garde"*. Read and verified in the earlier pass
(`classes:S4`); not re-read today.

**Military words in the Anglo-Norman Dictionary** [S14]: *arblaster* (crossbowman; forms include
*arbelastier*, *arblastier*); *garnisun* (provisions, and also "garrison, body of soldiers stationed
in a castle"); *centener* and, in the same 1336 citation, *vinteners* (commanders of a hundred and of
twenty). Morris uses "centenar" and "vintenar" for the same ranks [S1, pp. 88, 92]. **Cross-checked.**

### Middle English (south-western and West Midland)

**Robert of Gloucester, *Metrical Chronicle*, c. 1300**, "the dialect of Gloucestershire" by the
editor's judgement, the Cotton manuscript "nearly contemporary" [S4, vol. 1, pp. xi–xii]. On French
and English, vol. 2, lines 7537–7547 (pp. 543–544; thorn and yogh restored from OCR):

> Þus com lo engelond • in to normandies hond •
> & þe normans ne couþe speke þo • bote hor owe speche •
> & speke french as hii dude atom • & hor children dude also teche •
> So þat heiemen of þis lond • þat of hor blod come •
> Holdeþ alle þulke speche • þat hii of hom nome •
> Vor bote a man conne frenss • me telþ of him lute •
> Ac lowe men holdeþ to engliss • & to hor owe speche ȝute •
> Ich wene þer ne beþ in al þe world • contreyes none •
> Þat ne holdeþ to hor owe speche • bote engelond one •
> Ac wel me wot uor to conne • boþe wel it is •
> Vor þe more þat a mon can • þe more wurþe he is •

"Thus, lo, England came into Normandy's hand, and the Normans could then speak only their own
speech, and spoke French as they did at home, and had their children taught it too, so that the high
men of this land who came of their blood keep all that same speech they took from them. For unless a
man knows French, people think little of him. But low men keep to English and to their own speech
still. I think there are no countries in all the world that do not keep to their own speech, except
England alone. But it is well known that it is good to know both, for the more a man knows, the more
he is worth."

Short lines from the same chronicle:

- **A drinking greeting.** Vol. 1, lines 2514–2519 (p. 179): *"Louerd king washayl"* ("Lord king,
  wassail", i.e. be in health); the king answers *"Drink hail"*. The OCR has "lo sede" for the
  speaker ("she said"); the variants give *heo*, *sche*. **Cross-checked** with Laȝamon (below).
- **Speech before battle, Evesham 1265** (the writer says he saw the darkness that day), vol. 2,
  lines 11,701–11,703 (p. 763): *"Vr soules he sede abbe god • vor vr bodies beþ hore"* "God have our
  souls, he said, for our bodies are theirs"; *"Sir henri he sede to is sone • þis haþ imad þi prute"*
  "Sir Henry, he said to his son, your pride has done this". These are Robert's words put in Simon de
  Montfort's mouth thirty-five years on, not a record of what Simon (who was born in France) said.
- *"An vewe dropes of reine • þer velle grete inou"* "A few drops of rain fell there, big enough"
  (quoted in vol. 1, p. xi, from line 11,746): weather talk with the south-western *v*.

**Laȝamon's *Brut*** (by a priest at "Ernleȝe", Lower Arley on the Severn in Worcestershire; Madden
argued for a date about 1205), vol. 2, vv. 14,295–14,342, p. 174 onward [S5]:

- *"Lauerd king wæs hæil, for þine kime ich æm uæin"* "Lord king, wassail; for your coming I am
  glad".
- *"Leoue freond wæs hail ... þe oþer seið drinc hail"* "'Dear friend, wassail!' The other says
  'drink hail!'" (Madden's translation gives "Dear friend, wassail! ... Drinchail!").

**The Owl and the Nightingale** (southern; Wells accepted "about 1220" but noted the debate) [S6,
lines 1–4 and 19]: *"Ich was in one sumere dale, / in one suþe diȝele hale, / iherde ich holde grete
tale / an hule and one niȝtingale"* "I was in a summer valley, in a very hidden nook; I heard an owl
and a nightingale hold a great debate". And *"Ho was þe gladur uor þe rise"* "She was the gladder for
the branch".

**A soldiers' and partisans' song, after Lewes (1264)**, copied in the Ludlow manuscript Harley 2253
(West Midland, c. 1340) [S7, pp. 69–70]: *"Sitteth alle stille ant herkneth to me"* "Sit all still and
listen to me" (the OCR reads "rue"); *"bi mi leauté"* "by my loyalty" (an oath with a French word);
*"Let him habbe, ase he brew, bale to dryng"* "Let him have, as he brewed, sorrow to drink". The copy
is about 75 years after the song and its spelling (*ant* for *and*) is the Ludlow scribe's.

**Not found:** any Middle English sentence written in south-west Wales in the 1280s, or any order or
oath given in English by an English soldier there. The English lines above are from Gloucestershire,
Worcestershire and the south, and are the nearest real models.

## 3. Writing new lines: spelling and grammar

### Anglo-Norman of the 1280s

Use the forms of the 1278 and 1282 letters [S2] as the model, since they are the nearest in time,
place and class. What they and the descriptions show:

- **Spelling.** *ke* for *que*, *ki* for *qui* ("k for the [k] sound spelt qu", [S18]); *u* for *o* or
  *ou* (*sunt*, *mun*, *nus*, *vus*; "u for SMF o or ou", [S18]); *-aun-* before a nasal and consonant
  (*saunz*, *graund*, *maund*, *Estraunge*, *Yrlaund*). Menger dates *aun* to "the second part of the
  thirteenth century", the earliest dated example 1266 [S9, pp. 47–48], so it is right for 1282.
  **Cross-checked** (description and letters).
- **ei and e, not oi.** Insular French reduced *ai* and *ei* to *e* (*faire/fere*) [S18], and Menger
  says *ei, ai, e* are "the real Anglo-Norman products" while *oi* "was an imitation of continental
  usage" [S9, p. 50]. The letters write *seyt*, *seyent*, *esteyt*. **Cross-checked.**
- **Pronouns and verbs.** *jo* (Valence), *je* (Peckham to the queen), *vus/vous*, *nus*; first person
  plural in *-um/-uns/-oms* (*nus priums*, *savums* in Peckham; "-om -um -oun and -oms ... for SMF
  -ons", [S18]).
- **Letter formulae.** Opening: *[name] a son cher amy [name] saluz e amour*; to a superior: *A son
  trenoble seignur ... saluz honurs et reverences*; body: *Sachez ke ...*; requests *jo vous pri*;
  close *Dieu vous eyt en sa garde*.
- **Address in speech.** *Sire*, *Beau sire*, *beau fiz*, *Madame* [S9][S2].
- **Grammar.** The old two-case system was breaking down early in England ("the reduction of cases",
  "the early adoption of the accusative for the nominative") [S9, pp. 2, 114]. Word order allowed
  object before verb more often than modern French (Ingham's tables for continental French: 36% of
  auxiliated clauses c. 1275, 18% c. 1300) [S12]. Perfect infinitives after a modal are normal: *pout
  aver change* "could have changed" [S12].
- **Avoid**: modern spellings (*que*, *qui*, *nous*, *oi* for *ei*, *-ons* for *-um*), modern words,
  and modern French grammar such as dropping *ne*. (Valence does write *ne pount pas estre*, so *ne
  ... pas* itself is period.) Menger warns there was no stable standard and each writer varied [S9,
  p. 3], so mild inconsistency is authentic.

### South-western Middle English of c. 1280–1300

Use Robert of Gloucester [S4] as the model: right date, right region, the right social picture.

- **Pronouns and verbs seen in the texts**: *ich* (I) [S4][S5][S6]; *hii* or *hi* (they), *hor*
  (their), *hom* (them) [S4]; *heo* or *ho* (she) [S5][S6]; plural present in *-eþ* (*holdeþ*,
  *beþ*) [S4]; *me* as "one, people" (*me telþ*, "people reckon") [S4]; past participles with *i-*
  (*ido*, *ibrott*, *isei*, *imad*, *islawe*) [S4]; negative *ne* (*ne couþe*) [S4].
- **The south-western v and z.** Wright: "The initial voiceless spirants f, s, þ became the voiced
  spirants v, z, ð ... in Kentish and the southern, especially the south-western dialects, as vader,
  vat, vlesch, vrend; zaule ... zinne", still alive in "Glo., ... Wil., Som., and Dev." and in "s.
  Pem." [S13, § 236]. The texts write it: *vor*, *uor* "for", *vaire* "fair", *vewe* "few", *velle*
  "fell" [S4][S6]; *uæin* "fain" [S5]. **Cross-checked.** Spell a Somerset soldier's "for" as *vor*.
- **Rounded vowels.** Wright lists front rounded vowels among Middle English sounds, with spellings
  such as *horte* (heart), *huden* (hide), *fur* (fire) [S13, § 23]. Which dialects kept them was not
  checked in this pass, so do not rely on them for Somerset.
- **Words for the scene** (all seen in [S4] or [S5]): *louerd* (lord), *king*, *knitt* ([S4]) or
  *cniht* ([S5]) (knight),
  *wassail/drinc hail*, *engelond*, *engliss*, *frenss* (French), *Walssemen* or *Walische* (Welsh,
  from the editor's glossary of [S4]).
- **Word order.** Mostly verb before object by now: the Penn-Helsinki corpus has about 3% OV in
  1250–1350 (Pintzuk and Taylor, as quoted by Ingham) [S12].
- **Avoid Chaucer's London English of a century later**: *I* for *ich*, *they/their/them* for
  *hii/hor/hom*, plural *-en* (*they holden*) for *-eþ*, *for* for *vor*, and silent final *-e*.
  Avoid the northern taunt-rhymes of 1296 in Langtoft [S8] as models: wrong region.

## 4. Pronunciation and the voices

### Anglo-Norman read by `fr-FR-HenriNeural` / `DeniseNeural`

What the period sound was, and what the voice will do instead:

- **Final -e** was sounded [ə] (`classes:S39`); the voice drops it, so *lettre*, *grevouse*, *guere*
  lose a syllable. Single-source for the period value (Wikipedia, read in the earlier pass).
- **Final consonants.** The earlier note quotes Old French as pronouncing all written final
  consonants "except for s preceding non-stop consonants and t in et" (`classes:S39`). **Menger
  contradicts this for Anglo-Norman**: "Final t lost. This is the most frequent of all the
  phenomena", from the earliest texts (*es* = *est*, *ai* = *ait*), and "the value of final s ...
  apparently, was not stable" [S9, pp. 97, 114]. Both are recorded; the ⓘ should say final consonants
  were "often" sounded, not "always".
- **ch and j** were [tʃ] and [dʒ] (`classes:S37`, `classes:S39`); the voice says [ʃ] and [ʒ]. Menger
  confirms the three sounds k, ch and ts were all used and spelt variously [S9, p. 98].
- **-aun-**: its sound "has not been definitely established" [S9, p. 48]; the voice will give the
  modern nasal [ɑ̃]. The earlier note gives *soun* as once like "soon" (`classes:S37`).
- **ei** was a diphthong or [e], never "wa" [S9][S18]; the voice reads *oi* as "wa" and *ei* as [ɛ].
- **r** was trilled (`classes:S39`); the voice uses the uvular r.
- **Honest ⓘ text (draft):** "Modern French voice. In 1282 Anglo-Norman French was spoken in
  England with its own accent: final -e was sounded, ch was said as in English 'church', r was
  rolled, and many final consonants were still heard."

### Middle English read by `en-GB-RyanNeural` / `SoniaNeural`

- **Long vowels before the Great Vowel Shift.** Wright's values: long *i* "like the i in NE.
  machine" (*tide*, *finden*); long *u*, written *ou*, "like the ou in Fr. sou" (*hous*, *doun*);
  long *a* "like the a in NE. father" (*name*); close long *e* "like the e in NHG. reh" [S13, § 23].
  **Cross-checked** with `classes:S40`. The voice will say *time*, *house*, *name* in modern form.
- **gh and h.** *ȝ/gh* finally and before *t* was "a voiceless guttural or palatal spirant like the ch
  in NHG. noch beside ich" (*doughter*, *fighten*); initial *h* was pronounced [S13, § 24]. **Cross-
  checked** with `classes:S40`, `classes:S46`. The voice drops it in *knight*, *fought*.
- **Final -e was still sounded in the south in 1282.** Wright: it was lost earliest in the north
  ("by about the middle of the thirteenth century"), "latest of all in the southern dialects"; "In
  the southern dialects the final -e ceased to be pronounced in all forms in the second half of the
  fourteenth century" [S13, § 141]. **Cross-checked** with `classes:S46` ("By Chaucer's time, this
  vowel was silent in normal speech"). This firms up the earlier note's inference.
- **kn-, wr-** were sounded (`classes:S46`); not re-checked in Wright.
- **v and z** for initial f and s in Somerset speech, as above [S13, § 236].

**Will an en-GB voice manage Middle English text?** Not tested by ear in this pass. What was tested
(2026-10-02, edge-tts 7.2.8, word-boundary events): the voice emits a word for every token, including
words spelt with þ and æ, so it does not skip them, but how it sounds þ is unknown [S19]. edge-tts
escapes the text into its own SSML (`escape(...)` in `communicate.py`), so **phoneme tags cannot be
passed; respelling is the only lever** [S19].

**Respelling advice (inference, to be tried by ear like the Latin voice was):**

- Always transliterate þ and ð to *th*, ȝ to *y* (initially) or *gh*, æ to *a*.
- For an en-GB voice, write the old vowels in modern spellings: *hous* → "hoos", *tide* → "teeduh",
  *name* → "nahmuh", *wassail* → "wass-ahl". Write final *-e* as "-uh" where it should be heard.
- *gh* has no good en-GB respelling; "ch" makes the voice say [tʃ]. Leave it silent and say so in
  the ⓘ, or test a German-voice clip (an option, not researched).
- Respelling text shown on screen would mislead: show the real spelling, voice the respelling, and
  say so (a data-model question, see open questions).
- **Honest ⓘ text (draft):** "Modern English voice reading 1280s English from the south-west. Then,
  'house' sounded like 'hoose', 'time' like 'teem', the final e was spoken, the gh in 'knight' was a
  rasp like Welsh ch, and Somerset men said 'vor' for 'for'."

## 5. Names for imagined characters

### English soldiers and workmen (Somerset and the south-west)

From the Somerset tax roll of 1327 (Exchequer Lay Subsidy 169/5, printed in *Kirby's Quest*), 45
years after 1282 but the nearest large list of ordinary Somerset men found [S16]. Raw counts of
Latin first names in the OCR text (ablative forms; approximate, and including a few women and
preface mentions): *Johanne* (John) 2,438; *Willelmo* (William) 1,628; *Roberto* 823; *Richardo*
about 790; *Thoma* 629; *Waltero* 616; *Henrico* 360; *Rogero* 337; *Adam* 323; *Nicholao* 294;
*Hugone* 170; *Galfrido* (Geoffrey) 142; *Radulpho* (Ralph) about 136; *Stephano* 104; *Philippo* 98;
*Simone* 78. **Single-source**; John and William far ahead is the clear pattern.

Bynames seen in the same roll: trade or office names with *le* (*le Hopere*, *le Webbe*, *le Baker*,
*le Smyth*, *le Taillour*, *le Reve*, *le Hayward*, *le Muleward*, *le Touker*, *le Carpenter*,
*le Clerk*, *le Knyght*); nicknames (*le White*, *le Yonge*, *le Lange*, *le Kyng*); and place names
with *atte* (*atte Mulle*, *atte Brouke*, *atte Wykes*, *atte Grene*, *atte Stone*). The editor's own
favourites: *Hugh le Honylikkere*, *Thomas le Otemangere*, *Hugh le Blodleter*, *Adam Puddyng*,
*William le Rat* [S16, pp. xxxi–xxxii]. In the documents of 1282 such men are written in Latin or
French, so an English soldier called "Watte atte Mulle" by his mates is "Walterus atte Mulle" on the
roll (inference).

Soldiers' bynames of the period: *Peter le Arblaster* (1278) and *Geoffrey Arbalister* (1198) are
cited by the Anglo-Norman Dictionary as surnames from "crossbowman" [S14]. Stephen de Frankton, the
man said to have killed Llywelyn, was "a centenar of Shropshire infantry from l'Estrange's own estate
of Ellesmere, Frankton being a village in the neighbourhood" [S2, p. 506; S1, p. 184]: a real example
of a foot officer named from his home village. **Single author** (Morris, in two works).

### Knights and their men

Plukenet's troop leaders in 1282 [S1, p. 163]: Geoffrey de Camville, Alan Plukenet, Hugh de
Courtenay, John de Beauchamp, Ralph Daubeny, Nicholas de Montfort, William de Mohun, Henry de
l'Ortiay (as the OCR reads it), Simon de Montacute, Hugh Pointz, John de Cogan; with Robert de Mohun in June,
and later "a Grenville, a Raleigh, a Marmion" [S1, p. 168]. These are **real people**: do not give
them invented words. For imagined knights, use the same pattern: a common first name (Geoffrey,
Hugh, John, Ralph, Nicholas, William, Henry, Simon, Robert, Philip) and *de* + a south-western
manor, or a family byname. Several of these families still held Somerset land in 1327 (Hugh de
Courtenay at Crewkerne, William de Montague, John de Mohun, John de Beauchamp) [S16, p. xxxii],
which supports Morris's "Somerset and Devon tenants". **Cross-checked.**

## The canons of Talley, c. 1185

- **Where they came from.** Talley was founded by the Lord Rhys "as a dependency of Amiens, St Jean"
  (Monastic Wales) and "was founded from the monastery of St. John's, Amiens" (VCH Notts) (`classes:S13`,
  `classes:S15`, both read in the earlier pass). **Cross-checked.**
- **So their French was not Anglo-Norman.** Amiens is in Picardy, and *Amiénois* is listed as a
  Picard dialect [S17]. Old Picard kept *k* and *g* where central Old French had [tʃ] and [dʒ]:
  "Picard *keval* ~ Old French *cheval*", "*gambe* ~ *jambe*", "*kief* ~ *chef*", and Picard *cachier*
  for *chacier* (to hunt) [S17]. Menger notes the "graphic signs of Central French and of South
  Norman by the side of those of Picard and North Norman" in Anglo-Norman manuscripts, by
  "immigrants from different provinces of France" [S9, p. 98], which confirms the distinction exists
  but not these words. **Single-source** for the Picard details (Wikipedia, a lead); that the canons
  spoke Picard is **inference** from their mother house.
- **Liturgy in Latin** and dealings with tenants in Welsh, as in `classes:`. Whether the founding
  community was all from Amiens, or soon recruited locally, is not known (`classes:` open questions).
- **Voice.** The same `fr-FR` voice, with an ⓘ saying it is a modern stand-in for 12th-century
  northern French.

## Contradictions, side by side

| Question | One source | Another | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Was French the gentry's first language c. 1280? | No: learned, English was the mother tongue (Menger [S9]; Rothwell [S11]) | Acquired naturally in school and household until c. 1400 (Ingham [S12]); high men "hold" French (Robert of Gloucester [S4]) | Open; write knights as bilingual |
| Were final consonants pronounced in Anglo-Norman? | All written finals pronounced, with two exceptions (Old French, `classes:S39`) | Final t "most frequently" lost; final s "not stable" (Menger [S9]) | Open; say "often" |

## Open questions

- **The Calendar of Ancient Correspondence concerning Wales** (J. G. Edwards, 1935) was found only as
  catalogue records; its text, with any letters from Tibetot, Plukenet or the Dinefwr constable in
  1282, was not read. It is the most likely source of more local Anglo-Norman.
- **The Middle English Dictionary** blocked automated access, so no MED entry (e.g. *arblaster*,
  *wassail*) was checked.
- **No Middle English from south-west Wales of the 1280s**: still none found (as in `classes:`).
  South Pembrokeshire kept the south-western *v/z* into Wright's day [S13], which hints at the
  settlers' speech there, but nothing contemporary was read.
- **How the voices sound**: an A/B listening test by Dewi (plain spelling against respelling, for
  both languages), as was done for Latin. Not done.
- **Show one spelling, voice another?** If respelling is used, the content model needs a separate
  "voiced text" field, so the screen shows the real spelling. A design decision for Dewi.
- **Picard**: a scholarly description of Old Picard (e.g. Gossen's grammar) was not read; the Picard
  forms rest on Wikipedia.
- **Dates of the model texts**: the Owl and the Nightingale's date is debated (Wells's c. 1220 was not
  checked against modern work); Laȝamon's c. 1205 is Madden's.
- **Gloucestershire men later**: John Giffard of Brimpsfield (Gloucestershire), who held Carreg
  Cennen from 1283, is named in the preface to Robert of Gloucester [S4, vol. 1]; his men would make
  Robert's dialect apt for 1283–87 too. Not researched further.
