# Ripples from afar

Research for the "Ripples from afar" decision in `CLAUDE.md`: distant events (eruptions, pandemics,
famines, a nuclear accident) and the evidence that their effects reached this valley. Written
2026-10-02 from sources fetched that day. The brief is in
[`briefs/ripples-from-afar.md`](briefs/ripples-from-afar.md).

**The rule this note applies.** A distant event is linked to Llandeilo only where a source records
the effect here. Otherwise it is shown at the level the sources reach, and labelled as such.

**Levels of evidence** (where the effect is recorded): *here* (inside about 10 miles of Llandeilo,
including Ffairfach, Trap, Cwmdu, Ammanford, Dryslwyn); *county* (Carmarthenshire, mostly Carmarthen
town, about 14 miles away); *region* (south-west or south Wales, or a medieval region that includes
this valley, such as Ystrad Tywi); *Wales*; *Britain* (England or Britain, nothing Welsh); *none*.

**Tiers** (what the app may show): *documented* (a source records the effect at that level);
*reconstructed* (inferred by analogy, with the analogy named); *regional only* (show it as a
ripple card naming where it is recorded, not as an effect in the valley scene); *not supportable*
(show nothing, or say "we don't know").

Citations of the form [victorian:S54] point into other research notes; bare [S1] points into this
note's list. Distances are rough great-circle figures from the volcano's usual coordinates, for
orientation only.

Independently checked 2026-10-02 (see [reviews/ripples-from-afar-check-2026-10-02.md](reviews/ripples-from-afar-check-2026-10-02.md)); corrections applied.

## Summary

- **Evidence here (inside the radius): three.** The 1849 cholera epidemic (a few cases in the town
  and its neighbourhood, by a gap-filled reading of the OCR, and a subscription to fit up a house for destitute patients) [S24]; the
  1918 influenza (all Llandeilo schools and Sunday schools shut, two of the three doctors ill,
  chapels nearly empty, deaths in Llandeilo, Ffairfach and Trap, schools reopened by about 21
  November) [S36][S37][S38][S39][S40][S43]; and, weakly, one man's influenza in January 1848 in
  Thomas Jenkins's diary, which no source ties to the European epidemic of that winter [S42].
- **Evidence at county or regional level only: eight.** The Black Death of 1349 (the Carmarthen
  district "suffered severely"; "serious ravages throughout the commotes of Ystrad Towy", which
  include this valley, but no Llandeilo record) [S9]; the 1816 "year without a summer" (Carmarthen
  hay ruined by floods, nearly seventy days of rain reported in Swansea (the paper likened the season
  to 1692, quoting Burnet's "a very cold and wet summer" about that year; *Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*), high
  Carmarthen wheat prices,
  and a harvest that the same paper called abundant) [S16][S18][S19][S21]; potato blight (Carmarthen
  1846) [S29]; cholera at Carmarthen and Ystalyfera [S25][S26][victorian:S54]; the 1865 to 1866
  cattle plague (fear and precautions) [S31][S32]; Krakatoa's red afterglows (English and Welsh
  skies, late 1883) [S33][S34][S35]; the Yellow Plague of 547 (Wales, with the Teilo legend as the
  only local thread) [S1][S2][S5]; and the 1790s corn riots at Carmarthen, which are not one distant
  event [S14].
- **No evidence found in Wales: six.** The 536 dust veil as weather (only "mortality in Britain and
  Ireland" in 537) [S1][S3]; Samalas 1257 [S6][S7]; the Great Famine 1315 to 1317 [S8]; Huaynaputina
  1600 [S10]; the frosts of 1683 to 1684 and 1739 to 1740 [S11]; Laki 1783 (well recorded in England,
  nothing Welsh read) [S12]. Chernobyl 1986 reached Welsh sheep, but the restricted areas named were
  in north Wales [S41].

## Detail per event

### 536 to 541: the dust veil, and "mortality" in 537

**Distant event.** Tree rings from the Russian Altai and the European Alps show "an unprecedented,
long-lasting and spatially synchronized cooling following a cluster of large volcanic eruptions in
536, 540 and 547 AD", which the authors call the Late Antique Little Ice Age (536 to about 660) [S3].
The chronologies are not British. The size of the cooling was challenged in a published comment
("Limited Late Antique cooling") that the authors answered; that exchange was seen only as a title
in search results and was not read.

**Evidence in Wales.** The *Annales Cambriae* (A text, Harleian MS 3859, Phillimore's 1888 edition)
put at the year Phillimore reckons as 537: "Gueith cam lann inqua arthur et medraut corruerunt. et
mortalitas" [S2], translated "The battle of Camlann, in which Arthur and Medraut fell: and there was
plague in Britain and Ireland" [S1]. The annals' years are putative, added by the editor [S2].
Nothing in them describes cold, dark skies or failed harvests in the 530s. An entry at 447 says
"Days as dark as night" [S1], far too early to be the 536 veil; it is a later-text addition (‡ in S1),
not in the Harleian A text [S2] (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*).

**What the app could show.** Nothing in the valley's weather. A ripple card can say: a cold decade
is recorded in tree rings far away, and the Welsh annals record a mortality in Britain at about this
time. Tier: not supportable (cold), regional only (mortality). Single-source for each strand.

### 547: the "great mortality" and the Yellow Plague (Y Fad Felen)

**Distant event.** The first plague pandemic began as the Justinianic Plague in 541. Ancient DNA
gives "genetic evidence for the presence of the Justinianic Plague in the British Isles, previously
only hypothesized from ambiguous documentary accounts" [S4]. The abstract does not name the British
site; Wales is not mentioned.

**Evidence in Wales.** *Annales Cambriae*, at Phillimore's 547: "Mortalitas magna inqua pausat
mailcun rex genedotae" [S2] ("The great death in which Maelgwn, king of Gwynedd died") [S1]. That is
Gwynedd, in the north. *Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: the later texts of the annals (marked ‡ in S1 as not in the
Harleian MS) add at 547: "Thus they say 'The long sleep of Maelgwn in the court of Rhos'. Then was
the yellow plague" [S1]. So the name *Y Fad Felen* has an annal witness (the B and C texts) as well
as the Book of Llandaff; the local thread is still only the Teilo legend.

**The local thread is a legend.** The Life of St Teilo in the Book of Llandaff (a 12th-century text
[effects:S18]) says Teilo could not stay at Llandaff "on account of the pestilence which nearly
destroyed the whole nation. It was called the Yellow Pestilence, because it occasioned all persons
who were seized by it, to be yellow and without blood, and it appeared to men as a column of a
watery cloud ... passing through the whole country like a shower going through the bottom of
vallies". It "seized Maelgwn, King of North Wales". Teilo led survivors to Armorica (Brittany),
planted fruit groves "from Dôl as far as Cai" with St Samson, and came home when the plague "should
depart and vanish from the whole island of Britain" [S5]. The 1840 editor's notes add Welsh
tradition: Maelgwn died of it in the church of Llanrhos, and a Triad calls it "the Yellow Plague of
Rhôs" [S5]. The Life places Teilo at Llandaff, not Llandeilo, and is hagiography.

**What the app could show.** At about 547, a ripple card: a great mortality in Wales (annals), the
plague's presence in Britain (DNA). The Teilo story can appear as *legend*, clearly labelled, since
Llandeilo is his church: the saint who fled the Yellow Plague. No deaths in the valley are recorded.
Tier: regional only; legend labelled. Annal and DNA are independent, so the plague in Britain is
cross-checked; the Welsh mortality is single-source.

### 1257 to 1258: Samalas

**Distant event.** Ice cores record "a colossal volcanic eruption ... ca. A.D. 1257 or 1258", the
"largest volcanic sulfur release to the stratosphere of the past 7,000 y"; its source is argued to
be Samalas on Lombok, erupting "between May and October A.D. 1257" [S7].

**Evidence in Wales.** None. The *Brut y Tywysogion* entries for 1257 and 1258 were read in the
Rolls Series text and are about Llywelyn ap Gruffudd's wars and oaths; they mention no weather or
dearth [S6]. The Brut does record weather elsewhere (a great snow in March 959; a great famine in
Maredudd's territory in 993; drought in 1252 then floods that broke bridges and mills) [S6], so its
silence for 1257 to 1259 is worth noting, though not proof. English chronicle evidence for a 1258
famine was not read in this pass.

**What the app could show.** Nothing. Tier: not supportable.

### 1315 to 1317: the Great Famine

**Distant event and evidence.** For England, "composite crop yields were about 40, 60 and ten per
cent below their normal levels in 1315, 1316 and 1317" (Philip Slavin, conference paper of 2012, read
as a summary on medievalists.net) [S8]. Despite the paper's title ("England and Wales"), the summary
read has nothing Welsh [S8]. Llywelyn Bren's rising of 1316 in Glamorgan is sometimes linked to the
famine; no source read here makes that link.

**What the app could show.** Nothing in the valley. Tier: not supportable. Single-source, and only a
summary.

### 1349: the Black Death in Ystrad Tywi

**Evidence.** William Rees, "The Black Death in Wales" (Transactions of the Royal Historical
Society, 1920), built from ministers' accounts and court rolls because "contemporary chronicles throw
no light", "with the exception of Geoffrey le Baker's reference to the spread of the disease to Wales
by the year 1349" [S9] (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: the quotation had been cut before its qualification). In the Crown lands: "the Carmarthen district suffered severely at this first
visitation, especially the small manor of Llanllwch", where the dozen *gabularii* (rent-paying
tenants) "all died" (contested: J. E. Lloyd, *A History of Carmarthenshire* (1935), as cited by
Wikipedia, says "Eleven of the twelve gafol-men died from the Black Death", in 1349 to 1350 [S44];
Lloyd not read directly; *Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*); the two officials of the Staple at Carmarthen died "about the end of March";
by 1353 land "lay waste and unmeasured for lack of buyers"; the borough's mill tolls and fisheries
"were seriously diminished and the fairs could not be held" [S9]. Then: "During the summer, the
pestilence made serious ravages throughout the commotes of Ystrad Towy and Cardigan", though,
because communities paid dues as a body, "it is almost impossible to make any but very general
statements". "In every district great difficulty was experienced in obtaining anyone to hire the
local offices of beadle, reeve or serjeant"; advowry tenants "either died or fled"; in south
Cardiganshire 97 of 104 *gabularii* "withdrew before midsummer for fear of the pestilence" [S9]. A
milder "Second Pestilence" followed in the winter of 1361 to 1362, and another in 1369 [S9].

**Level.** Ystrad Tywi is the medieval region of the Tywi valley, so the statement covers this
valley's commotes as a group. No entry names Llandeilo, Dinefwr, Talley or Dryslwyn. Single-source:
Rees is the classic study but dates from 1920, and later work was not checked.

**What the app could show.** In summer 1349: fewer people in the fields, empty holdings, no fair, a
funeral. Tier: documented at region level; anything specific to the town (who died, which farms
emptied) is reconstructed by analogy with Llanllwch and the Ystrad Tywi accounts, and labelled.

### 1600 to 1601: Huaynaputina

The 1600 eruption is studied as a "possible trigger for persistent cooling in the North Atlantic
region" (White et al., 2021 preprint) [S10]. The preprint mentions Ireland's winter pastures but
nothing British or Welsh that was read. Tier: not supportable here.

### 1683 to 1684 and 1739 to 1740: the great frosts

William Andrews (1887) describes the Thames frost fairs of both winters [S11]. Nothing Welsh was
found in his book or in this pass. A warning against assuming Wales froze too: of the long frost of
1762 to 1763, Andrews says "in Cornwall, Wales, and Ireland, this frost was felt but slightly" [S11].
Tier: not supportable here. A Welsh almanac or parish-register pass could change this.

### 1783: Laki and the dry fog

**Distant event and British evidence.** The Laki fissure eruption in Iceland emitted 122 Mt of SO2
and "maintained a sulfuric aerosol veil" over the Northern Hemisphere [S12]. "Some sources indicate
that the haze first appeared in Great Britain on 16 June; it remained for 2 to 3 months, and did not
disappear until Michaelmass" (29 September). At Selborne, "the blades of wheat in several fields are
turned yellow and look as if scorched with frost. Sun was seen red through the haze" (Gilbert White);
in Norfolk the gloom made "the sun ... scarcely visible even at midday" and "caused the corn to
wither" [S12]. July 1783 is "the second warmest on record in England after 1995" [S12]. The winter
of 1783 to 1784 was severe in Europe and North America [S12]. Mortality in England in 1783 to 1784
has been studied (Witham and Oppenheimer, 2004) [S13], but only the title and journal were read.

**Evidence in Wales.** None read. The diary of William Thomas of Michaelston-super-Ely, Glamorgan
(1762 to 1795) covers that summer and is the obvious next source; it was not available online.

**What the app could show.** A ripple card for June to September 1783: a dry, smelling haze, a red
sun, recorded across Great Britain. In the valley scene it would be *regional only*: a haze look is
reasonable but no Welsh witness is cited.

### 1795 to 1801: the corn dearths (listed for completeness)

Not a single distant shock (poor harvests in wartime), but the clearest pre-1816 county evidence of
dearth. From D. J. V. Jones's *Before Rebecca* (1973), as summarised by GENUKI: at Carmarthen in
1795 "a large mob led by small farmers dragged the brass Winchester measure from the market place to
the Dark Gate and destroyed it"; about 1800 to 1801, "At Carmarthen, where wheat was 20s per
Winchester bushel and barley 11s to 14s, soldiers were called out to stop the colliers causing a
riot" [S14]. Single-source (a summary of one book).

### 1815 to 1817: Tambora and the year without a summer

**Distant event.** "The eruption of Tambora (Indonesia) in April 1815 had substantial effects on
global climate and led to the 'Year Without a Summer' of 1816 in Europe and North America" [S15].

**Evidence in Carmarthenshire and south Wales** (all from *The Cambrian*, Swansea, read from the OCR):

- 27 July 1816: "severe losses having been sustained in Glamorganshire and Carmarthenshire by the
  rivers overflowing, and carrying away or greatly injuring the crops of hay. It is a lamentable
  fact, that the hay harvest is in a very wretched state"; much grass still uncut because farmers
  had "no confidence in fine and flattering mornings" [S16]. The *North Wales Gazette* of 1 August
  carries the same report [S17], so it is one report printed twice, not two witnesses.
- 31 August 1816: "The corn harvest has partially commenced in South Wales ... the crops of wheat,
  barley, and oats, wear every appearance of abundance. The hay is mostly secured in a good state,
  considering the late unfavourable weather" [S18].
- 28 September 1816: the paper compares "the present season" with 1692, quoting Burnet: "a very cold
  and wet summer; great deluges of rain continued till the very time of reaping", followed by "a very
  plentiful crop", and hopes "the coincidence will be completed by the present propitious weather"
  [S19]. The same column reports sunspots.
- Grain prices ("Average prices of grain in Wales", per quarter, Carmarthen wheat): 97s 8d (2 Nov
  1816), 90s 8d (9 Nov), 112s 0d (23 Nov), 93s 2d (30 Nov), 94s 10d (14 Dec 1816) [S21]; 92s 4d (8
  Nov 1817) [S22]; 69s 6d (22 Jan 1820) and 55s 10d (14 Oct 1820) [S23]. OCR digits are uncertain in
  places. No pre-1816 Carmarthen figure was found, so the rise cannot be measured.

**A contradiction, kept side by side.** The hay was "in a very wretched state" on 27 July [S16], yet
"mostly secured in a good state, considering the late unfavourable weather" on 31 August [S18]. The
corn crops then looked abundant [S18] while Carmarthen wheat stayed above 90s through late 1816 and
1817 [S21][S22], and only fell by 1820 [S23]. Both are recorded; neither is chosen. (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*:
the note set ruined hay against an abundant corn harvest, which are different crops.)

**Riot: reported and denied.** A paper reprinted in the *North Wales Gazette* (15 August 1816), probably
a London one (inferred from "Here, as in London"; the text does not name it; *Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*),
says "there has not been riot ... as related, in Carmarthenshire", calling such stories "the infernal
machinations of scoundrels" [S20]. So a disturbance in the county was reported somewhere and denied
by a government-minded paper. Neither the original report nor its truth was found.

**Emigration.** A search for emigrants from Carmarthen to America in 1816 to 1818 found nothing
relevant. Not supportable from this pass.

**What the app could show.** Summer 1816: grey wet weather, a flooded Tywi meadow with hay lost
(county documented, single report), dear bread through 1817 (county documented). Tier: documented at
county level; the valley scene is reconstructed from the county reports.

### 1832, 1849 and 1866: cholera

**1849, here.** *The Welshman*, 21 December 1849, under "LLANDILO": "During the late epidemic a
considerable sum of money was subscribed by the inhabitants of that town for the purpose of fitting
up and erecting a receptacle, to which any destitute patients who [might be] attacked with cholera
should be removed, and [where] proper accommodation and attendance might [be had]. A committee was
then appointed ... but in consequence of [only a] few cases of cholera having occurred in the [town
and neighbour]hood, the expenses incurred were [small]". The subscribers met and spent the balance on
"clothing and other necessaries for the destitute poor of the town"; Lord Dynevor and Col. Rice
Trevor gave £10 each [S24]. The bracketed words fill gaps in poor OCR, and are a reading, not a
quotation. The donors confirm this is Llandeilo Fawr, not Llandeilo Tal-y-bont. Single-source. This
corrects the gap recorded in the Victorian note, which found no local case [victorian:S54].

**1849, county.** An inquest at Pensarn, near Carmarthen, on a woman "supposed ... to have died from
Asiatic Cholera"; the doctor agreed, but the jury found "cramp, cholic, and diarrhoea" (27 July 1849)
[S25]. A death rumoured to be cholera on a vessel arriving from Tenby was the subject of a Carmarthen
inquest (6 July 1849) [S26].

**1832.** No local case found. A surgeon of Newcastle upon Tyne, from a family near Llandeilo, who "fell a sacrifice to fatigue
occasioned by attendance on the cholera patients" (where is not stated; presumably where he practised),
died while visiting an aunt at Llanstephan and was to be buried in Llandilo Church (*The Cambrian*, 17
November 1832) [S27]. That is a cholera-related death brought home, not an outbreak.

**1866.** The nearest found is Ystalyfera, about 12 miles (19 km) away, just outside the circle
[victorian:S54] (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: was "about 20 miles"). Regional only, and single-source: it rests on
Wikipedia alone, so a primary report from the 1866 Welsh papers is needed before it carries a number
such as a death toll.

**What the app could show.** 1849: worry, a fitted-up isolation house, a few cases, then relief
money turned into clothing for the poor. Tier: documented (single source). The look of the
"receptacle" is unknown.

### 1845 to 1847: potato blight

- 5 September 1845, *The Welshman*: "Potatoes promise a most abundant yield, and we do not hear that
  in any part of this county the weather has had the same injurious effect ... which it has had in
  some of the southern and western counties of England" [S28].
- 9 October 1846, *Pembrokeshire Herald*: a gas-fitter's garden in Friar's Park, Carmarthen, was replanted (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: was "a Carmarthen gardener") after "a crop of the same
  kind had been attacked by the disease", and "In the neighbourhood of Carmarthen, all the potatoes in
  different places exhibited signs of the attack within the space of a few days, if not a few hours"
  [S29].
- 2 July 1847, *Pembrokeshire Herald* (Quarter Sessions): "several Irish paupers were encamped in
  different parts of the county" [S30], a likely ripple of the Irish famine, though the report does
  not say why they came.

Nothing for Llandeilo itself, and nothing in Thomas Jenkins's transcribed diary. Tier: documented at
county level (1846, single-source); the 1845 report shows the county escaped the first year.

### January 1848: Thomas Jenkins's influenza

Jenkins wrote on 8 January 1848, two days after his wife died: "I was taken very ill with the
influenza" [S42]. It is local and dated, but no source read here ties it to the epidemic of that
winter elsewhere. Tier: documented as his illness; the distant link is not supportable.

### 1865 to 1866: the cattle plague

*The Welshman*, 27 October 1865: "The counties of Carmarthen and Cardigan have not yet taken
precautionary measures against the cattle plague, which has come very close upon us ... It has
extended over nearly the whole of England, and has found its way into Scotland and into several
counties in North Wales"; it was thought to travel "in railway trucks, and in the clothes of farmers
and drovers" [S31]. In August 1866 Pembrokeshire's justices were licensing cattle fairs under
cattle-plague orders [S32]. No outbreak in Carmarthenshire, and no closure of Llandeilo's fairs, was
found. Tier: regional only (fear and controls). Worth a follow-up because Llandeilo was a fair town.

### 1883: Krakatoa's afterglows

*The Cambrian*, 21 December 1883: "A great deal has been written recently about the unusual beauty of
the afterglow, which has followed the sunsets of English and Welsh skies during the last couple of
months", with no better explanation than "a peculiar condition of the atmosphere"; that morning "the
foreglow in the eastern sky was quite comparable" [S33]. The *Wrexham Advertiser*, 14 December 1883:
the sun over the Berwyn "went down in a great sea of brilliant blood colour, which afterwards turned
to gold and yellow, the afterglow lasting two hours" [S34]. The Royal Society's Krakatoa Committee
(1888) gathered such reports worldwide and lists glows at Beaumaris in late November [S35].
Cross-checked (two Welsh papers, one report). Nothing names Llandeilo, but the glows were seen across
Britain. Tier: reconstructed for the valley (vivid red and gold afterglows, late November and December
1883), labelled as recorded in Wales generally.

### Autumn 1918: influenza in Llandeilo

- 31 October 1918, *Amman Valley Chronicle*: "The influenza epidemic is levying a heavy toll on
  Llandilo and district. A very large number of the inhabitants were confined to their beds by the
  scourge, and there have been several deaths. The places of worship were very sparsely attended ...
  Day and Sunday Schools have been closed" [S37].
- 1 November 1918, *Carmarthen Weekly Reporter*: "The town and locality is in the grip of this
  devastating disease"; two of the three doctors "are among its victims"; "All the schools, county,
  elementary, and Miss McArthur's have been closed", and the Sunday schools at the Medical Officer's
  request; deaths at Trap and Ffairfach, one of a former secretary of the Llandilo Bridge Mart who
  "probably contracted the complaint by visiting sufferers" [S36].
- 14 November 1918, *Cambria Daily Leader*: a death from influenza in Rhosmaen Street [S40]. 15
  November, *Carmarthen Journal*: at Ammanford "All the schools in the area had been closed" [S43].
- 21 November, *Amman Valley Chronicle*, and 23 November, *Herald of Wales*: the schools "have again
  re-opened", a sign the epidemic "is abating"; a soldier home on leave from France died of it
  [S39] (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: S38's soldier on leave is a different man, whose child had died). A December death at Cwmdu followed "influenza followed by pneumonia" (*Carmarthen
  Journal*, 20 December 1918, seen as a search snippet) [S43].

Cross-checked across four papers. Names of ordinary people are left out of this note on purpose; the
sources have them. It coincided with the Armistice. **What the app could show:** late October to
mid-November 1918, shut schools, empty chapels, funerals, a lone elderly doctor on his rounds. Tier:
documented.

### 1986: Chernobyl

On 20 June 1986 the government prohibited "for the next 21 days the movement and slaughter of sheep"
in designated areas of "South West Cumbria" and "parts of North Wales", shown on a map placed in the
Lords Library [S41]. No Carmarthenshire area was named. Tier: not supportable here.

### Others considered

- **1197:** the Brut records "a dreadful season of mortality over all the isle of Britain and the
  borders of France", the same year it laments the Lord Rhys's death [S6]. It does not say he died of
  it. Region (Britain); a ripple card at most.
- **988 and 993:** "a great mortality ... through famine" and "a great famine ... in the territory of
  Maredudd" (Deheubarth) [S6]. Regional, but not known to be distant in cause.
