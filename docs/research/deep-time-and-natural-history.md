## Summary

The 10-mile radius around Llandeilo sits across one of the classic ground-truths of British geology: Ordovician and Silurian mudstones/sandstones in the Tywi valley itself (intensely folded and faulted by the Caledonian Orogeny), a rampart of Devonian Old Red Sandstone to the south (the Black Mountain/Mynydd Du massif), and, capping the Black Mountain's southern flank, a narrow east–west belt of Carboniferous Limestone with karst caves, on which the isolated crag of Carreg Cennen sits. The town of Llandeilo itself gave its name to a now-obsolete British regional stage of the Ordovician (the "Llandeilo Series/Stage"), a place in the history of stratigraphy that traces back to Edward Lhuyd's 1698 discovery of Britain's first recorded trilobite fossil, close to Dinefwr.

During the last glaciation (the Devensian), Welsh mountains disappeared under an ice cap thick enough to bury them, feeding an "Irish Sea Glacier" that pushed round the Welsh coast; roughly 22,000 years ago the area now the Brecon Beacons/Fforest Fawr/Black Mountain was largely ice-covered, and a much smaller, separate re-glaciation of the high cwms occurred again in the Loch Lomond Stadial (Younger Dryas, c.12,900–11,700 years ago), carving the cirques that hold Llyn y Fan Fach and Llyn y Fan Fawr and building the moraines still visible below the escarpments.

As the ice retreated the land passed through tundra, then birch and pine/hazel woodland, then closed-canopy oak/elm/lime "wildwood," with a marked Elm Decline (c.4300–3250 BCE) and Neolithic clearance; on the Black Mountain plateau itself, at Waun Fignen Felen, Mesolithic hunter-gatherers were burning heath and birchwood to manage game from around 8000 years ago, dated palaeoecological evidence for exactly this kind of vegetation disturbance. Blanket bog subsequently spread across these uplands, in a process a landmark 1973 study linked to that same prehistoric land use. Megafauna — mammoth, reindeer, and the hyenas that denned in limestone caves near the coast — gave way after the Ice Age to aurochs, elk, wild boar, brown bear, lynx and wolves, each going extinct in Britain (and specifically in Wales, for the wolf) at documented but staggered dates between the Neolithic and the high Middle Ages; some of that fauna is now being deliberately brought back nearby (red kite, pine marten, beaver), and Dinefwr's White Park cattle and fallow deer sit at the folkloric end of that story, claimed to be very ancient but with disputed evidence.

## Geology (with the Llandeilo stage explained)

**Bedrock sequence, oldest to youngest.** In the extreme northwest of the wider Brecon Beacons/Fforest Fawr area (which reaches into the Llandeilo/Llangadog ground), the oldest exposed rocks are the Llanvirn-age mudstones of the **Abergwilli Formation** (Ordovician), overlain by the "hard and often pebbly sandstones of the overlying **Ffairfach Grit Formation** which form the low hill of Y Garn Goch," and above that the "thinly laminated sandstones of the **Llandeilo Flags Formation**" [S1] — i.e. there is a real, current, named rock formation called the Llandeilo Flags right at the type locality. Silurian rocks (Wenlock, Ludlow and Pridoli age sandstones and mudstones) form the **Myddfai Steep Belt**, a NE–SW aligned structural tract [S1]. The whole Ordovician–Silurian sequence "stretch[es]... eastwards through Carmarthenshire up the Vale of Towy" and is "intricately intermixed," having been "intensely faulted and folded" by the **Caledonian Orogeny** [S2].

From late Silurian into the early Carboniferous, the area sat within the Anglo-Welsh Basin, depositing the **Old Red Sandstone**: the St Maughans, Senni and Brownstones formations (Lower ORS, Early Devonian) — the rock Picws Du and Fan Brycheiniog are built from — followed by the Upper ORS **Plateau Beds** and **Grey Grits**, which cap peaks like Pen y Fan and Corn Du [S1] [S4]. The Old Red Sandstone's red/purple colour comes from iron oxide, though colours actually range "from grey and green through to red and purple," and the depositional setting included "dunes, and sediments... laid down in lakes, river, estuaries, and possibly other coastal environments," with some marine/brackish evidence rather than pure desert [S4].

South of the sandstone summits, a **narrow east–west belt of Carboniferous Limestone** crosses the park, with extensive karst including "some of Britain's most extensive cave networks" (e.g. Ogof Ffynnon Ddu) [S1] [S5], and "interstratal karst along the outcrop of the overlying **Twrch Sandstone**" (Millstone Grit) [S1]. Further south again lies the **South Wales Coalfield**: Coal Measures with "thick, workable seams," coal rank rising from bituminous in the east to **anthracite** "mostly to the north and west of Neath" — i.e. the Amman Valley/western Carmarthenshire end of the coalfield [S5].

**Structure.** Three major lineaments are named for this ground: the Myddfai Steep Belt, the **Carreg Cennen Disturbance**, and the **Neath Disturbance** — reflecting both the older Caledonian Orogeny and later reactivation during the Variscan Orogeny [S1] [S3]. The Carreg Cennen Disturbance is "a zone of ancient geological faults and folds stretching from Pembrokeshire to Shropshire," most visibly expressed at Carreg Cennen itself, where the castle's crag is "an isolated block of Carboniferous Limestone trapped within two faults" surrounded by Old Red Sandstone country; the River Cennen follows the fault line for over 4 km because glaciation and erosion found the deformed rock easier to cut through [S6]. Beneath the castle a natural cave system contains a freshwater spring and evidence of prehistoric human use [S6].

**Fossils and the Llandeilo Series/Stage.** Llandeilo has a genuine, well-documented place in the history of British stratigraphy, though it is via a fossil find rather than a Murchison naming that I could directly verify. In 1688, the Welsh naturalist **Edward Lhuyd** recorded (in a letter to Martin Lister, published 1689) what is now recognised as the **first written record of a trilobite** — a specimen he could only describe as the "skeleton of some flat fish," later identified as ***Ogygiocarella debuchii*** (Brongniart, 1822) — found "probably on the grounds of Lord Dynefor's Castle," i.e. Dinefwr, right by Llandeilo [S17]. Lhuyd published it in his 1699 *Lithophylacii Britannici Ichnographia*, "the first catalogue ever of fossils," funded in part by Isaac Newton [S17]. *Ogygiocarella* — a flat, oval Middle Ordovician trilobite — is described as "first occurring in the **Lower Llandeilian Stage** as developed at Llandeilo in Wales," making Llandeilo the type locality for that stage of the old British regional scheme [S18]. Other trilobites recorded from the district include ***Gravicalymene***, from Birdshill Quarry near Llandeilo [S19], and ***Bumastus***, whose "Dobrotivian age/stage" occurrences are described elsewhere as equivalent to "Llandeilo age" [S20] — Dobrotivian being the Baltoscandian regional name for roughly the same interval, which in the modern global (ICS) timescale corresponds approximately to the Darriwilian–Sandbian transition of the Middle–Upper Ordovician [S20] [S21] [S22].

Historically, the "Llandeilo Series" sat as the middle unit of a threefold Welsh Ordovician succession — Arenig (lower), Llandeilo (middle, "mainly volcanic, with shale"), Caradoc/Bala (upper) — the term Arenig itself having been coined by Adam Sedgwick in 1847 for what he originally took to be the middle (Llandeilo) unit [S9]. This is a single, regional British scheme that predates Charles Lapworth's 1879 proposal to unify the disputed Cambrian/Silurian middle ground into a new period, the **Ordovician** [S23] — the period the Llandeilo rocks are now simply assigned to. The **BGS Lexicon of Named Rock Units** confirms "Llandeilo Rocks (undifferentiated)" as an **obsolete** entry (code LLDO), spanning the "Abereiddian Stage to Caradoc Series," 468–449.75 Ma, now folded into the successor entry LLDC ("Llanvirn and Caradoc Rocks (undifferentiated)") [S24] [S25]. So: the *stage/series* name "Llandeilo" is obsolete in modern chronostratigraphy (superseded by the global Darriwilian/Sandbian stages), but the place-name survives, alive and well, as the **Llandeilo Flags Formation**, a real mapped rock unit at the type locality itself [S1].

## Ice ages

Wales lay under a substantial share of the **British-Irish Ice Sheet** during the Devensian glaciation. At the Last Glacial Maximum (occurring "some time after 24,000 years ago"), reconstructed glacier-surface elevations were around 2,000 m over St George's Channel, 2,250 m over Anglesey and 2,500 m over the Isle of Man — implying "the mountains of Wales... must also have been submerged beneath thick glacier ice at the time" [S7]. A **Welsh ice cap centred on the Brecon Beacons** fed an "Irish Sea Glacier," which was pushed south along the Welsh coast by that Welsh ice [S7]; sea level was about 120 m lower than present [S7]. Around **22,000 years ago**, glacial ice is thought to have covered the majority of the modern Brecon Beacons National Park, including the Fforest Fawr/Black Mountain massif [S1].

As the main ice sheet decayed, a distinct, smaller-scale **re-glaciation of the high cwms** occurred during the **Loch Lomond Stadial** (the British/Irish name for the Younger Dryas, c.12,900–11,700 years BP) [S1] [S8]. This carved (or re-carved) small **cirques** on the north and northeast faces of peaks such as Picws Du — Pwll yr Henllyn and Pant y Bwlch — which drain via the Afon Sawdde into the Tywi [S26]. It built the "large linear moraines below the cliffs" seen at Fan Hir and elsewhere, some retaining small pools, and dammed the glacial lakes **Llyn y Fan Fach** and **Llyn y Fan Fawr** [S26] [S27]. Britain-wide, the Younger Dryas brought cooling of 2–6°C, icefields/glaciers in upland Britain and permafrost in the lowlands (implying roughly −5°C cooling there), a tundra landscape dominated by the alpine plant *Dryas octopetala*, and an abrupt end — warming back to previous levels over just 50–60 years [S8].

As the ice left, upland Wales was periglacial rather than glaciated: freeze-thaw action produced **blockfields** (large angular boulder fields — the Wikipedia periglaciation article cites Snowdonia as a good Welsh example, and the same mechanism applies across upland Wales generally) [S9], **solifluction lobes** (waterlogged soil slumping downslope into U-shaped lobes) [S9], and **patterned ground** (stone circles/polygons/stripes from frost heave) [S9]. The north face of Picws Du today shows an "apron of talus... deeply gullied through the action of debris flows," reckoned "the best examples of such flows in Britain" — a legacy of that periglacial regime [S26].

Superimposed on this, the **Elm Decline** — "a widespread decline in the number of elm trees across Britain, with millions of them disappearing from the archaeological record" — occurred c.4300–3250 BCE, at the Neolithic transition [S11]. Neolithic clearance elsewhere in Britain used "stone axes... ring barking and burning," and in many cases "forests had regrown within a few centuries" after initial clearing, i.e. early clearance was not always permanent [S11].

**Local evidence.** The best-documented site inside/immediately adjacent to the radius is **Waun Fignen Felen**, a shallow basin at 485 m on the Black Mountain limestone plateau (grid SN825178 — right at the eastern edge of the radius as defined). Excavation and pollen work there (Berridge 1979–81; Smith & Cloutman 1988; Barton et al. 1995) produced "dated palaeoecological evidence for disturbance of the vegetation cover" spanning the Mesolithic and after: the earliest artefacts sit on an old soil surface predating the mid-10th millennium BP, and from the 8th millennium BP onward there is evidence of **deliberate burning of heathland and birch woodland to manage game**, maintaining "tracts of open ground" that may also have been kept open by grazing animals; hazelnuts were recovered from the deposits, and the fire regime contributed to peat formation [S12]. This is a rare case of directly dated local vegetation-disturbance data, rather than a generic regional narrative, and is cross-checked between the archaeological (Coflein) record and the underlying published excavation reports it cites.

A landmark national-scale study, **Moore (1973)**, "The influence of prehistoric cultures upon the initiation and spread of blanket bog in upland Wales" (*Nature* 241, 350–353), argued that prehistoric human activity (Neolithic/Bronze Age clearance and burning) triggered the initiation and spread of blanket bog across upland Wales — the process that, over time, converted areas like the Black Mountain plateau from wooded/heath ground into the peat-covered moorland seen today [S13]. I could not access the full text (paywalled), so this is reported as a single, though highly citable/landmark, source.

As regional context (outside the strict 10-mile radius but the nearest well-documented long peat record), **Cors Caron** (Tregaron Bog) "began to be formed 12,000 years ago, at the end of the last glacial period," progressing from open lake to marsh to fen to raised bog; trees that died around 3000 BC and were preserved in the bog are used for past-climate study, and the important peat-forming moss *Sphagnum imbricatum* was present until as recently as the 18th century but is now locally absent [S28].

By the Bronze Age, climate had turned wetter, and population was "forced... away from easily defended [upland] sites... into the fertile valleys" — consistent with, and possibly a driver of, the upland bog spread described above [S14]. Archaeological evidence for Bronze Age barrows and "extensive settlement and cultivation of the high land" on Fan Brycheiniog is explicitly read as evidence the climate then was "warmer than currently" [S1] [S27].

## Climate phases

- **Younger Dryas / Loch Lomond Stadial (c.12,900–11,700 years BP):** cooling of 2–6°C in Britain; icefields/glaciers in upland Britain (matching the Picws Du/Fan Brycheiniog cirque moraines above), permafrost in lowlands; tundra dominated by *Dryas octopetala*; ended abruptly, warming to previous levels within 50–60 years [S8].
- **Holocene Climatic Optimum (c.9,500–5,500 years BP, peak ~8,000 BP):** general Northern Hemisphere/northwest European warming; no Wales-specific data found in the sources checked — flagged as a gap [S41].
- **Bronze Age deterioration:** climate turned "much wetter," pushing population out of defensible hilltop sites into valleys; consistent with the spread of blanket bog onto the Black Mountain plateau in the same broad period [S14] [S13].
- **Medieval Warm Period (c.950–1250 CE):** "exceptionally warm" in western Europe, Iceland and Greenland per Hubert Lamb's synthesis; no Wales-specific temperature/agricultural data found — flagged as a gap [S15].
- **Little Ice Age (16th–19th centuries CE):** cooling after the Medieval Warm Period; no Wales-specific data found in the sources checked — flagged as a gap [S16].

## 3D art notes

- **Ordovician/Silurian bedrock (Tywi valley floor):** dark grey-blue mudstones and sandstones (Abergwilli Formation, Myddfai Steep Belt), tightly folded — good for exposing tilted/kinked strata in road cuts and riverbanks rather than flat bedding.
- **Ffairfach Grit / Llandeilo Flags:** pale, pebbly to thinly-laminated sandstone — a lighter grey-buff band, forming the low hill of Y Garn Goch specifically (useful visual anchor since another workstream covers that hillfort).
- **Old Red Sandstone (Black Mountain, Fan Brycheiniog, Picws Du):** the characteristic red-brown-purple palette from iron oxide, but not uniformly red — include grey/green bands per [S4]; forms the big stepped escarpment and flat "Plateau Beds" summits (Pen y Fan-style mesa tops).
- **Carboniferous Limestone belt (south flank of the Black Mountain, Carreg Cennen):** pale grey/white weathering limestone, karst — sinkholes, cave mouths, limestone pavement with perched Old Red Sandstone erratics (a real, sourced detail: glacial erratics of ORS sitting on limestone pavement) [S1].
- **Coal Measures/Millstone Grit (far south of radius, Amman Valley):** darker grey sandstones and grits, a duller, more muted palette than the ORS.
- **Devensian ice sheet over the terrain:** should read as genuinely burying the mountains — reconstructed surfaces of 2,000–2,500 m regionally mean the whole Black Mountain massif (topping out under 800 m) would be entirely submerged, a smooth white/blue-white dome with no rock exposed, versus the later Loch Lomond Stadial which is small valley/cirque glaciers only (ice tongues in high north-facing hollows, rest of landscape bare periglacial tundra/blockfield).
- **Periglacial phase:** bare ground, patterned/polygonal ground on the plateau, angular scree/blockfields on north faces, solifluction lobes as soft rounded terracing on slopes — a browns-and-greys, near-vegetation-free look.
- **Vegetation keyframes (colour/shape cues):** tundra (Ia–III) = low, sparse, pale green-brown, Dryas mat; birch forest (IV) = pale, airy, silver-white trunks, light green; pine/hazel (V–VI) = darker green conifer + understorey shrub layer; oak-elm-lime wildwood (VII–VIII) = tall, dense, dark-green closed canopy; post-clearance/blanket bog (Bronze Age on, upland only) = open, brown-purple heather/peat moorland with scattered relict pine stumps (bog oak/pine visible in section, cf. Cors Caron's preserved trees).
- **Megafauna silhouettes for keyframes:** mammoth/reindeer/hyena for the glacial phase (regional, not on-site); aurochs, elk, wild boar, red deer, wolf for the post-glacial wildwood phase; domesticated cattle/sheep by the Neolithic; White Park cattle and fallow deer as a distinct "designed landscape" fauna once Dinefwr's park exists.

## Almanac facts

- 🦴 The first trilobite ever recorded in Britain was found near Llandeilo (grounds of Dinefwr/"Lord Dynefor's Castle") by Edward Lhuyd in 1688, who could only call it the "skeleton of some flat fish"; it's now known as *Ogygiocarella debuchii*. [S17]
- 🪨 "Llandeilo" is a real place in the history of geological time — the old British "Llandeilo Series/Stage" of the Ordovician was named for the town, though the stage itself is now obsolete in modern global stratigraphy (superseded by the Darriwilian/Sandbian). The place-name lives on as the **Llandeilo Flags Formation**, a mapped sandstone unit right at Llandeilo/Llangadog. [S1] [S24]
- 🧊 At the peak of the last ice age, ice over the region was thick enough that the Black Mountain and Brecon Beacons would have been completely buried — regional ice-surface reconstructions put the surface 2,000–2,500 m up, far above any local summit. [S7]
- ⛰️ Llyn y Fan Fach and Llyn y Fan Fawr are glacial lakes, dammed by moraines left as small local glaciers reformed in the high cwms during the Younger Dryas cold snap, thousands of years after the main ice sheet had gone. [S26] [S27]
- 🔥 On the Black Mountain plateau at Waun Fignen Felen, Mesolithic people were deliberately burning heath and birch woodland to manage game roughly 8,000 years ago — one of the best-dated examples of prehistoric landscape management in the area. [S12]
- 🐺 Wolves went extinct in Wales specifically in **1166** — over 200 years before England (1390) and over 500 before Scotland (1680). [S31]
- 🐄 Dinefwr's White Park cattle are claimed to have been there "for over a thousand years" and to appear in the medieval Laws of Hywel Dda — but Wikipedia's own breed article flags this history as disputed, containing folklore and unsourced claims. [S35] [S36]
- 🪶 Red kites came within a handful of pairs of extinction in Britain, all of them in south/mid Wales, bottoming out at just two pairs in the 1930s — Llanddeusant, on the Black Mountain, is now a public feeding/viewing site. [S38]
- 🦫 Beavers, extinct in Britain since the 16th century, are back in Wales on a trial basis at Cors Dyfi since 2021, with free-living animals confirmed beyond the reserve by 2024. [S34]
- 🐟 The Tywi is reputed to produce more big (10 lb+) sewin (sea trout) than any other river in Britain, and is still fished from Carmarthen-style coracles — rounder and deeper than their Teifi cousins because they work tidal water. [S40] [S41b]

## Open questions

- No source could be found confirming that **Roderick Murchison** personally named the Llandeilo Stage/Series, or giving an exact publication date for it — this is widely assumed common knowledge but I could not verify it directly in the time available (Wikipedia's Murchison, Llandovery Group, Lapworth and Caradoc articles were all checked and none confirm it). Needs a dedicated stratigraphy-history source (e.g. a BGS memoir or a paper on the history of Ordovician nomenclature) before the app states this as fact.
- **Species-specific arrival dates for domesticated cattle, sheep and pigs in Neolithic Britain** were not found (only the general "carried by boat... c.4000 BC" statement) — needs a dedicated archaeozoology source.
- **When chickens and horses arrived** and were adopted in Britain was not confirmed from the sources checked (the oft-repeated "Britons didn't eat chicken" claim attributed to Caesar was not verified here either) — flag before use.
- **Holocene Climatic Optimum, Medieval Warm Period and Little Ice Age** are documented at Britain/NW-Europe scale but I found no Wales- or Tywi-valley-specific data (temperature, agricultural impact) — worth another pass against Welsh climate-history literature if the app needs local specifics.
- **Ice Age megafauna directly within the 10-mile radius** (as opposed to the nearest documented sites, Coygan Cave near Laugharne and Paviland on Gower, both outside it) was not found — it's plausible mammoth/reindeer remains exist locally but unconfirmed; worth checking Amgueddfa Cymru (National Museum Wales) natural history collections directly, which I could not reach (no working URL found without a search engine).
- **Waun Fignen Felen's exact grid reference (SN825178) sits right at/just outside the stated eastern boundary (E280000)** of the 10-mile radius as given in the brief — worth the app owner double-checking whether this counts as "in" the radius; I have treated it as effectively on the boundary/relevant regardless.
- This research pass had **no working web-search tool** (the session's search budget was exhausted before this task started) and relied entirely on direct page fetches (mostly Wikipedia, Coflein, and BGS's Lexicon) plus one attempted-and-blocked academic source (Moore 1973 in *Nature*, paywalled). BGS's Geology of Britain map viewer is also now retired (replaced by the BGS Geology Viewer/GeoIndex, both interactive map tools I could not query point-by-point without search). A follow-up pass with search access and/or direct BGS GeoIndex/NRW SSSI-citation lookups would likely fill several of the gaps above, especially the Llandeilo/Murchison naming question and Wales-specific climate-phase detail.
