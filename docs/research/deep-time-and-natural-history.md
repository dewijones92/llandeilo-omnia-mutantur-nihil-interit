## Summary

The 10-mile radius around Llandeilo sits across one of the classic ground-truths of British geology: Ordovician and Silurian mudstones/sandstones in the Tywi valley itself (intensely folded and faulted by the Caledonian Orogeny), a rampart of Devonian Old Red Sandstone to the south (the Black Mountain/Mynydd Du massif), and, capping the Black Mountain's southern flank, a narrow east–west belt of Carboniferous Limestone with karst caves, on which the isolated crag of Carreg Cennen sits. The town of Llandeilo itself gave its name to a now-obsolete British regional stage of the Ordovician (the "Llandeilo Series/Stage"), a place in the history of stratigraphy that traces back to Edward Lhuyd's 1698 discovery of Britain's first recorded trilobite fossil, close to Dinefwr.

During the last glaciation (the Devensian), one reconstruction has Welsh mountains under an ice cap thick enough to bury them, though another source says the highest peaks stood out as nunataks (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*); roughly 22,000 years ago the area now the Brecon Beacons/Fforest Fawr/Black Mountain was largely ice-covered, and a much smaller, separate re-glaciation of the high cwms occurred again in the Loch Lomond Stadial (Younger Dryas, c.12,900–11,700 years ago), building cirque moraines, including those around Llyn y Fan Fach, still visible below the escarpments (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: no source read says the lakes are dammed by Younger Dryas moraines; S1 says the lakes "occupy glacially excavated rock hollows or cirques"; both lie just outside the circle).

As the ice retreated the land passed through tundra, then birch and pine/hazel woodland, then closed-canopy oak/elm/lime "wildwood," with a marked Elm Decline (c.4300–3250 BCE) and Neolithic clearance; on the Black Mountain plateau just east of the circle, at Waun Fignen Felen, Mesolithic hunter-gatherers were burning heath and open birch woodland to encourage large game from the eighth millennium BP, dated palaeoecological evidence for exactly this kind of vegetation disturbance. Blanket bog subsequently spread across these uplands, in a process a landmark 1973 study (not read) is said to link to that same prehistoric land use. Megafauna — mammoth, reindeer, and the hyenas that denned in limestone caves near the coast — gave way after the Ice Age to aurochs, elk, wild boar, brown bear, lynx and wolves, each going extinct in Britain at staggered dates between the Neolithic and the late Middle Ages (the wolf probably in the 13th or 14th century in Wales; *Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: none of these extinctions is attested inside the ten miles); some of that fauna is now being deliberately brought back nearby (red kite, pine marten, beaver), and Dinefwr's White Park cattle and fallow deer sit at the folkloric end of that story, claimed to be very ancient but with disputed evidence.

## Geology (with the Llandeilo stage explained)

**Bedrock sequence, oldest to youngest.** In the extreme northwest of the wider Brecon Beacons/Fforest Fawr area (which reaches into the Llandeilo/Llangadog ground), the oldest exposed rocks are the Llanvirn-age mudstones of the **Abergwilli Formation** (Ordovician), overlain by the "hard and often pebbly sandstones of the overlying **Ffairfach Grit Formation** which form the low hill of Y Garn Goch," and above that the "thinly laminated sandstones of the **Llandeilo Flags Formation**" [S1] — i.e. there is a real, current, named rock formation called the Llandeilo Flags right at the type locality. Silurian rocks (Wenlock, Ludlow and Pridoli age sandstones and mudstones) form the **Myddfai Steep Belt**, a NE–SW aligned structural tract [S1]. The whole Ordovician–Silurian sequence "stretch[es]... eastwards through Carmarthenshire up the Vale of Towy" and is "intricately intermixed," having been "intensely faulted and folded" by the **Caledonian Orogeny** [S2].

From late Silurian into the early Carboniferous, the area sat within the Anglo-Welsh Basin, depositing the **Old Red Sandstone**: the St Maughans, Senni and Brownstones formations (Lower ORS, Early Devonian) — the rock Picws Du and Fan Brycheiniog are built from — followed by the Upper ORS **Plateau Beds** and **Grey Grits**, which cap peaks like Pen y Fan and Corn Du [S1] [S4]. The Old Red Sandstone's red/purple colour comes from iron oxide, though colours actually range "from grey and green through to red and purple," and the depositional setting included "dunes, and sediments... laid down in lakes, river, estuaries, and possibly other coastal environments," with some marine/brackish evidence rather than pure desert [S4].

South of the sandstone summits, a **narrow east–west belt of Carboniferous Limestone** crosses the park, with extensive karst including "some of Britain's most extensive cave networks" (e.g. Ogof Ffynnon Ddu) [S1] [S5], and "interstratal karst along the outcrop of the overlying **Twrch Sandstone**" (Millstone Grit) [S1]. Further south again lies the **South Wales Coalfield**: Coal Measures with "thick, workable seams," coal rank rising from bituminous in the east to **anthracite** "mostly to the north and west of Neath" — i.e. the Amman Valley/western Carmarthenshire end of the coalfield [S5].

**Structure.** Three major lineaments are named for this ground: the Myddfai Steep Belt, the **Carreg Cennen Disturbance**, and the **Neath Disturbance** — reflecting both the older Caledonian Orogeny and later reactivation during the Variscan Orogeny [S1] [S3]. The Carreg Cennen Disturbance is "a zone of ancient geological faults and folds stretching from Pembrokeshire to Shropshire," most visibly expressed at Carreg Cennen itself, where the castle's crag is "an isolated block of Carboniferous Limestone trapped within two faults" surrounded by Old Red Sandstone country; the River Cennen follows the fault line for over 4 km because glaciation and erosion found the deformed rock easier to cut through [S6]. Beneath the castle a natural cave system contains a freshwater spring and evidence of prehistoric human use [S6].

**Fossils and the Llandeilo Series/Stage.** Llandeilo has a genuine, well-documented place in the history of British stratigraphy: Murchison divided the Lower Silurian into the Caradoc Sandstone and a lower group "called, from a town in Carmarthenshire, the Llandeilo flags" (Lyell 1871 [S46]; no year given; *Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: the note said this could not be verified). In 1698 the Welsh naturalist **Edward Lhuyd** recorded, in "a letter written (1698) to Dr. Martin Lister and published (1699)" [S18][S43], what is now recognised as the **first written record of a trilobite** — a specimen he could only describe as the "skeleton of some flat fish," later identified as ***Ogygiocarella debuchii*** (Brongniart, 1822) — found "probably on the grounds of Lord Dynefor's Castle," i.e. Dinefwr, right by Llandeilo [S17] [S18]. (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: the note gave 1688, published 1689, from S17, which contradicts its own 1699 publication date; S18 and Amgueddfa Cymru say 1698.) Lhuyd published it in his 1699 *Lithophylacii Britannici Ichnographia*, "the first catalogue ever of fossils," funded in part by Isaac Newton [S17]. *Ogygiocarella* — a flat, oval Middle Ordovician trilobite — is described as "first occurring in the **Lower Llandeilian Stage** as developed at Llandeilo in Wales," making Llandeilo the type locality for that stage of the old British regional scheme [S18]. Another trilobite recorded from the district is ***Gravicalymene***, from the Birdshill Limestone at Birdshill Quarry near Llandeilo [S19], which is Ashgill age (Pusgillian or lowest Cautleyan), later than the Llandeilo stage. (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: *Bumastus* was listed here, but S20's "Llandeilo age" occurrences are in China and France, with nothing from Llandeilo, so it is removed.) The Llandeilo interval corresponds approximately to the Darriwilian–Sandbian transition of the Middle–Upper Ordovician in the modern global (ICS) timescale [S21] [S22].

Historically, the "Llandeilo" sat in a Welsh succession of Arenig, Llandeilo and Caradoc/Bala; Sedgwick "had described, in 1843, strata ... in the Arenig mountain", and Lyell calls the lowest unit "the Arenig or Lower Llandeilo formation" [S46] (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: this paragraph was cited to S9, *Periglaciation*, which contains none of it; the 1847 date and the "mainly volcanic" gloss are dropped as unsourced). This regional British scheme predates Charles Lapworth's proposal to unify the disputed Cambrian/Silurian middle ground into a new period, the **Ordovician** [S23] (S23 gives no year, and names the Highlands Controversy as a separate achievement, so the "1879" and that gloss are dropped; *Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*) — the period the Llandeilo rocks are now simply assigned to. The **BGS Lexicon of Named Rock Units** confirms "Llandeilo Rocks (undifferentiated)" as an **obsolete** entry (code LLDO), spanning the "Abereiddian Stage to Caradoc Series" (the Ma figures and lithology are not on the page today; *Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*), now folded into the successor entry LLDC ("Llanvirn and Caradoc Rocks (undifferentiated)") [S24] [S25]. So: the *stage/series* name "Llandeilo" is obsolete in modern chronostratigraphy (superseded by the global Darriwilian/Sandbian stages), but the place-name survives, alive and well, as the **Llandeilo Flags Formation**, a real mapped rock unit at the type locality itself [S1].

## Ice ages

Wales lay under a substantial share of the **British-Irish Ice Sheet** during the Devensian glaciation. At the Last Glacial Maximum (occurring "some time after 24,000 years ago"), reconstructed glacier-surface elevations were "most likely" around 2,000 m over St George's Channel, 2,250 m over Anglesey and 2,500 m over the Isle of Man — from which "it follows that" "the mountains of Wales... must also have been submerged beneath thick glacier ice at the time" [S7]. **Contradiction (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*):** S7 itself warns that "the ice surface altitude over the mountains might have been around 800 m lower than predicted", and S1 says "the highest peaks protruded above the ice as nunataks" and that the larger part of the (eastern) Black Mountains stayed unglaciated. (In the earlier **Anglian** glaciation, S7 says the Irish Sea Glacier's edge "was pushed southwards by ice coming from the Welsh ice cap on the Brecon Beacons"; *Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: the note put this in the Devensian.) Sea level was about 120 m lower than present [S7]. Around **22,000 years ago**, glacial ice is thought to have covered the majority of the modern Brecon Beacons National Park [S1].

As the main ice sheet decayed, a distinct, smaller-scale **re-glaciation of the high cwms** occurred during the **Loch Lomond Stadial** (the British/Irish name for the Younger Dryas, c.12,900–11,700 years BP) [S1] [S8]. S1 names "several cirque moraines which are thought to date from the Loch Lomond Stadial ... These include the features around Llyn y Fan Fach and Llyn Cwm Llwch", and says the lakes "occupy glacially excavated rock hollows or cirques" [S1]. Picws Du has cirques, Pwll yr Henllyn and Pant y Bwlch, on its northwestern and northeast faces, "home to small glaciers during the ice ages" and draining via the Afon Sawdde into the Tywi (undated in S26) [S26], and "large linear moraines below the cliffs" are seen at Fan Hir and elsewhere [S26]. (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: the note said the stadial "dammed" Llyn y Fan Fach and Llyn y Fan Fawr, citing S26 and S27, which give no date and no damming; Wikipedia's *Llyn y Fan Fach*, a lead, mentions moraines "damming the Lake", undated. Both lakes lie just outside the circle, 10.8 and 12.6 miles from the centre.) The Younger Dryas brought cooling of 2–6°C in Europe; in Britain, icefields/glaciers in the uplands and permafrost in the lowlands (implying roughly −5°C cooling there); *Dryas octopetala* fossils are abundant in European, particularly Scandinavian, sediments; and an abrupt end — warming back to previous levels over just 50–60 years [S8].

As the ice left, upland Wales was periglacial rather than glaciated: freeze-thaw action produced **blockfields** (large angular boulder fields — the Wikipedia periglaciation article cites Snowdonia as a good Welsh example, and the same mechanism applies across upland Wales generally) [S9], **solifluction lobes** (waterlogged soil slumping downslope into U-shaped lobes) [S9], and **patterned ground** (stone circles/polygons/stripes from frost heave) [S9]. The north face of Picws Du today shows an "apron of talus... deeply gullied through the action of debris flows," reckoned "the best examples of such flows in Britain" — a legacy of that periglacial regime [S26].

## Vegetation through time

The European pollen zones (S10: "based on the work of J. Iversen, published in 1954"), which the vegetation renderer can use as keyframes, run as below. **Calibration warning (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*):** S10 says the dates, "given in years BC, are best viewed as being based on uncalibrated C-14 dates", with larger discrepancies from the end of the Boreal, so the true calendar dates are earlier (for example its Older Dryas start of 10,000 BC calibrates to about 12,000 BC). In calendar years the Late Glacial runs: the Bølling–Allerød interstadial 14,690 to c. 12,890 BP (about 12,740–10,940 BC) [S48]; at Llanilid in south Wales its early part was the warmest, with mean July temperatures "around 20°C" and juniper scrub, before two cooling steps (the second around 13,100 cal BP, about 11,150 BC) cut back juniper and then birch woodland; the Younger Dryas there is c. 12,600–11,400 cal BP (about 10,650–9,450 BC), "a scrub tundra with Betula, Salix and a range of open-habitat taxa"; and the Holocene opens c. 11,400 cal BP with "an abrupt temperature rise of the order of 9°C" and "rapid expansion of Betula woodland" [S47]. Superimposed on this, the **Elm Decline** — "a widespread decline in the number of elm trees across Britain, with millions of them disappearing from the archaeological record" — occurred c.4300–3250 BCE, at the Neolithic transition [S11]. Neolithic clearance elsewhere in Britain used "stone axes... ring barking and burning," and in many cases "forests had regrown within a few centuries" after initial clearing, i.e. early clearance was not always permanent [S11].

**Local evidence.** The best-documented site near the radius is **Waun Fignen Felen**, a shallow basin at 485 m on the Black Mountain limestone plateau (grid SN8250017840), 20.1 km (12.5 miles) from the app's map centre, about 4 km outside the circle, whose eastern edge is near E 279000 (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: was "right at the eastern edge"). Excavation and pollen work there (Berridge 1979–81; Smith & Cloutman 1988; Barton et al. 1995) produced "dated palaeoecological evidence for disturbance of the vegetation cover" spanning the Mesolithic and after: the earliest artefacts sit on an old soil surface predating the mid-10th millennium BP, and "from the eighth millennium BP, local heathland and open birch woodland were burnt, to encourage large game" (whether BP is calendar or radiocarbon is not stated), maintaining "tracts of open ground" that may also have been kept open by grazing animals; hazelnuts were recovered from the deposits, and the fire regime contributed to peat formation [S12]. This is a rare case of directly dated vegetation-disturbance data near the radius, rather than a generic regional narrative. Coflein does not itself mention pollen or charcoal; it says "dated palaeoecological evidence".

A landmark national-scale study, **Moore (1973)**, "The influence of prehistoric cultures upon the initiation and spread of blanket bog in upland Wales" (*Nature* 241, 350–353), is said to argue that prehistoric human activity triggered the initiation and spread of blanket bog across upland Wales [S13]. I could not access the full text (paywalled). (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: S13, *Blanket bog*, no longer mentions Moore; the paper exists, doi:10.1038/241350a0, but its content is unread, so this is a lead.)

As regional context (outside the strict 10-mile radius but the nearest well-documented long peat record), **Cors Caron** (Tregaron Bog) "began to be formed 12,000 years ago, at the end of the last glacial period," progressing from open lake to marsh to fen to raised bog; trees that died around 3000 BC and were preserved in the bog are used for past-climate study, and the important peat-forming moss *Sphagnum imbricatum* was present until as recently as the 18th century but is now locally absent [S28].

By the Bronze Age, climate had turned wetter, and population was "forced... away from easily defended [upland] sites... into the fertile valleys" [S14] — said in S14 of the Wessex culture of southern Britain, not Wales. Archaeological evidence for Bronze Age barrows and "extensive settlement and cultivation of the high land" on Fan Brycheiniog is explicitly read as evidence the climate then was "warmer than currently" [S1] [S27].

## Climate phases

- **Late Glacial interstadial (Bølling–Allerød, 14,690 to c. 12,890 BP, about 12,740–10,940 BC) [S48]:** at Llanilid, south Wales, warmest early (July around 20°C, juniper scrub), then cooling steps from about 11,150 BC cut back juniper and birch [S47]. (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: added; the climate keys rely on it.)
- **Younger Dryas / Loch Lomond Stadial (c.12,900–11,700 years BP; c. 12,600–11,400 cal BP at Llanilid [S47]):** cooling of 2–6°C in Europe; icefields/glaciers in upland Britain (matching the Picws Du/Fan Brycheiniog cirque moraines above), permafrost in lowlands; tundra; ended abruptly, warming to previous levels within 50–60 years [S8].
- **Holocene Climatic Optimum (c.9,500–5,500 years BP, peak ~8,000 BP):** general Northern Hemisphere/northwest European warming; no Wales-specific data found in the sources checked — flagged as a gap [S41].
- **Bronze Age deterioration:** climate turned "much wetter," pushing population out of defensible hilltop sites into valleys (said of southern Britain's Wessex culture) [S14].
- **Medieval Warm Period (c.950–1250 CE):** "exceptionally warm" in western Europe, Iceland and Greenland per the IPCC First Assessment Report (1990); Lamb is quoted on "a notably warm climate ... around 1000–1200 CE" (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: the "exceptionally warm" wording is the IPCC's, not Lamb's); no Wales-specific temperature/agricultural data found — flagged as a gap [S15].
- **Little Ice Age (16th–19th centuries CE; some experts prefer about 1300 to about 1850, S16 now read in full):** cooling after the Medieval Warm Period; no Wales-specific data found in the sources checked — flagged as a gap [S16].

## 3D art notes

- **Ordovician/Silurian bedrock (Tywi valley floor):** dark grey-blue mudstones and sandstones (Abergwilli Formation, Myddfai Steep Belt), tightly folded — good for exposing tilted/kinked strata in road cuts and riverbanks rather than flat bedding.
- **Ffairfach Grit / Llandeilo Flags:** pale, pebbly to thinly-laminated sandstone — a lighter grey-buff band, forming the low hill of Y Garn Goch specifically (useful visual anchor since another workstream covers that hillfort).
- **Old Red Sandstone (Black Mountain, Fan Brycheiniog, Picws Du):** the characteristic red-brown-purple palette from iron oxide, but not uniformly red — include grey/green bands per [S4]; forms the big stepped escarpment and flat "Plateau Beds" summits (Pen y Fan-style mesa tops).
- **Carboniferous Limestone belt (south flank of the Black Mountain, Carreg Cennen):** pale grey/white weathering limestone, karst — sinkholes, cave mouths, limestone pavement with perched Old Red Sandstone erratics (a real, sourced detail: glacial erratics of ORS sitting on limestone pavement) [S1].
- **Coal Measures/Millstone Grit (far south of radius, Amman Valley):** darker grey sandstones and grits, a duller, more muted palette than the ORS.
- **Devensian ice sheet over the terrain:** one reconstruction (S7) has surfaces of 2,000–2,500 m regionally, which would submerge the Black Mountain massif (topping out under 800 m), but S7 warns the surface over the mountains may have been about 800 m lower and S1 has the highest peaks standing out as nunataks (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*), so a dome with a few rock summits showing is the honest look, versus the later Loch Lomond Stadial which is small valley/cirque glaciers only (ice tongues in high north-facing hollows, rest of landscape bare periglacial tundra/blockfield).
- **Periglacial phase:** bare ground, patterned/polygonal ground on the plateau, angular scree/blockfields on north faces, solifluction lobes as soft rounded terracing on slopes — a browns-and-greys, near-vegetation-free look.
- **Vegetation keyframes (colour/shape cues):** tundra (Ia–III) = low, sparse, pale green-brown, Dryas mat; birch forest (IV) = pale, airy, silver-white trunks, light green; pine/hazel (V–VI) = darker green conifer + understorey shrub layer; oak-elm-lime wildwood (VII–VIII) = tall, dense, dark-green closed canopy; post-clearance/blanket bog (Bronze Age on, upland only) = open, brown-purple heather/peat moorland with scattered relict pine stumps (bog oak/pine visible in section, cf. Cors Caron's preserved trees).
- **Megafauna silhouettes for keyframes:** mammoth/reindeer/hyena for the glacial phase (regional, not on-site); aurochs, elk, wild boar, red deer, wolf for the post-glacial wildwood phase; domesticated cattle/sheep by the Neolithic; White Park cattle and fallow deer as a distinct "designed landscape" fauna once Dinefwr's park exists.

## Almanac facts

- 🦴 The first trilobite ever recorded in Britain was found near Llandeilo (probably the grounds of Dinefwr/"Lord Dynefor's Castle") and described by Edward Lhuyd in a letter of 1698, who could only call it the "skeleton of some flat fish"; it's now known as *Ogygiocarella debuchii*. [S18] [S43] (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: was 1688)
- 🪨 "Llandeilo" is a real place in the history of geological time — the old British "Llandeilo Series/Stage" of the Ordovician was named for the town, though the stage itself is now obsolete in modern global stratigraphy (superseded by the Darriwilian/Sandbian). The place-name lives on as the **Llandeilo Flags Formation**, a mapped sandstone unit right at Llandeilo/Llangadog. [S1] [S24]
- 🧊 At the peak of the last ice age, about 22,000 years ago, ice covered most of these mountains; one reconstruction puts the surface 2,000–2,500 m up around the Irish Sea, though the highest peaks may have stood out above it. [S1] [S7] (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*)
- ⛰️ Small glaciers reformed in the high cwms of the Black Mountain during the Younger Dryas; the moraines they left are thought to include those around Llyn y Fan Fach, just outside the circle. [S1] [S26] (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: no source read says the lakes are dammed by them)
- 🔥 On the Black Mountain plateau at Waun Fignen Felen, just east of the circle, Mesolithic people were deliberately burning heath and open birch woodland to encourage large game from the eighth millennium BP — one of the best-dated examples of prehistoric landscape management near the area. [S12]
- 🐺 The last written mention of a wolf in Wales is from **1166**, a probably exaggerated tale of a "mad wolf" that killed 22 people; wolves probably died out in Wales in the 13th or 14th century. [S42] (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: was "went extinct in Wales in 1166, over 200 years before England (1390)")
- 🐄 Dinefwr's White Park cattle are claimed to have been there "for over a thousand years" and to appear in the medieval Laws of Hywel Dda — but Wikipedia's own breed article flags this history as disputed, containing folklore and unsourced claims. [S35] [S36]
- 🪶 Red kites came within a handful of pairs of extinction in Britain, all of them in mid Wales: fewer than ten breeding pairs by the 1930s (BTO), or a single known nest (Aberystwyth University) — Llanddeusant, on the Black Mountain, has a feeding station. [S38] [S44] [S45] (*Corrected 2026-10-02 (independent check)*: "just two pairs" is not in S38)
- 🦫 Beavers, extinct in Britain since the 16th century, are back in Wales: a family has lived at Cors Dyfi since 2021, with free-living animals confirmed beyond the reserve by 2024. [S34]
- 🐟 The Tywi is reputed to produce more big (10 lb+) sewin (sea trout) than any other river in Britain, and its tidal reaches at Carmarthen are still fished from coracles — rounder and deeper than their Teifi cousins because they work tidal water. [S40] [S41b]
