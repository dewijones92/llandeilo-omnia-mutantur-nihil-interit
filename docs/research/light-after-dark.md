# Light after dark

Research for the lighting seam (`src/content/lamplight.ts`, [ADR 0028](../adr/0028-night-light-as-dated-content-keys.md)):
how homes and streets in and around Llandeilo were lit after dark, era by era. Written inline on
2026-10-03 from sources read that day, mostly Welsh Newspapers Online (National Library of Wales),
whose search results and article pages were fetched with `curl`. Not yet independently checked.

**"Llandilo" is two places** (see [process](process.md)). Every newspaper item below names the
Llandilo Gas Company, the Llandilo Local Board or the Llandilo Urban District Council, or a gas main
"from Ffairfach Gate to Llandilo Bridge", which are Llandeilo Fawr. Items about Llandilo-Talybont
(Glamorgan) and about Llandovery's 1863 gas lighting turned up in the same searches and are **not**
used.

## Summary

- **Hearth fire** is the light of every early home: the Mesolithic hut at Howick had hearths
  [hunters:S36], and Iron Age roundhouses are reconstructed with a central hearth (Garn Goch is
  unexcavated, so this is analogy). Reconstructed.
- **A medieval Welsh court burned candles; ordinary homes, firelight.** The Dimetian Code, the
  law-book of Deheubarth, sets a candle-bearer "before" the king at meat and dates his protection
  "from the time of lighting the first candle, in the palace, until the last shall be extinguished"
  [music:S3]. It is a prescriptive text, so it says what a court should have, not what was lit at
  Dinefwr on a given night. Nothing read here describes lighting in a medieval Llandeilo house.
- **Rushlights in farmhouses and cottages, by the 18th century.** At Cilewent, a long-house from
  Llansanffraid Cwmteuddwr near Rhayader, furnished at St Fagans to 1750, "in the evening, lighting
  was provided by a rushlight holder ... Reeds were dipped in fat, lit and placed between the two iron
  pieces" [S13]. Gilbert White's 1775 letter on rushlights says the poor had "five and a half hours of
  comfortable light for [a] farthing", that "working people burn no candles in the long days, because
  they rise and go to bed by daylight", and that "the very poor ... buy a halfpenny candle every
  evening" [S14]. White writes about Hampshire, and Cilewent is in mid Wales, well outside the ten miles, so for this
  valley both are analogy. Reconstructed, cross-checked as a practice (two independent sources).
- **Paraffin lamps were on sale in Carmarthen by 1864**: "NEW LAMPS for burning Petroline, Paraffin,
  and Cazeline Oils ... MODERATOR LAMPS" [S12]. Llandeilo's railway station still had "antiquated oil
  lamps" in December 1902 [S11]. Single-source each; how many Llandeilo homes had oil lamps, and from
  when, is not known.
- **Gas: the works were being built in 1864.** The Llandilo Gas Company accepted Mr G. Bower's tender
  of £96 "for plant and street mains"; the buildings and tanks were let to Morgan and Howell, masons of
  Llandilo, and the foundation stone was laid in August 1864 [S1]. A second paper reported gas works
  "in course of erection" at "Llandilo Fawr and Rhayader" in October 1864 [S2]. The company was allowed
  to lay a main under the turnpike road "from Ffairfach Gate to Llandilo Bridge" [S3]. Cross-checked
  (S1, S2). **This contradicts the c. 1860 date for the Ffairfach gas works** in [victorian:S2], a
  single Wikipedia page; the newspapers are primary.
- **Gas street lamps were lit by 1876, but the first lighting is not found.** In September 1876 the
  Local Board asked the Gas Company "to undertake the management of the lighting and repair of town
  lamps during the winter as heretofore", paying "eighteenpence per thousand feet of gas consumed
  beyond the current price" [S4]. "As heretofore" means this had been done before 1876. In September
  1902 the streets "were gas lit" before the electric light [S8]. **The date the streets were first
  lit with gas (between 1864 and 1876) is a gap**; several searches of Welsh Newspapers Online (1864 to 1867) found no
  report of it. The app draws gas street lamps from 1876 only.
- **Gas lamps lit only in winter?** S4 arranges the lighting "during the winter". Whether the town
  lamps were left dark in summer is not stated. Open question; the app does not model it.
- **Electric street lighting by early September 1902, the council's own.** A public meeting resolved
  in June 1899 "to have the town lit by electric light", at an estimated £1,500 [S5]. A shareholder
  protested that the Gas Company had "originally invested their money for the sole purpose of lighting
  the town" [S6]. The council accepted Mr Bertram Thomas's tender of £2,415 4s in June 1900 [S7] (the
  paper prints "Thorn"; Carmarthen Journal council reports of July and October 1902, not listed below,
  name the contractor Bertram Thomas). On 5 September 1902 a
  columnist mocked those "who profess that they can see no difference in the streets now that they are
  electrically lit than when they were gas lit" [S8]; a month later the streets were "left in
  darkness" one Saturday night when the electric light failed [S9]; in November the Urban Council "has
  just installed their own electric light" [S10]. Cross-checked (S8 and S10 are different papers). The
  exact switch-on day is not found, so the app uses September 1902.
- **Gas did not vanish.** In October 1902 the Reading Room's electric light was "not such a great
  improvement on the gas as was expected", and when the Institution's failed "luckily the gas had been
  left intact" (two Carmarthen Journal items of 10 and 31 October 1902, noted in passing, not listed
  below). Homes mixed gas, oil and electricity for
  decades; the share is not known.
- **The blackout, 1 September 1939.** "The first real effect of the Second World War for most British
  civilians was the blackout, which was introduced on 1 September 1939, two days before war was
  actually declared" [S15]. It "remained in force for five years until September 1944, when
  regulations were relaxed to allow a 'dim-out'" [S15]. National rules, so documented for Llandeilo
  as for everywhere; no local account of the blackout here was read.
- **The dim-out, from September 1944, was partial.** In October 1944 the Home Secretary spoke of "the
  recent relaxations for domestic lighting" but would not "relax all restrictions during any part of
  the black-out period", and said "in some parts, the black-out is now more effective than it was
  before the relaxation" [S16]. In February 1945 he would not yet do away with "blacking-out of
  private homes" [S17], while that sixth winter allowed "some alleviation of the street lighting
  position" [S17]. On 12 April 1945 he meant "to remove the black-out restrictions in all districts at
  a date not later than the end of the war in Europe" [S18], which was 8 May 1945. Cross-checked
  (IWM and Hansard). The app's dim-out draws faint windows and dim street lamps; how much light
  Llandeilo actually showed is reconstructed.

The shares of lit windows, the brightness and the colour of each key are **reconstructed**: no
source counts lit windows. Flame light (hearth, rush, candle, oil, flat-flame gas) is drawn warm
yellow-orange; electric light whiter. Gas mantles (whiter light) are not recorded for Llandeilo, so
gas is drawn as flame. Street lamp positions are not recorded; they are drawn along today's roads
inside the town.

## Open questions

- When were Llandeilo's streets first lit with gas (1864 to 1876)? How many lamps?
- Were the town's gas lamps lit only in winter, and put out at midnight?
- Who in Llandeilo had electric light at home before 1939, and when did farms get mains electricity?
- When did Ffairfach and the villages get street lighting at all?
- Roman oil lamps at the Dinefwr forts: no find is recorded in the notes, so the app shows none.
