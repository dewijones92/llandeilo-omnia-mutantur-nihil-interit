# Light after dark

Research for the lighting seam (`src/content/lamplight.ts`, [ADR 0030](../adr/0030-night-light-as-dated-content-keys.md)):
how homes and streets in and around Llandeilo were lit after dark, era by era. Written inline on
2026-10-03 from sources read that day, mostly Welsh Newspapers Online (National Library of Wales),
whose search results and article pages were fetched with `curl`.

Independently checked 2026-10-03 (see [reviews/light-after-dark-check-2026-10-03.md](reviews/light-after-dark-check-2026-10-03.md)); corrections applied.

**"Llandilo" is two places** (see [process](process.md)). Every newspaper item below names the
Llandilo Gas Company, the Llandilo Local Board or the Llandilo Urban District Council, or a gas main
"from Ffairfach Gate to Llandilo Bridge", which are Llandeilo Fawr. Items about Llandilo-Talybont
(Glamorgan) and about Llandovery's 1863 gas lighting turned up in the same searches and are **not**
used.

## Summary

- **Hearth fire** is the light of every early home: the Mesolithic hut at Howick had hearths
  [hunters:S36], and Iron Age roundhouses are reconstructed with a central hearth (no excavation of
  Garn Goch is recorded, so this is analogy) (*Corrected 2026-10-03 (independent check)*: the note said "Garn Goch is unexcavated", the wording the
  2026-10-02 Iron Age check corrected). Howick is in Northumberland, a far analogy: "there are no
  examples of houses known in Wales" [hunters:S20]. Reconstructed.
- **A medieval Welsh court burned candles; ordinary homes, firelight.** The Dimetian Code, Aneurin
  Owen's name for the Blegywryd redaction, the law-book associated with Deheubarth [medieval:S41], sets a candle-bearer "before" the king at meat and dates his protection
  "from the time of lighting the first candle, in the palace, until the last shall be extinguished"
  [music:S3]. It is a prescriptive text, so it says what a court should have, not what was lit at
  Dinefwr on a given night. Nothing read here describes lighting in a medieval Llandeilo house.
  (*Corrected 2026-10-03 (independent check)*: the app said the Deheubarth law-book was "in manuscripts from the early 13th
  century". [medieval:S41] says that of Welsh law's earliest surviving manuscripts in general, which
  are Latin; no source read dates the Deheubarth text's own manuscripts. Owen's introduction says the
  Dimetian Code mentions alterations by Rhys ap Gruffudd "about 1180", a lead, not a manuscript date.)
- **Rushlights in farmhouses and cottages, by the 18th century.** At Cilewent, a long-house from
  Llansanffraid Cwmteuddwr near Rhayader, furnished at St Fagans to 1750, "in the evening, lighting
  was provided by a rushlight holder ... Reeds were dipped in fat, lit and placed between the two iron
  pieces" [S13]. (*Corrected 2026-10-03 (independent check)*: Cilewent says reeds "dipped in fat"; the peeling of the rushes is White's,
  not Cilewent's.) Gilbert White's 1775 letter on rushlights says the poor had "five and a half hours of
  comfortable light for [a] farthing", that "working people burn no candles in the long days, because
  they rise and go to bed by daylight" (in the long days only: "Little farmers use rushes much in the
  short days, both morning and evening" (*Corrected 2026-10-03 (independent check)*: the app had dropped the "long days")), and that "the very poor ... buy a halfpenny candle every
  evening" [S14]. White writes about Hampshire, and Cilewent is in mid Wales, well outside the ten miles, so for this
  valley both are analogy. Reconstructed, cross-checked as a practice (two independent sources).
- **Paraffin lamps were on sale in Carmarthen by 1864**: "NEW LAMPS for burning Petroline, Paraffin,
  and Cazeline Oils ... MODERATOR LAMPS" [S12]. Llandeilo's railway station still had "antiquated oil
  lamps" in December 1902 [S11]. Single-source each; how many Llandeilo homes had oil lamps, and from
  when, is not known.
- **Gas: the works were begun in 1864.** The Llandilo Gas Company accepted Mr G. Bower's tender
  of £96 "for plant and street mains" (the figure as the OCR prints it; low for a gas plant, so
  unconfirmed); the buildings and tanks were let to Morgan and Howell, masons of
  Llandilo, and the foundation stone was laid in August 1864 [S1]. A second paper reported gas works
  "in course of erection" at "Llandilo Fawr and Rhayader" in October 1864 [S2]. The company was allowed
  to lay a main under the turnpike road "from Ffairfach Gate to Llandilo Bridge" [S3]. Cross-checked
  (S1, S2) that building began in 1864; when the works were finished is not found (*Corrected 2026-10-03 (independent check)*: the app said
  the works were "built in 1864", which S1 and S2 do not show). **This contradicts the c. 1860 date for the Ffairfach gas works** in [victorian:S2], a
  single Wikipedia page; the newspapers are primary.
- **Gas street lamps were lit by 1876, but the first lighting is not found.** In September 1876 the
  Local Board asked the Gas Company "to undertake the management of the lighting and repair of town
  lamps during the winter as heretofore", paying "eighteenpence per thousand feet of gas consumed
  beyond the current price" [S4]. "As heretofore" means this had been done before 1876. In September
  1902 the streets "were gas lit" before the electric light [S8]. **The date the streets were first
  lit with gas (between 1864 and 1876) is a gap**; several searches of Welsh Newspapers Online (1864 to 1867) found no
  report of it. The app draws gas street lamps from 1876 only.
- **Gas lamps lit only in winter, it seems.** S4 arranges the lighting "during the winter". In April
  1902 the lamplighting contract had been ended on 31 March "in anticipation of the electric light",
  and Mr Davies was to light the town lamps to "the end of the season" [S21], which supports seasonal
  gas lighting. With electricity the council resolved, in October 1902, "that the town lamps be
  lighted from half an hour after sunset till II [11] o'clock every night throughout the year" [S20].
  (*Corrected 2026-10-03 (independent check)*: these two sources were found by the check; the question is partly answered.) The app does
  not yet model seasons or an 11 o'clock switch-off.
- **Electric street lighting by early September 1902, the council's own.** A public meeting resolved
  in June 1899 "to have the town lit by electric light", at an estimated £1,500 [S5]. A shareholder
  protested that the Gas Company had "originally invested their money for the sole purpose of lighting
  the town" [S6]. The council accepted Mr Bertram Thomas's tender of £2,415 4s in June 1900 [S7] (the
  paper prints "Thorn"; the Carmarthen Journal of 10 October 1902 names "the contractor (Mr Bertram
  Thomas)" [S20] (*Corrected 2026-10-03 (independent check)*: that report was "not listed below")). In April 1902 the council agreed
  "that 16 new lamp posts be supplied by the contractor and fixed by him" [S22]. On 5 September 1902 a
  columnist mocked those "who profess that they can see no difference in the streets now that they are
  electrically lit than when they were gas lit" [S8]; a month later the streets were "left in
  darkness" one Saturday night when the electric light failed [S9]; in November the Urban Council "has
  just installed their own electric light" [S10]. Cross-checked (S8 and S10 are different papers). The
  exact switch-on day is not found, so the app uses September 1902. Not all was finished or lit: in
  October 1902 "several pillars and brackets had not been completed" [S20], and in December there was
  "no light whatever" on the corner of the turn from Trallwm to Station Road [S11]. In June 1902 a
  council (its name is lost in the OCR) asked Llandyfeisant Parish Council and the Urban District
  Council to join it "in lighting Llandilo Bridge by electric light" [S23]; the outcome is not found.
  (*Corrected 2026-10-03 (independent check)*: these finds are the check's.)
- **Gas did not vanish.** In October 1902 the Reading Room's electric light was "not such a great
  improvement on the gas as was expected" (Carmarthen Journal, 10 October 1902, noted in passing, not
  re-read), and when the Institution's failed "luckily the gas had been left intact" [S24]
  (*Corrected 2026-10-03 (independent check)*: the 31 October item is confirmed and now listed). The council sold electricity by the
  unit: "the charge for electric light at Llandilo be 6d. per unit" was suggested in May 1902 [S25],
  and a collector was paid "for each meter per quarter" [S20]. **Who had electric light at home is not
  found**, so the app keeps homes on flame light at the 1902 key (*Corrected 2026-10-03 (independent check)*: the app had said homes
  "mixed electric light, gas and oil" from 1902, which no source shows).
- **The blackout, 1 September 1939.** "The first real effect of the Second World War for most British
  civilians was the blackout, which was introduced on 1 September 1939, two days before war was
  actually declared" [S15]. It "remained in force for five years until September 1944, when
  regulations were relaxed to allow a 'dim-out'" [S15]. A home-front history website gives the same
  dates [S19]. National rules, so documented for Llandeilo as for everywhere; no local account of the
  blackout here was read. Street lamps were put out; later in the war a "more realistic approach" let
  some places use "'glimmer' or 'star' lighting" [S15], and Scarborough had star lighting from 1943
  [S30]. Whether Llandeilo did is not known, so the app keeps its streets dark until the dim-out.
  Wikipedia's "Blackout (wartime)" gives the same dates but cites S19 for them, so it is not a second
  source (*Corrected 2026-10-03 (independent check)*).
- **The dim-out, from 17 September 1944, was partial.** Circulars of 9 September gave "particulars of
  the relaxations due to come into force on 17th September", except in listed areas [S26]. In December
  the Home Secretary said "We have allowed half-lighting for houses, and removed the requirement that
  the full black-out shall be restored on the alert. We have allowed half-lighting or moon-lighting
  for the streets" [S27]; a Scarborough history says householders "could take down their blackout
  curtains and shutters and use their ordinary curtains" [S30]. The street-lighting standard was each
  council's choice: "I have no power to require any minimum standard of street lighting" [S28].
  (*Corrected 2026-10-03 (independent check)*: the note gave the month only, and the app said blackout curtains stayed, which S27 and S30
  contradict; homes still had to be screened [S17], but not with blackout material. S19's "full
  Blackout ... if an alert was sounded" was removed for houses by December 1944 [S27].) In October 1944 the Home Secretary spoke of "the
  recent relaxations for domestic lighting" but would not "relax all restrictions during any part of
  the black-out period", and said "in some parts, the black-out is now more effective than it was
  before the relaxation" [S16]. In February 1945 he would not yet do away with "blacking-out of
  private homes" [S17], while that sixth winter allowed "some alleviation of the street lighting
  position" [S17]. On 12 April 1945 he meant "to remove the black-out restrictions in all districts at
  a date not later than the end of the war in Europe" [S18], which was 8 May 1945; [S19] says full
  street lighting came in April 1945, in London on 30 April. When Llandeilo's lamps came back is not
  found, so the app uses 8 May 1945 as a latest date. The lifting was not uniform: "The restrictions
  mostly remained in place until 2 May 1945, and, even then, some coastal areas remained subject to
  the rules well into peacetime" [S29], and S18's question already spoke of districts "in which they
  are still in force". So 8 May is a latest date for an inland town like Llandeilo, not for "all
  districts" (*Corrected 2026-10-03 (independent check)*: the app said the blackout "was over everywhere" by 8 May). S19's "5 years and 123
  days" is wrong arithmetic (1 September 1939 to 30 April 1945 is 5 years and 241 days), a small mark
  against its care. The dim-out is cross-checked (IWM and Hansard);
  the lifting rests on Hansard's statement of intent and two secondary websites. The app's dim-out draws faint windows and dim street lamps; how much light
  Llandeilo actually showed is reconstructed.

The c. 1200 key draws nothing different from the hearth key: it exists so the almanac can say what a
Welsh court's law-book prescribes. The brief's "torchlight at Dinefwr" is not drawn, since no source
read says what Dinefwr lit. Each key's words and ⓘ show in the almanac as "Light after dark".

The shares of lit windows, the brightness and the colour of each key are **reconstructed**: no
source counts lit windows. Flame light (hearth, rush, candle, oil, flat-flame gas) is drawn warm
yellow-orange; electric light whiter. Gas mantles (whiter light) are not recorded for Llandeilo, so
gas is drawn as flame. Farms across the valley are drawn with the town's windows, which is a guess.
Street lamp positions are not recorded; they are drawn along today's roads near the town's houses,
inside an outline of Llandeilo north of the Tywi (its south and east edges follow OS Open Rivers),
because the sources speak of lighting "the town" and nothing read says Ffairfach was lit.

## Open questions

- When were Llandeilo's streets first lit with gas (1864 to 1876)? How many lamps? (16 new lamp posts
  were ordered for the electric light in April 1902 [S22]; how many there were in all is not found.)
- Were the town's gas lamps lit only in winter? Partly answered: seasonal, it seems [S4][S21]; and
  from October 1902 the lamps were lit from half an hour after sunset till 11 o'clock all year [S20].
  The app models neither the season nor the 11 o'clock switch-off yet.
- Who in Llandeilo had electric light at home before 1939, and when did farms get mains electricity?
- When did Ffairfach and the villages get street lighting at all? (The app lights only the town north
  of the Tywi.)
- When did Llandeilo's own lamps come back on in 1945? Did the town use star lighting before the
  dim-out?
- Was Llandilo Bridge lit by electricity after the June 1902 proposal [S23]?
- Roman oil lamps at the Dinefwr forts: no find is recorded in the notes, so the app shows none.
